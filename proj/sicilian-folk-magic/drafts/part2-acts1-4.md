## 4 · Act 1 · Catania: Agatha goes away, and comes back

When empires keep changing overhead, a town learns to anchor itself to something that will not pack up and leave when the next fleet arrives. Nowhere is that clearer than in Catania, a port city on Sicily's eastern, Ionian coast sitting directly at the foot of Mount Etna [X1-200a]. Catania's protector is Saint Agatha, whom Catholic tradition remembers as a young Christian woman of the city put to death there for her faith in the 250s, during the reign of the Roman emperor Decius [X1-82, X2-01]. To understand why a provincial woman's refusal mattered to Rome, it helps to know what Decius had just demanded: around January 250, rattled by Gothic raids along the Danube and turmoil inside the empire, the emperor issued an edict ordering every inhabitant—apart from Jews, who held an established exemption—to sacrifice to the Roman gods and for the imperial well-being, and to obtain a signed papyrus certificate (*libellus*) proving they had complied [X1-254]. Because Christian monotheism forbade offering sacrifice to other gods, refusal meant imprisonment, torture, or execution, unleashing the empire's first systematic, empire-wide persecution [X1-254]. Whatever contemporary court records may once have existed for Agatha are gone, yet fifth- and sixth-century church martyrologies and a Roman basilica named by Pope Gelasius show Christians were honoring her by late Roman times [X1-83].

In the medieval Mediterranean, however, a patron saint was not merely a memory in a prayer book; scholars note that Christians held their saints to be physically present in, and actively defending, the communities that kept their bodies [X2-152]. About 1040, during his Sicilian campaign of 1038–40, the Byzantine general George Maniakes stripped Catania of that shield and carried Agatha's body off to Constantinople [X1-84, X1-85, X2-02, X2-03]. Even that famous abduction is hazy at the edges: Bishop Maurice's own Latin account gives no year for the theft, the Byzantine chroniclers Skylitzes, Psellos, and Attaleiates pass over it in silence, and later writers cannot agree on whether the relics were presented to Emperor Michael IV or tucked into a convent of virgins by Empress Theodora [X1-85, X2-03, X2-05, X2-07].

For more than eighty years Catania lived without its saint, until 17 August 1126, when two soldiers named in Bishop Maurice's account as Gislebertus and Goselinus smuggled the body back across the sea by way of Taranto and Messina [X1-86, X1-87, X2-08, X2-09, X2-10, X2-11, X2-13]. Catania's clergy walked out to meet the landing *nudis pedibus et in albis vestibus*—barefoot and dressed in white—an arrival whose nine-hundredth anniversary falls in 2026 [X2-12, X1-97, X2-36]. The historian Mariano Papasidero notes that Agatha was one of the few female saints whose relics were stolen in the Middle Ages, and Catania's archbishop Luigi Renna still calls that 1126 recovery a *sacro furto*, a "holy theft" [X2-15, X2-16]. Every year Catania keeps both dates on its calendar: the great winter *Festa di Sant'Agata* from 3 to 5 February, and the quieter procession of the return on 17 August [X1-90, X2-35]. During the February feast, trade guilds haul towering candle-structures through the streets—the folklorist Elisabetta Di Giovanni counted eleven of these *candelore* in 2007—while devotees march in a five-piece white robe, cap, cord, gloves, and handkerchief called the *sacco* [X1-91, X1-92, X1-200, X1-201]. A fond local tradition claims the robe commemorates the nightshirts Catanians supposedly ran out in when the relics docked in 1126; historian Francesco Benigno gently dates the *sacco*, cap, and gloves to the eighteenth century, even if Bishop Maurice's chronicle really did put the welcoming clergy in white [X1-93, X2-12, X2-34].

**Inside the silver woman.** Here is the detail that tends to stop readers who did not grow up Catholic: Agatha is not buried under a stone slab. Most of her bones sit inside a gilded-silver reliquary bust made by Giovanni di Bartolo of Siena and dated 1376 by its Latin inscription (brought back from the papal court at Avignon in December 1377, according to a Catanese document cited by historian Stefano Riccioni), its metal surface so thick with jewels and gifts hung on it over the centuries that the silver barely shows [X2-18, X2-19, X2-150]. Archbishop Renna notes that a piece of her pelvis is sealed inside the bust, and that on 3 February 1963 her skull was lifted out so the faithful could venerate it directly; meanwhile Maria Concetta Di Natale records two thighbone reliquaries kept inside a great chest, and because Agatha's legend tells that her torturers cut off her breasts, one breast relic is venerated across the water at Galatina in Apulia [X2-23, X2-24, X2-25, X1-250, X2-37]. On her feast days the jewel-crusted bust leaves the cathedral and rides through the city streets—a practice Act 7 will examine across Europe as a whole [X2-151].

Civic pride has naturally polished a few legends onto the metal. Guides routinely repeat that the crown on Agatha's bust was a gift from Richard the Lionheart in 1191, though Di Natale and earlier art historians place its making in the late fifteenth century, nearly three centuries after Richard sailed past [X2-21]. You will also read almost everywhere that Catania's feast is "the world's third-largest religious festival," a ranking that rests on no documented headcount and that Catania's own daily newspaper, *La Sicilia*, eventually dismissed as a figure "never verified" after thirty years of uncritical repetition [X1-96].

**Is Agatha a goddess in disguise?** Still, the myths that matter most for our story are not the ones Catanians told about royal crowns, but the ones nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century scholars projected onto Agatha herself. Two ancient goddesses usually get drafted as her secret identity: Ceres, the Roman goddess of growing grain whose cult merged early with that of the Greek Demeter, widely worshipped across Sicily [X1-94a]; and Isis, the great Egyptian goddess whose worship spread across the Roman Empire [X1-94b]. In 1823 the English clergyman J. J. Blunt set Agatha beside Ceres and candidly admitted he could not prove the connection [X1-95]; in 1905 the historian Emanuele Ciaceri compared Catania's February procession to an Isis festival described by the Roman writer Apuleius, pointing to crowds, white robes, and lights [X1-94, X1-94c]. A 2025 study by the historian Cristiana Soraci, titled bluntly *"Né Demetra, né Iside"* ("Neither Demeter nor Isis"), lays out why the parallel collapses: in Apuleius's procession every participant wore white, whereas in 1126 only the clergy did (the lay devotees' white *sacco* arrived in the 1700s), and Isis herself is attested in Catania by nothing more than a few statuettes, a lamp, and some coins [X1-94, X1-94c, X1-93].

**The veil.** Alongside Agatha's bones, Catania's cathedral keeps another cherished relic tied directly to the smoking peak above the town: the martyr's veil, which devotees revere as "the instrument of this prodigious intercession" against Mount Etna's lava [X2-28]. That faith faced its supreme test in 1669, when Etna erupted continuously from March to July [X2-29]. Beginning on 13 March, clergy and townsfolk carried the veil and other relics in urgent prayer processions through villages directly in the lava's path and inside Catania itself, while citizens led by Diego Pappalardo went out with iron tools to try diverting the molten channels away from the city [X2-29, X2-29a, X2-29b]. On 23 April the flow reached the sea at the harbor, and on 30 April it breached the western walls and crushed part of the fortifications and surrounding buildings [X2-29a]. Yet the rest of Catania survived, and believers credited the veil with sparing the whole city from ruin—a conviction durable enough that in 1886, as a University of Teramo historical study records, Archbishop Dusmet again raised the veil before advancing lava at Nicolosi, where the flow halted short of the town [X2-29b, X2-30].

Catania spent centuries draping silver, jewels, and miracle chronicles around a martyr from Roman antiquity. Cross the island to Palermo, however, and you watch a city elevate a brand-new patron saint in twelve frantic days, with the Senate voting first and the investigators catching up months later.

## 5 · Act 2 · Palermo: Rosalia and the plague

In May 1624—on 7 May, by the Vatican's account, aboard a ship arriving from Tunis—bubonic plague reached Palermo, though physicians did not formally confirm the outbreak until early August [X1-98]. As Sicily's viceregal capital and seat of Parliament, Palermo had formidable civic pride and an impatient Senate, yet its spiritual defenses felt suddenly thin [X1-255]. Before that summer the city looked to four principal patronesses—Agatha, Cristina, Ninfa, and Oliva—and as the contagion spread, clergy carried the reliquaries of Cristina and Ninfa through the stricken streets [X2-94]. Off in the background hovered Rosalia, remembered in local tradition as a twelfth-century noblewoman turned hermit whose cult, historians note, had remained minor and quiet—*seppur debole* [X1-106, X1-107, X2-92].

Then, on Monte Pellegrino, the sheer limestone headland rising above Palermo's harbor, a discovery rewrote the city's devotional hierarchy. Tradition holds that a sick woman from Ciminna named Geronima La Gattuta climbed the mountain in late May 1624 after seeing Rosalia in a vision or dream, and that her visions guided searchers to a cave high on the rock (the municipality's history adds a sailor named Vito Amodeo as the man who actually found the remains, while Franciscans and Jesuits oversaw the digging) [X1-100, X2-68, X2-69]. On 15 July 1624, bones were pulled from the grotto [X1-99, X2-67].

Here is the timeline, and it is the punchline:
- **15 July 1624:** bones found. [X1-99]
- **27 July 1624:** the Senate elects Rosalia patron of Palermo. [X2-70]
- **November 1624:** the Church sets up a commission to check whether the bones are hers. [X2-72]
- **February 1625:** the commission says yes. [X1-102, X2-76]
- **9 June 1625:** the relics' first procession. [X1-104, X2-78]
- **15 July 1625:** the plague is declared over, one year to the day after the finding. [X1-105, X2-80]

That sequence captures the collision between civic desperation and Church caution. Only twelve days after the bones came out of the cave, Palermo's Senate elected Rosalia patron of the city and its Public Council ordered a silver reliquary ark—nearly four months before Cardinal Giannettino Doria convened a formal Church commission on 30 November, chaired by the Jesuit Giordano Cascini (whose credibility, historian Luisa Scalisi notes, some of his fellow Jesuits quietly doubted), to decide whether the bones actually belonged to a medieval hermit [X1-101, X2-70, X2-71, X1-103, X2-72, X2-73]. At first, the examiners were deeply uneasy: Palermo Cathedral's own history acknowledges that the physicians were puzzled because the remains seemed to come from more than one person, which a 2024 *L'Osservatore Romano* account specifies as "the bones of three different men" [X2-74].

Between those doubts and the eventual approval sits the most human episode in the cathedral's chronicle. On 4 February 1625, a Palermitan soap-maker named Vincenzo Bonelli—some accounts spell his surname Bonello—climbed Monte Pellegrino meaning to kill himself [X2-75]. On the mountain, he later told authorities, Rosalia appeared to him and asked for a procession of her relics through the city [X2-75]. Whatever broke the deadlock inside the commission, the doctors and theologians reversed course and recognized the bones as Rosalia's in February 1625—seven months after Palermo's Senate had already placed her on its civic throne [X1-102, X2-70, X2-76]. On 9 June 1625 her relics were carried through the streets in their first procession, and on 15 July 1625—one year to the day after the cave discovery—the city declared the plague over, crediting Rosalia with its deliverance in a conviction Palermitans have honored for four centuries [X1-104, X1-105, X2-78, X2-80].

Once the crisis passed, Palermo and the Counter-Reformation orders turned a hometown hermit into a baroque monument [X1-122a]. In 1627 Cascini published her formal biography—a *vita*, as traditional saints' *Lives* are called—and about 1630 her name entered the Roman Martyrology, the Church's official calendar-catalogue of saints and martyrs newly revised under Pope Urban VIII, to whom Scalisi notes Palermo sent relics in 1626 (with the pope reportedly wearing one of Rosalia's teeth on his chest) [X1-108, X1-109, X1-109a, X1-110, X2-91]. Since 1631 her remains have lain inside Palermo Cathedral in a silver urn designed by Mariano Smiriglio, while Anthony van Dyck painted her interceding for the plague-stricken city [X1-113, X2-85, X1-112, X1-111]. Even so, Rosalia remained largely Palermo's own miracle: in Giuseppe Pitrè's survey of about 150 Sicilian towns around 1900, she served as principal patron of just seven [X1-183, X1-186]. Today Palermo celebrates her *festino* every 10 to 15 July—pulling a towering triumphal cart, the *carro* (first built by architect Paolo Amato in 1686 and paraded annually from 1693, as historian Margiotta traces), on the evening of the 14th, processing her silver urn on the 15th, and making the night climb, the *acchianata*, up Monte Pellegrino on 3–4 September [X1-115, X1-116, X1-117, X1-202, X1-203].

Like Agatha's feast, Rosalia's cave attracted its share of later speculation, though none of it holds up to inspection. Some writers suggested her Monte Pellegrino grotto had once been a Punic shrine to Tanit, the great goddess of Carthage; yet even the scholar Valerio Petrarca, who floated the idea, hedged it with "probably," no inscription or find from the cave backs it up, and the *festino* itself is unmistakably a baroque civic spectacle [X1-121, X1-122]. At the opposite extreme of skepticism sits a famous dinner-table yarn claiming the English geologist William Buckland inspected the cathedral relics in 1825 and pronounced them the bones of a goat—an anecdote that surfaced only in a memoir published in 1900, seventy-five years after the supposed visit, with zero evidence that Buckland ever handled the remains inside the silver urn [X2-93].

Palermo had met a crisis by conjuring a patron saint out of a mountain cave and civic nerve. Yet its fierce eastern rival at the Strait of Messina had already spent a century playing an even bolder hand—one backed not by a medieval hermit, but by a personal letter from the mother of Jesus.

## 6 · Act 3 · Messina: a letter from Mary

To understand why Messina needed a miracle of its own, you have to picture the long, bitter rivalry between Sicily's two great ports. Throughout the late Middle Ages and the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, wealthy, silk-trading Messina fiercely contested Palermo's claim to be *Caput Regni*—the kingdom's true capital—forcing the Spanish viceroy to divide his official residence between the two cities while Messina guarded extraordinary privileges: minting its own coin, holding exclusive court jurisdiction in its province, and claiming ambassadorial rank for its Senate's envoys [X1-255]. That dualism ended brutally when Spain crushed Messina's 1674–78 revolt, dissolved its Senate, shuttered its university, and stripped its ancient charters [X1-255].

Before that collapse, however, Messina answered rival civic prestige with an apostolic trump card: every 3 June, the city celebrates the *Madonna della Lettera*, honoring a letter that legend says the Virgin Mary wrote to the people of Messina from Jerusalem in the year 42 CE, promising perpetual protection (*"Vos et ipsam civitatem benedicimus"*—"We bless you and your city itself") and binding the parchment with a lock of her own hair [X1-123, X2-98, X2-99, X2-113]. Messina's elites and expatriate merchants wielded the *Madonna della Lettera* across the Mediterranean as a spiritual charter of civic independence and preeminence—citing it to King Philip II in 1575, and commissioning grand altarpieces in Rome in 1642 and in Valletta, Malta, in 1664 by Mattia Preti, moves that promptly triggered local resistance and Inquisition inquiries [X2-104, X1-269].

It is a magnificent civic legend, and a surprisingly young one. Whatever supporters later claimed about veneration stretching back to the Middle Ages [X1-128, X2-108], historians Chiara Franceschini and Carmelo Campagna show the story was almost certainly invented in the first half of the sixteenth century [X1-124, X2-100]: Campagna notes donations to a "Holy Licterio" chapel in 1518 and an engraving dated 1522 carrying the oldest surviving text, while Franceschini traces the first printed Latin text to Francesco Maurolico in 1556, and both confirm the oldest manuscript copy dates only to 1599, entered into Messina's register of privileges [X1-124, X2-101, X2-102, X2-103, X2-104]. When critics pointed out the absence of older paperwork, the letter's champions answered that the Greek original had been discovered in the 1400s and translated by the Byzantine humanist Constantine Lascaris, who reached Messina in 1467—yet no scholar has ever produced that Greek text [X1-127, X2-107].

Even Rome refused to play along without a fight. Franceschini notes that the Holy Office condemned the letter as apocryphal in 1598 [X1-125, X2-105]; and when the Jesuit scholar Melchior Inchofer published an elaborate defense of it in 1629, the Vatican's Congregation of the Index—the office that censored books—objected, forcing a corrected edition in 1632 [X1-126, X2-106]. Messina shrugged and kept its letter anyway: as historian Francesco Ciaramitaro records, when a famine broke on 3 June 1636 "by a miracle of Our Lady of the Holy Letter," the Senate vowed an even grander celebration, and by 1657 the Virgin's hair was riding at the head of an eight-day festival with Archbishop Simone Carafa walking before it [X1-129, X2-109, X2-110].

Messina stages that devotion on two distinct processional floats, each called a *vara* [X1-204]. On 3 June, the hair relic—sealed in a tiny gold, enamel, and crystal capsule that Italy's cultural heritage catalogue (ICCD) dates by style to the late sixteenth or early seventeenth century—rides out on the **silver *vara***, an enthronement still scheduled in the Archdiocese of Messina's 2026 festival programme [X1-131, X2-111, X2-112]. Two months later, on 15 August, comes the Feast of the Assumption and Messina's monumental **Vara**, a baroque votive tower whose mechanical lineage is traced to Emperor Charles V's 1535 entry into the port [X1-133, X1-256]. Standing roughly fourteen meters tall and weighing about eight tons, the Assumption *Vara* rises in cosmological tiers from the Virgin's bier and the Twelve Apostles through revolving sun, moon, stars, and angels—in earlier centuries played by strapped-in live children, today by statues—to Christ cradling Mary's soul at the summit [X1-256]. Mounted on iron sleds with neither wheels nor brakes, it is hauled roughly three kilometers along continuously wetted asphalt by about a thousand devotees, many barefoot, pulling two hemp ropes over a hundred meters long to cries of *"Viva Maria!"* while steering crews guide its sharp turn into Piazza Duomo [X1-257].

For all its fierce municipal pride, Messina did share its treasure once, in a gesture that turned a relic into an enduring cross-strait bond. During a devastating plague in the 1570s (accounts vary between 1571, 1572, and 1575), the Calabrian town of Palmi just across the water sent aid to the stricken port; in gratitude, Messina's Senate gave Palmi a single hair from the Virgin's lock—by local tradition in 1582—and Calabria's regional legislative record confirms that single strand remains in Palmi's cathedral today, a relic split and handed over as a thank-you note [X2-158, X2-159].

Agatha's silver bust, Rosalia's marble cave, and Messina's eight-ton *Vara* were urban monuments forged by senates, guilds, and cathedrals. Step off the main piazza and into an ordinary Sicilian kitchen on 19 March, however, and you find a ritual theater just as intricate—built not out of silver and hemp rope, but out of braided bread, toasted breadcrumbs, and neighbors' vows.

## 7 · Act 4 · St Joseph's tables

In the Gospels, Joseph is Mary's husband and the quiet carpenter who raised Jesus as his own [X1-237]. His feast falls on 19 March—fixed on the Church calendar by the sixteenth century, as folklorists Karen Canning and Christine Zinni note, after his liturgical cult took shape in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, per Luisa Del Giudice—and across much of Sicily, especially in the western provinces of Palermo, Trapani, Enna, and Agrigento as well as towns in Ragusa, Catania, and Syracuse, it becomes a feast for the poor [X1-138, X1-139, X1-140, X1-145]. As Italy's ICCD heritage catalogue records, families fulfill personal vows by building tiered domestic banquets—*tavolate* or *altari di San Giuseppe*—lit the night before by neighborhood *vampata* bonfires on 18 March [X1-143, X1-147, X1-206]. On the feast day itself, households invite three of the poorest people they can find, often orphans or local children (though in the Madonie mountains unmarried young people take the seats), dress them as Jesus, Mary, and Joseph, and serve these *virgineddi* at the head of the table before everyone else eats [X1-143, X1-144, X1-205].

Because 19 March falls squarely inside Lent, the feast laid across those white tablecloths is strictly meatless, turning pious restriction into culinary theater [X1-259]. Around towering, braided ceremonial loaves called *cucciddati*—which Del Giudice notes often weigh three to seven kilograms—bakers sculpt ritual breads into St Joseph's flowering staff, crosses, monstrances, ladders, carpentry tools, fish, birds, and flowers [X1-146, X1-258]. Alongside them sit whole fish, fried artichokes, vegetable *frittate*, fava-bean soup (*maccu*), *pasta con le sarde*, and *pasta con la mollica*, scattered with toasted breadcrumbs to represent carpenter's sawdust, followed by ricotta-filled *sfince di San Giuseppe*, fig cookies (*cuccidati*), and honeyed *pignolata*, while guests pocket dried fava beans at the door to carry home as "lucky beans" [X1-258, X1-259].

Nobody knows when the custom began, as Del Giudice observes [X1-141]; Giuseppe Pitrè documented these charity banquets by 1900 at the latest, and Del Giudice found "no counterpart in Spain or its colonies," making St Joseph's tables one of the clearest signatures of a distinctly Sicilian Catholicism [X1-142, X1-148]. Even in modern bureaucracy the carpenter outstrips every rival: on Sicily's official intangible heritage register, the [REIS](https://www.regione.sicilia.it/istituzioni/regione/strutture-regionali/assessorato-beni-culturali-identita-siciliana/dipartimento-beni-culturali-identita-siciliana/altri-contenuti/reis) (*Registro delle Eredità Immateriali*, created in 2005), the September 2013 list—the latest full tally available—devotes 11 of the 86 entries in its celebrations book to St Joseph, more than to any other saint [X1-190, X1-192, X1-193, X1-194].

Ask at a Sicilian-American St Joseph's altar in Los Angeles, New Orleans, or western New York how the feast started, and you will almost certainly hear a moving origin story: a medieval Sicilian drought and famine broken when St Joseph answered the island's prayers with rain and a harvest of fava beans [X1-149, X1-260]. Yet when Del Giudice hunted for that legend in the archives, she found it "almost exclusively in diaspora" sources, with scarcely a trace in homeland Italian literature [X1-149]. Why, then, do emigrant families tell it with such conviction? Scholars interpret the overflowing table and the famine tale together as a "cultural exorcism of hunger": a way for communities shaped by generations of rural scarcity and emigration to invert remembered poverty into public abundance, neighborly *caritas*, and a story that explains why an humble animal-fodder legume sits as a lucky token at the center of a sacred table [X1-260].

Nor do the tables fare any better as a disguised pagan relic. Pitrè declared the eve bonfires "certainly pagan," and later writers cast the whole March banquet as a Christianized spring rite for Demeter, the Greek goddess of grain [X1-150]. Del Giudice grants the seasonal parallels—springtime renewal, greenery, bread—while noting that proving direct descent from antiquity is far harder than spotting a resemblance, and Sicily's own heritage records frame the feast cleanly by what happens in the room: vows kept, the hungry fed, and a neighborhood gathered around the bread [X1-150, X1-143].

Through four acts, our tour has stayed in the daylight of cathedrals, harbor squares, and crowded family kitchens—places where Sicily's towns bargained openly with heaven through silver busts, disputed letters, and seven-kilogram loaves of bread. As dusk settles over Palermo and the bonfires burn down to embers, however, the city's prayers turn toward darker crossroads and stranger partners: the executed criminals buried beside the Oreto river, and the cunning healers who soon found themselves answering to the Spanish Inquisition.
