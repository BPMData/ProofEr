## 8 · Act 5 (night) · The dead who help

_The stage goes dark; the candle wall lights._

As the embers of St Joseph's bonfires cool and daylight fades out of the piazza, the story moves indoors and down to the riverbanks, where Sicilian devotion turns from the saints in heaven to the unquiet, ordinary dead.

Catholics pray *for* the dead as well as *to* the saints. Most people who die at peace with God, the Church teaches, are not ready for heaven at once; first they must pass through a "final purification" called purgatory, and the prayers of the living help shorten their wait [X1-229]. What makes that traffic truly two-way, in the words of the *Catechism*, is that "our prayer for them is capable not only of helping them, but also of making their intercession for us effective" [X1-228 (the Catechism, named)]. In Palermo, that reciprocal bargain found its strangest and most loyal brokers: the *decollati*, "the beheaded"—known locally as the *armi di li corpi decullati* ("souls of the beheaded bodies"), just as Acireale called its own executed dead the *beati* ("the blessed") and Trapani called them the *armiceddi* ("little souls") [X1-207 (Pitrè 1889, named)].

At first glance, asking a convicted murderer for favors seems perverse, yet within early modern Sicilian Catholicism it followed an austere, compassionate logic. From 1541 onward, a white-robed confraternity in Palermo, the [[Compagnia dei Bianchi|Compagnia dei Bianchi]], existed specifically to comfort those condemned to death [X1-151 (Pitrè 1889, named), X1-230]. For a prisoner's final three days and nights, the brothers stayed at his side, received him into their brotherhood, walked with him to the scaffold, saw to his burial, and afterward begged alms to buy prayers for his soul [X1-153, X1-230]. Under the Church's own theology, a condemned man who confessed and repented before the blade fell died reconciled with God, like the *good thief* crucified beside Jesus who admitted his guilt and was promised paradise that very day [X1-231, X1-236 (Luke 23:39–43, named)]. His execution settled his debt to earthly justice; purgatory would finish what the scaffold began [X1-231]. When Giuseppe Pitrè studied the cult in the 1880s, he emphasized that ordinary Palermitans treated the repentant executed as martyrs—a thoroughly Christian logic rather than a pagan survival, echoing late-medieval confraternities that had taken root across Europe between 1350 and 1550 [X1-231, X1-160].

The executed were buried together beside the Oreto river, at a small church once called the Madonna del Fiume and rebuilt—according to Fatta and Pitrè—in 1785 and again in 1857–65 [X1-154 (Fatta 2016 and Pitrè 1889, named)]. Along those dark river roads, where the city's grim architecture of punishment once stood—a head-pyramid inscribed in 1604 near the execution ground before the Senate ordered it moved in 1779, and corpses displayed at the Sperone until a viceregal ban in 1788, as Fatta (2016) documents [X1-155, X1-156 (Fatta 2016, named)]—devotees believed the souls of the *decollati* walked at night in white robes to advise, warn, and defend the living [X1-232]. If robbers ambushed a traveler after dark, people held that you had only to call on the beheaded souls and they would come to your aid [X1-232].

Their help was practical and unpretentious. In the 1880s, Pitrè recorded devotees praying to the *decollati* for help in business, for three lucky numbers in the lottery, and for a mother's children to return home safely; later historians Davies and Matteoni add health, protection, and sons spared an awful death [X1-233]. Faithful visitors walked out to the Oreto church on Mondays and Fridays, often barefoot, to say a rosary for the souls, while those who could not make the journey kept a nine-day novena by candlelight at home [X1-158 (Pitrè 1889, named)]. And when a soul—or a saint—answered a prayer, gratitude had to be posted where the whole neighborhood could read it. In Pitrè's day, the walls of the *decollati* church were hung with about a hundred painted votive tablets of thanks [X1-157 (Pitrè 1889, named)]. Called a *miraculu* in Sicilian dialect—like the painted-tin panel for a miracle of 23 November 1890 preserved in the Casa-Museo Antonino Uccello [X1-169, X1-211]—an ex-voto makes a private rescue public: painted panels depict the peril, the survivor, and the heavenly helper, while offerings of wax, metal, or terracotta reproduce the healed limb or organ [X1-168]. As Palermo scholar I. E. Buttitta puts it, a painted ex-voto serves as a "standing declaration of debt" to the saint [X1-168 (Buttitta, named)]. Some writers have looked at silver body parts hanging beside ancient terracotta limbs in Sicilian churches and seen unbroken pagan continuity, yet the dated painted tablets collected in Sicily belong to the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; visual resemblance is not a genealogical tree [X1-171].

By the Church's own rulebook, of course, none of these executed men could be saints: their names never appeared on the official rolls of the blessed or canonized, and canon law forbids public veneration of anyone outside them [X2-124, X1-234]. On Palermo's streets, however, official lists were beside the point; what mattered was a reputation for holiness won in the final hour. As historians Davies and Matteoni observe, these were saints of the street, "popular, though not officially recognised," and other Sicilian towns cultivated their own [X1-234, X1-235]. In Paceco, near Trapani, one of the most vivid was Francesco Frusteri, a farmer who murdered his own mother and was beheaded on 5 November 1817 [X1-159]. An inscription in the church of San Francesco di Paola noted sparingly that he died "resigned and contrite"—yet seventy years later, Pitrè found Frusteri spoken of "in fame of sanctity," with miracles credited to his name across Trapani, Paceco, and the Isola Grande [X1-159].

Nor did Sicily's dead return only along lonely riverbanks for desperate adults; once a year, on 2 November, the Day of the Dead, they came gently through the front door carrying sweets for the children [X1-162]. On that morning, families set out a basket (*canistru*) heaped with fruit, nuts, and *pupi di zùccaru*—painted sugar dolls, traditionally a *pupa* for a girl and a knight (*cavaliere*) for a boy, as Campanella's oral testimonies note—and told the young ones that their departed relatives had slipped in during the night to leave them [X1-162, X1-212, X1-163 (Campanella 2023, named)]. Outsiders routinely trace those sugar-filled baskets straight back to ancient Roman offerings at family tombs, though when you go looking for an unbroken documentary chain linking classical grave meals to Palermo's sugar-casters, the paper trail simply isn't there [X1-167].

Still, the night was never populated solely by the souls of the dead. While ordinary families waited for November gifts and prayed to the *decollati* by candlelight, another kind of nocturnal traffic—of fairy-led healers, muttered charms, and suspicious neighbors—was steadily filling the leather-bound registers of Palermo's Spanish Inquisition.

## 9 · Act 6 (night) · Witches, healers and the evil eye

_scene 16_

Remember that first Spanish inquisitor appointed for Sicily in 1487 [X3-01]? The Inquisition was the Church's court for rooting out heresy—beliefs and practices judged false—and from 1487 until 1782 (operating steadily as a tribunal from 1500 onward) Sicily hosted a branch of the Spanish Inquisition that answered to Madrid rather than to Rome [X3-01, X3-04, X3-09, X3-115]. From 1601 it sat inside Palermo's [[Palazzo Chiaramonte-Steri|Steri palace]], a crenellated fortress in the Kalsa quarter whose stones already held three centuries of Sicilian power: built by the Chiaramonte family in the early 1300s (with its famous painted Sala Magna ceiling added in 1377–80), confiscated in 1392 when Andrea Chiaramonte was beheaded for treason, and used successively as a royal residence, the seat of the Spanish viceroys from 1468 to 1517, and the Royal Customs before the Holy Office moved in [X3-13, X3-214]. Centuries later, in 1906, Giuseppe Pitrè spent six months scraping whitewash off the Steri's prison walls to reveal drawings left by what he called "a whole vanished generation," as historian Giovanna Fiume recounts—though contrary to popular tour-guide lore, those cells held prisoners on every sort of charge, and Fiume notes that only a single graffito can be securely attributed to a woman [X3-18, X3-113 (Fiume 2017, named)].

When the Sicilian tribunal was finally abolished on 16 March 1782 at the request of the reformist viceroy Domenico Caracciolo, its archive was seized—and a year later, on 27 June 1783, more than 5,000 trial files went up in smoke [X3-09, X3-10, X3-11, X3-116]. Historian Vittorio Sciuti Russi calls the destruction "a political bonfire," dictated by reasons of state [X3-116]. Contemporaries such as Villabianca and Münter reported that the inquisitor general, Salvatore Ventimiglia, had requested it himself, and the island's aristocracy and senior clergy were only too glad to watch the pages burn, since those registers named every noble family that had served the court for privilege and blood-purity status [X3-116]. What historians rely on today are largely the annual summary reports (*relaciones de causas*) that Palermo dispatched to Madrid [X3-27].

For all the gothic reputation of the Steri dungeons, the tribunal's surviving tallies upend one of the most durable clichés about early modern Sicily. Over nearly three centuries, the court tried roughly 6,000 to 7,000 people, around 900 to 1,000 of them on charges of magic or witchcraft [X3-24, X3-33]. The Inquisition did not carry out executions itself; it "relaxed" the condemned to the king's officials, "the secular arm" [X3-35]. Yet for witchcraft, in nearly three hundred years, historian Maria Sofia Messana's count finds only two people handed over for execution—both of them men—while no woman is known to have been executed as a witch by the Sicilian tribunal [X3-35, X3-36].

Who, then, actually died at the stake? In its brutal early decades before about 1540, the tribunal hunted *conversos*—people accused of secretly keeping Jewish practices after converting to Christianity—handing over somewhere between 200 and 440 of them to be burned in person, depending on the years and count [X3-31]. Between 1540 and 1700, executions in person dropped sharply to between 25 and 52 people across all charges combined [X3-30]. Many of those victims were Protestants, whom the inquisitors called "Lutherans": historian Abbate counts 36 burned alive during the 1500s out of 253 tried [X3-117 (Abbate 2022, named)]. The overwhelming bulk of the court's everyday business, however, was far less dramatic and rarely fatal: in a letter of 26 April 1652, the Palermo inquisitors noted that the 207 culprits paraded in their public *autos de fe* were there "mostly blasphemy, bigamy and sorcery" [X3-40 (the inquisitors' letter, named)].

**The ladies from outside.** The strangest dossiers in those Madrid summaries concern the *donne di fuora*—"the ladies from outside"—whom Sicilians also called *patroneddri di casa* ("little masters of the house") or *donni di locu* ("ladies of the place") [X3-52]. Islanders described them as fairy women who roamed at night in small companies, slipping through closed doors invisibly "like a breath of air" to play with the children, open household chests, eat and drink, and leave a tidy home blessed [X3-56]. Three centuries later, Pitrè described them as "something of a fairy and something of a witch although, one cannot really distinguish which is which"—"odd and very capricious spirits, but not wicked by nature" [X3-49, X3-50]. Nor were they a late invention: as early as about 1450, a Sicilian manual for confessors instructed priests to ask penitents whether they believed in them [X3-48].

What brought ordinary Sicilians—mostly poor women, though men stood trial alongside them—into the tribunal's orbit was not malice, but neighborhood medicine [X3-46, X3-53]. According to historians Gustav Henningsen and Maria Sofia Messana, the people who claimed to ride with the companies were above all local healers: when a child fell ill because the fairies had taken offense, families sought out someone who traveled with them to set things right [X3-53 (Henningsen 2020 and Messana 2007, named)]. Testimony said the companies rode out on Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Saturdays—never Fridays or Sundays—with a great seasonal gathering on St John's Eve (23–24 June), and in the Spanish clerks' spelling their spirit rulers carried courtly names: Doña Inguanta, Doña Zabela, and King Cuco [X3-54, X3-55, X3-57 (Henningsen 2020, named)].

When those village narratives met the courtroom, two worlds talked past each other in tragic slow motion. In 1587, Lauria de Pavia, the elderly wife of a poor fisherman, stood before Palermo's inquisitors and described flying about six *palmos* above the ground all the way to Benevento on the mainland, where a red-haired young man and a beautiful woman reigned as King and Queen [X3-59, X3-60 (Henningsen 2020, named)]. To Lauria, she was recounting a nocturnal company of healing spirits; to inquisitors steeped in continental demonology, it sounded like an alarming conspiracy: "If what this woman says is true," they wrote to Madrid, "a new sect of witches has arisen" [X3-59 (Henningsen 2020, named)].

Dozens stood trial, men among them [X3-46, X3-114]. In 1624—the very plague summer in which Palermo's Senate was crowning Saint Rosalia—forty-year-old Catarina Calandrino, a lay member of the Franciscan third order, was denounced by eighteen witnesses; her company reportedly numbered twenty-six or twenty-seven people, and after the 1628 *auto de fe* she was sentenced to perpetual reclusion inside Palermo's great hospital [X3-61 (Henningsen 2020, named)]. Five years earlier, in 1619, summoned before a bishop's court in Sciacca after neighbors complained, Gracia la Chiaczisa left one unforgettable image of the ladies who haunted Sicilian sleep: "They look like people in front and empty on the back like the tiles of the roofs" [X3-68 (Geremia 2024, named)].

Henningsen reads the *donne di fuora* trials as evidence of a Sicilian fairy cult—a "white" sabbath, a shared body of cultural dreams that inquisitors forcibly translated into witchcraft, since before the Spanish tribunal Sicily had no native concept of the devil-worshipping witch [X3-72, X3-73]. Was it a form of shamanism? Henningsen argues no, because no trance technique is discernible in the records, though Ginzburg and other historians compare the night-riders to the *dominae nocturnae* who followed Herodias or Diana and to Friuli's *benandanti* [X3-72, X3-75, X3-78]. Either way, the trial files document a lived belief system of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries—not an unbroken cult surviving intact from classical antiquity [X3-110].

**The evil eye.** If the fairy companies faded from courtroom dockets after the Inquisition's abolition, the quieter anxieties that fed them—envy, sudden fright, and a child's inexplicable fever—never needed a court record to survive. Sicilians call the evil eye *ittatura* ("evil-casting") or *malocchiu*, and in the Madonie mountain town of Gratteri it is *l'ucchiatura* [X1-210, X1-247, X1-248]. Nothing about the fear itself is uniquely Sicilian: a 2017 Pew Research Center survey across fifteen Western European countries found a median of 16 percent believing in the evil eye (ranging from 9 percent in Denmark and Sweden to 33 percent in Spain and 48 percent in Portugal) [X1-179, X3-106]. What belongs to Sicily is the local dialect, the parish saints drafted into the remedy, and the endurance of the healers who knew the words.

To turn aside an envious gaze, early twentieth-century English collectors in Sicily documented coral amulets, silver-bell rattles for children, a mother's raised thumb and little finger when a stranger stared at her child, and the *cimaruta* [X1-176, X3-93 (Intorre 2025, named)]—a traditional southern Italian silver compound charm shaped like a branching sprig of rue (*ruta*), hung with miniature emblems such as a crescent moon, key, fish, serpent, flower, hand, rooster, or dagger [X1-268]. (Collectors around 1900 eagerly linked the *cimaruta* to the Roman goddess Diana, a survivals-era leap that modern scholarship treats with skepticism [X1-176, X3-93].)

**Healing with prayers.** Far more intimate than any silver charm were the prayers whispered in kitchen doorways. In Gratteri, perched in the Madonie mountains about seventy kilometers from Palermo, historian Marco Fragale collected healing formulas from women born between 1869 and 1923 [X1-247, X1-248 (Fragale 2021, named)]. The *ciarmavermi*—worm-charmers, whom Fragale describes as "empirical doctors"—treated children afflicted with intestinal worms [X1-248 (Fragale 2021, named), X1-172]. Tracing crosses on the child's belly and spitting three times, the healer recited in Sicilian: *"San Pantaleu, San Pantaliuni, / Vui siti lu medicu di nostru Signuri"* ("Saint Pantaleon, you are the doctor of Our Lord"), walking the prayer day by day through Holy Week to Easter Sunday, often paired with a necklace of seven garlic cloves because, as the folk maxim explained, *l'agliu ammazza i viermi*—garlic kills worms [X1-248 (Fragale 2021, named), X1-173].

_figure gratteri_

Healers also treated *scantu*, a sudden fright thought to throw the entire body out of balance [X1-209, X1-248]. Laying a hand on the patient's chest at *"a vucca di l'arma"* ("the mouth of the soul"), the healer appeared to slip into a trance while praying for three evenings in a row or once on a Friday, sometimes over a photograph of the patient; that formula was kept secret, passed on only to a trusted heir of the opposite sex [X1-248 (Fragale 2021, named)]. To diagnose *l'ucchiatura* or *malocchio*—a rite recorded across Sicily from Giuseppe Pitrè's 1896 *Medicina popolare siciliana* and Elsa Guggino's fieldwork to Fragale's Gratteri testimonies and Maffei's Aeolian ethnography—healers dropped olive oil into a shallow plate of water (*u piattinu*) while praying: if the drops held their round shape, the person was clear; if they spread or broke apart, the evil eye was confirmed [X3-215, X1-248]. In Gratteri, women then traced crosses on the forehead with the tongue, chanting *"Quattru uocchi e quattru pisci, / 'st'ucchiatura sinni sprisci"* ("Four eyes and four fish, may this evil eye vanish") [X1-248 (Fragale 2021, named)].

Nor are these practices merely nineteenth-century folklore. Between 2002 and 2006, researcher Napoli interviewed about twenty-five active *ciarmavermi* across five Sicilian provinces—mostly women over sixty alongside a handful of men in their seventies and eighties and one healer nearly a hundred—who held that they could not accept money for fear of losing their power, taking at most a gift in kind [X1-172, X3-99, X3-100, X3-104 (Napoli 2008, named)]. As practitioners themselves frame it, it is healing done with prayers [X1-172].

With morning breaking over the Madonie and Palermo's rooftops, the candles burn down, the prison registers close, and the stage lights return to the cathedral treasuries—where Sicily's devotion takes physical, gilded, and surprisingly portable form inside the relic cabinet.
