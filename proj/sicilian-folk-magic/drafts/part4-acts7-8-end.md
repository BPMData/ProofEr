## 10 · Act 7 · The relic cabinet opens

Morning returns, and with it we step back from the candlelit kitchens of the *ciarmavermi* to the gilded treasuries of Sicily's cathedrals. All through the island's three great city feasts we have been sliding drawers into a cabinet—Agatha's bones in Catania, Rosalia's in Palermo, Mary's lock of hair in Messina—without yet asking why Catholic cities went to such lengths to keep pieces of their saints close at hand. For most of Christian history, a relic was never a morbid curiosity or a museum souvenir: a relic is a saint's body, a fragment of it, or an object that touched the saint [X2-126], and as the historian Patrick Geary summarized medieval conviction, towns believed their saints quite literally lived on inside their bones and acted on behalf of the communities that possessed them [X2-152]. Keeping the saint present meant keeping the saint's protection on your side of the city wall; consequently, cities treasured relics, bargained for them, and now and then stole them outright [X2-152, X2-156].

**Meet Lucy.** Before opening the full cabinet, one last Sicilian martyr needs her own drawer: Lucy of Syracuse, Catania's rival and sister in antiquity. By Christian tradition she was put to death in her native city in 304 (the 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* notes 303 as an alternative) during the persecutions under the emperor Diocletian, and her feast falls on 13 December [X1-251]. Honored as patron of Syracuse and of eyesight—medieval painters regularly depicted her carrying her own eyes upon a dish—she rests on an early legend whose fifth-century *Acts* even the *Catholic Encyclopedia* admitted in 1910 "cannot unfortunately be accepted without criticism" [X1-251, X2-45].

**One saint, two bodies.** Yet whatever historians make of her fifth-century biography, Lucy's afterlife turns the relic cabinet into a comedy of geography, because by our own side-by-side count of church traditions, two European cities claim her entire body at once [X2-65]. Venice maintains that crusaders brought Lucy's body from Constantinople after sacking the Byzantine capital in 1204 (officially translating the relics in 1206, some Venetian accounts add); today she lies in the Venetian church of San Geremia inside a glass case, her face covered by a silver mask while her bare, dried feet remain visible at the far end of her robe [X2-47, X2-48, X2-55, X2-157]. Across the Alps in France, however, the abbey of St Vincent in Metz preserves a rival western tradition set down by the eleventh-century monk Sigebert of Gembloux: by that account, a Duke Faroald of Spoleto carried Lucy's body north from Sicily to Corfinium, from which it reached Metz in 972 under Emperor Otto I [X2-59, X2-60]. Syracuse itself, where she was supposedly martyred and laid to rest, keeps only the empty tomb—though under an agreement with the Venetian patriarchate, as *Avvenire* reported in 2024, Venice lends the body back to Syracuse roughly once every ten years, as it did in 2004, 2014, and December 2024, when Syracuse's city council politely asked to keep her permanently and the archdiocese politely declined [X2-56, X2-58, X2-66]. Even her head is claimed twice when separate records are laid next to each other: the Comune di Venezia notes that thieves who stole Lucy's body from San Geremia on 7 November 1981 left the head and its silver mask behind (before the body was recovered on 13 December, her feast day), yet the 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* records that Bourges Cathedral in France claimed Venice had given Lucy's head to King Louis XII in 1513, alongside another account tracing it from Rome [X2-55, X2-51]. Both doubles are our own comparison from records that rarely compare notes [X2-65, X2-51, X2-55].

**Agatha adds up.** Compared with Lucy's bi-located corpse, Catania's Agatha looks remarkably well-accounted-for—at least at the level of major bones. One Catania tourism list counts 31 places outside the city—17 across Italy and 14 abroad in six other countries—that claim a piece of her [X2-43]. Yet when you check the large parts against human anatomy, the arithmetic holds rather than multiplies: Catania's 1376 gilded silver bust encloses her skull and a piece of her pelvis, while Maria Concetta Di Natale notes two femur reliquaries resting inside a large chest in Catania [X2-18, X2-23, X2-24, X2-25]; Palermo Cathedral holds what the scholar Paolo Palazzotto identifies as her arm [X2-41]; and while Agatha's legend holds that her torturers cut off her breasts, Archbishop Luigi Renna of Catania confirmed in 2026 that one breast relic is venerated in Galatina in Apulia while Catania's tourism list claims the other [X1-250, X2-37, X2-43]. Spread far and wide, yes; anatomically impossible, not quite [X2-23, X2-37, X2-41, X2-43].

_cabinet louis_

**A king, boiled.** Nor was division reserved for early martyrs: medieval royalty underwent the same surgical piety. When King Louis IX of France died on crusade at Tunis in 1270, Charles of Anjou—then ruler of Sicily—ordered the royal corpse dissected and boiled so that the clean bones could be carried back to the French royal abbey of Saint-Denis near Paris [X1-66, X2-115, X2-117, X2-118]. What remained behind was laid to rest in Sicily's great Norman cathedral of Monreale above Palermo: certainly his entrails, and according to his confessor Geoffroy de Beaulieu his heart as well, though the modern historian Jacques Le Goff argued it was more likely the heart traveled north to Saint-Denis with the bones [X2-115, X2-116].

Palermo Cathedral says its Chapel of the Relics alone holds 17 reliquaries—six silver-topped urns, the urn of Saint Cosmas, and ten carved wooden cases—yet no census has ever counted the reliquaries across the whole island, leaving each local treasury a small world unto itself [X2-97 (Palermo Cathedral, named)].

**Why Europe wanted bones.** It had not always been normal to slice up the dead and trade them across seas. In 386 a Roman imperial decree, later preserved in the Theodosian Code, flatly forbade the sale of martyrs' bodies [X2-135, X2-153]. Two centuries later, in 594, Pope Gregory the Great wrote to the Byzantine empress Constantina politely refusing her request for the head of Saint Paul: "it is not the custom of the Romans, when giving relics, to presume to touch any part of the body," he insisted, sending instead a cloth (*brandeum*) that had lain near the tomb and filings from Saint Peter's chains, while adding that he could "scarcely believe" Greek churchmen actually dug up saints' bones—though recent historians such as Freeman (2025) note that this neat East–West divide did not always hold everywhere in practice [X2-154]. Hunger for physical presence eventually won. In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea ruled in its seventh canon that every church altar must contain relics, threatening any bishop who consecrated a church without them with deposition [X2-155]. Once every altar required bone, relics became urgent civic property, bought, traded, and lifted across medieval Europe. In his classic 1978 study *Furta Sacra*, the historian Patrick Geary explained why medieval Christians so often applauded **furta sacra**—"holy theft": if a saint allowed herself to be carried away, she had chosen her new home, which helps explain why Papasidero notes Catania's 1126 recovery of Agatha as rare among thefts of female saints, and why Catania's own archbishop could still refer to it in 2026 as a *"sacro furto"* [X2-156 (Geary 1978, named), X2-15, X2-16].

Far from halting dismemberment, medieval and early modern popes mostly tried to regulate the traffic. Even after Rosalia's bones surfaced in 1624, the historian Ilaria Scalisi notes, Palermo sent fragments to other Sicilian towns, European courts, Antwerp, and Pope Urban VIII himself, who reportedly wore one of her teeth on his chest [X2-91 (Scalisi 2023, named)]. Earlier, the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 had forbidden exhibiting old relics outside their vessels or putting them up for sale, and required papal approval for newly discovered ones [X2-133]. In Session 25 in December 1563, the **Council of Trent** reaffirmed that saints' bodies "are to be venerated," while commanding bishops to strip away "every superstition," abolish "all filthy lucre," and inspect any newly claimed relic before admitting it [X2-134 (Council of Trent, Session 25, named)].

**The critics.** To Protestant reformers of the sixteenth century, regulation merely polished a lucrative racket. In 1543 John Calvin published his *Treatise on Relics* in Geneva, a crisp little octavo whose full French title argued that Christendom would profit handsomely from an honest inventory of every saint's body and relic [X2-137, X2-138, X2-139]. Gather every splinter of the True Cross across Europe, Calvin scoffed, and you would have enough timber for *"la charge d'un bon grand bateau"*—a whole ship's cargo; every apostle possessed more than four bodies, each saint at least two or three, and Calvin himself tallied fourteen nails from the Crucifixion [X2-140, X2-141, X2-142]. Modern writers routinely credit Calvin with counting "about a dozen" heads of John the Baptist as well, yet checking his text shows that count actually comes from a wry footnote added by his nineteenth-century English translator, Valerian Krasinski [X2-145].

**The rules today.** Modern Catholic law is far stricter than the medieval marketplace. The 1983 *Code of Canon Law* declares without qualification, "It is absolutely forbidden to sell sacred relics," and bars giving away or permanently transferring major relics without the Apostolic See's consent [X2-121, X2-122]. The Vatican's 2017 instruction, *Relics in the Church: Authenticity and Conservation*, likewise outlaws both barter and sale, forbids displaying relics without certificates of authenticity, and prohibits dividing a saint's body unless Rome gives explicit permission [X2-125, X2-127, X2-128, X2-129]. Tellingly, the 2017 instruction recognizes only two legal tiers: "significant" relics (*insigni*—the body, notable parts of it, or the entirety of the ashes) and "non-significant" ones (*non insigni*—small fragments or objects that touched the saint) [X2-126]. The popular three-tier ladder of first-, second-, and third-class relics that readers often meet in parish pamphlets is a widespread devotional convention, not the Church's own current law [X2-132].

All those drawers—bones, veils, teeth, and gilded urns—did not stay penned inside Sicily's harbors; they moved along the sea lanes of empire, and when Spain ruled both Sicily and half the Americas, Sicilian saints boarded the same ships.

## 11 · Act 8 · To the Americas

Walk into a church in Salvador da Bahia, Lima, Puebla, or Cabimas, and the kinship with Sicily hits you before a word is spoken: walls blazing with gilt woodwork, banks of flickering votive candles, painted miracle tablets, and processions that pull an entire neighborhood into the street. It is tempting to credit all that shared baroque theater to nineteenth-century Sicilian emigrants arriving with their hometown saints packed in steerage. Yet the truth is older, stranger, and rooted in imperial politics: from 1516 to 1713 Sicily belonged to the Spanish crown [X1-71]. For two centuries the same Madrid administration that governed Sicily's viceroys and ran Sicily's branch of the Spanish Inquisition also governed Mexico, Peru, and New Granada [X3-115, X3-116]. Officials literally rotated between the two worlds—Francisco Fernández de la Cueva, 8th Duke of Alburquerque, served as Viceroy of New Spain from 1653 to 1660 before arriving in Palermo as Viceroy of Sicily from 1667 to 1670 [X3-120]—and Sicilian saints crossed the Atlantic along those imperial corridors long before steamships existed [X3-211].

**Benedict of San Fratello.** The most extraordinary traveler of all never set foot on a ship in his lifetime. Born in 1524 (some older lives say 1526) in the mountain town of San Fratello to African parents who had been enslaved—biographies differ on whether he was born free or emancipated at eighteen—Benedict spent his youth herding sheep before joining a band of Franciscan-affiliated lay hermits led by Girolamo Lanza near San Fratello and later on Monte Pellegrino above Palermo [X3-122, X3-123, X3-124, X3-125, X3-216]. When Pope Pius IV disbanded independent hermit groups in the early 1560s, Benedict entered the Observant Franciscan friary of Santa Maria di Gesù in Palermo as an illiterate lay brother and went to work in the kitchen [X3-216, X3-217]. Yet such was the quiet authority of this friary cook that in 1578 his fellow Franciscans elected him guardian—the superior of the entire convent—and master of novices, an office he discharged with reluctant wisdom before returning joyfully to his pots [X3-217]. By the time he died at Santa Maria di Gesù on 4 April 1589, all of Palermo knew his door: the poor came for alms, while nobles and Spanish viceroys alike sought his healing prayers and counsel [X3-126, X3-218].

Rome moved at its own deliberate pace: although Sicily's viceroy, the Duke of Escalona, petitioned King Philip III and an inquisitorial inquiry opened in 1594, Benedict was not beatified until May 1743 or canonized by Pope Pius VII until 24 May 1807 [X3-121, X3-127, X3-128]. Devotion on the ground refused to wait two centuries for parchment. By 1606 relics of Benedict had already been sent to Spain, where Lope de Vega dramatized his life onstage in *El Santo negro Rosambuco de la ciudad de Palermo* by 1607 [X3-130, X3-131]; in 1652 Palermo's own Senate proclaimed him patron of the city [X3-218]. More remarkably, Benedict became the adopted champion of Black Catholics across the Iberian Atlantic. By 1609 a confraternity of São Benedito was established at the Mosteiro de Santa Joana in Lisbon, and in 1619 visiting senators from Palermo watched enslaved Africans in Lisbon march behind the banner of a Black Sicilian friar [X3-134, X3-135]. From the early 1600s, Franciscan convents and Black Catholic brotherhoods carried his cult across Brazil—to Salvador da Bahia, Recife, Rio de Janeiro (where the São Benedito confraternity formally united with the Rosary brotherhood in 1668–69), and south to Colônia do Sacramento in modern Uruguay by 1680—as well as back across the ocean to Angola [X3-136, X3-140, X3-141, X3-142, X3-143]. In eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Brazil, as the historian Lucilene Reginaldo writes, devotion to São Benedito "broke free of the friars and became hugely popular among Black people," while Giovanna Fiume calls him "the champion of the religion of enslaved Black Africans throughout the Spanish Empire" [X3-142, X3-145]. In western Venezuela—across Zulia, Trujillo, and Mérida, where Spaniards introduced his devotion in the late colonial period—his festive cycle runs from October to 2 February to the thunder of flags, maracas, flutes, and the seven or eight *chimbangueles* drums whose very name scholars trace to the Bantu *Imbangala* [X3-155, X3-156, X3-158, X3-159, X3-160].

**Antonio of Noto.** Benedict was not even Sicily's first African-descended Franciscan to make that crossing. Born around 1490 to a Muslim family in the Barca mountains of Cyrenaica in modern Libya, Antonio was captured by Sicilian galleys and enslaved on the island [X3-147, X3-219]. Put to work as a shepherd near Avola and Noto, he converted to Christianity, won his freedom for his integrity, and spent his remaining years as a Franciscan tertiary and hermit in the countryside near Noto, caring for the sick, the poor, and prisoners until his death in March 1549 or 1550 [X3-148, X3-219]. Thirty-eight witnesses testified to his sanctity in April 1549, and in 1599 the Sicilian Inquisition granted leave for his image to be painted [X3-149, X3-154]. Yet while Antonio's cult gradually faded in Noto itself, as Fiume notes, Black brotherhoods from the seventeenth century onward carried him across the Atlantic—and today in Brazil, inside churches of Our Lady of the Rosary of Black Men from Salvador to Ouro Preto and Tiradentes, he is still venerated as *Santo Antônio de Categeró* [X3-146, X3-150, X3-151]. Popular Brazilian accounts often state flatly that *Categeró* means the Sicilian hill town of Caltagirone, though the scholar Zarattini offers that link only as a cautious guess—a possible garbling of Caltagirone or a confusion with a friar Antônio Caltanisseta [X3-152, X3-153].

**Rosalia** crossed to New Spain along those same imperial and Jesuit channels, though her Atlantic passage began later in the seventeenth century [X3-166]. After Jaime de Palafox y Cardona moved from the archbishopric of Palermo (from 1677) to Seville (1684–1701) and planted her feast there, painters in New Spain such as Juan Tinoco in Puebla and Juan Correa (around 1695) were already painting Palermo's plague-saint [X3-169, X3-171]. As a Los Angeles County Museum of Art catalogue notes, "Sicilian missionaries introduced her cult to the viceroyalty," where floods, earthquakes, and epidemics drove wide devotion by the mid-eighteenth century—reaching as far north as New Mexico and outliving the 1767 expulsion of the Jesuits [X3-167, X3-168]. Still, it is easy to overread every New World place-name: Baja California's Mission Santa Rosalía de Mulegé was founded in 1705 by Father Basaldúa rather than the Sicilian Jesuit Francisco María Píccolo (who wrote from Santa Rosalía in 1717), and no surviving document proves Sicilian Jesuits chose the name for their home saint [X3-174, X3-177, X3-178, X3-180]. Meanwhile, traffic around Saint Joseph ran largely from the Spanish center outward: in 1555 the First Mexican Provincial Council named **Saint Joseph** general patron of "this new Church" in New Spain, and images of Joseph surged after the **Council of Trent** around 1600 [X3-181, X3-182]. As the folklorist Luisa Del Giudice notes, Joseph's prominence in Sicily was itself "considered to be largely the product of Spain"—yet the elaborate Sicilian St Joseph's tables have "no counterpart in Spain or its colonies" [X3-183, X1-148].

Why, then, do people so often credit nineteenth-century Sicilian emigrants with carrying these saints to Argentina, Brazil, and Venezuela? Because the dates get telescoped in memory. As Anna Maria Ratti's 1931 migration study documents, when mass emigration left Sicily between 1876 and 1926, Sicilians went overwhelmingly to the United States and Canada, while Argentina and Brazil drew heavy shares from northern Italy; and the transplanted southern Italian village cults that did arrive with later migrants in Argentina and Uruguay—such as San Cono of Teggiano—came from Campania or Calabria rather than Sicily [X3-205, X3-210, X3-211]. Sicily's saints had already crossed the Atlantic two to three centuries earlier, inside Spanish and Portuguese imperial networks [X3-211].

**Check:** Sicilian emigrants carried these saints to the Americas · they crossed centuries earlier through Spanish and Portuguese networks [X3-211]

What about the uncanny visual echo between Sicily and Latin America—the forests of votive candles, the baroque gold, the painted miracle plaques, the sugar treats of 2 November? Here, too, resemblance is not the same as export [X3-203]. No scholar traces that shared look to a Sicilian blueprint: votive candles and painted ex-votos—*retablos* in Spanish America, *miraculi* in Sicily—belonged to a pan-Catholic European habit brought to the colonies in the sixteenth century and turned up to full volume by post-Trent baroque piety [X3-201, X3-203]. Nor did Sicily invent Mexico's Day of the Dead or borrow its *pupi di zùccaru* from Mexico: historians such as Stanley Brandes argue that Mexico's 2 November celebrations grew largely out of the colonial European All Souls' feast combined with Indigenous traditions and Spanish sugar-paste (*alfeñique*) craft, with no recorded transfer in either direction between Sicily and Mexico [X3-192, X3-195, X3-199, X1-162]. St Joseph's tables never took root in Latin America at all [X3-189], though in the United States Sicilian women did build them in a largely Latino parish in East Los Angeles, and Black Catholics in New Orleans were building St Joseph altars in Tremé by 1945 [X3-186, X3-187, X3-188]. Across two hemispheres, an empire-wide Church gave different peoples the same liturgy, saints, and baroque stagecraft—and each place turned them to its own necessities [X3-203].

## 12 · Curtain call

Put away the Egyptian tarot cards and the Victorian goddesses, then, because the island left behind in the archives is far richer—and far more human—than the exotic mirage outsiders invented for it [X1-219, X1-94, X1-95]. Across an unbroken succession of foreign crowns, from Byzantines and Arabs to Normans and Spaniards [X1-56, X1-58, X1-60, X1-71], ordinary Sicilians did not quietly preserve someone else's paganism under Catholic robes [X1-19]; they took the liturgy, relics, canon law, and feast calendar of a universal Church and bent them to the immediate urgencies of their own streets—lifting a martyr's veil against Mount Etna's lava [X2-28, X2-29b], voting an unverified hermit onto Palermo's civic throne seven months before her bones were certified [X2-70, X2-76], claiming a forged letter from Mary in the teeth of Vatican censors [X1-123, X1-125, X1-126], turning repentant convicts on the scaffold into protective watchmen by the Oreto [X1-230, X1-231, X1-232], and bargaining with night fairies and saints alike when a child fell ill [X3-53, X1-248].

What looks from a distance like timeless superstition turns out, up close, to be something much nearer to craft: the patient, inventive work of an island making an empire-spanning religion answer in its own dialect. Whether inside a gilded silver bust in Catania [X2-18], a crowded kitchen at Santa Maria di Gesù in Palermo [X3-217], or the thunder of *chimbangueles* drums across the Atlantic in Venezuela [X3-158], the story of Sicily's saints and spells is not a museum of dead gods—it is the record of living people refusing to face plague, hunger, fear, and power empty-handed.

## Glosses (tap to open; each adds something the sentence doesn't say)
- *syncretism*: From the Greek *synkretizein* ("to act like Cretans"): Erasmus revived the word in a 1519 letter to Melanchthon to mean Christian scholars uniting across differences; in the 17th century Protestant theological quarrels turned it into an insult, and only in the 19th century did it take on its modern sense of a religious mixture [X1-01, X1-02, X1-03, X1-04 (Levinskaya 1993, named)].
- *intercession*: In Catholic theology, saints are petitioners rather than independent miracle-workers: the Catechism (§956) specifies that they offer God the merits they earned on earth "through the one mediator… Christ Jesus," so praying to a saint means asking an advocate to plead your case, not bypassing God [X1-227 (the Catechism, named)].
- *sacco*: The full outfit worn by Agatha's devotees in Catania consists of five pieces—a white tunic, black velvet cap, cord, white gloves, and handkerchief (Di Giovanni 2007). While Maurice's medieval account puts Catania's clergy in white robes (*"in albis vestibus"*) in 1126, the historian Francesco Benigno dates the lay *sacco*, cap, and gloves to the 18th century [X1-92, X2-12, X1-93, X2-34].
- *festino*: Palermo's numbered *festino* counts from the first procession of 1625 (making 2024 its 400th edition), though historians note the civic-political festival took shape around 1649 and stretched to four days in 1693. It also bends with civic grief and migration: in 1992, after the murders of Falcone and Borsellino, Palermo replaced fireworks with a torchlight procession, and today Tamil Hindu devotees join the feast, honoring Rosalia as a "Great Mother" [X1-114, X1-115, X1-118, X1-119, X1-120].
- *carro*: Palermo's towering triumphal float for Saint Rosalia: the architect Paolo Amato's design was first rolled out in 1686 and rebuilt annually from 1693 (Jean Houël's 1780s print shows one version on the stage). In 2023, the city of Palermo gifted a *carro* that landed at New York's Pier 85 for the diaspora feast [X1-115, X1-116, X3-212 (Salerno 2024, named)].
- *acchianata*: Literally "the climb" in Sicilian: the night pilgrimage on foot up Monte Pellegrino to Rosalia's hilltop cave on 3–4 September, the eve of her traditional death day—marking the older hermitage site where a modest local cult already existed before the 1624 plague [X1-106, X1-107, X1-116, X1-117, X1-203].
- *vara*: A processional float. Alongside the silver *vara* that carries the Holy Hair on 3 June, Messina's monumental Assumption *Vara* on 15 August—escorted by the giant figures Mata and Grifone—is a pyramidal machine roughly 14 meters tall and 8 tons heavy, mounted on iron sleds (*cippu*) with no wheels or brakes and hauled about 3 km over wetted asphalt by roughly 1,000 devotees pulling two long hemp ropes [X1-131, X1-133, X1-204, X1-256, X1-257].
- *virgineddi*: "Little virgins": the honored guests at a St Joseph's table, traditionally poor children or orphans dressed as Jesus, Mary, and Joseph—or unmarried young people in the Madonie mountains, and family children in western New York's diaspora altars. Because 19 March falls in Lent, the banquet they are served first is strictly meatless, featuring sculpted breads, *pasta con le sarde* or *con la mollica* (toasted breadcrumbs as "sawdust"), whole fish, and fried artichokes [X1-144, X1-205, X1-258, X1-259].
- *vampata*: Literally a "blaze": the St Joseph's Eve bonfire lit on 18 March across Sicilian neighborhoods (Palermo's *Vampata di San Giuseppe* is entry no. 96 on Sicily's heritage register). Folklorists from Pitrè in 1900 onward declared the fires "certainly pagan," though modern scholars such as Luisa Del Giudice and Dorothy Noyes argue that pre-Christian descent cannot be proven from seasonal bonfires alone [X1-147, X1-150, X1-206].
- *decollati*: In Palermo dialect, *armi di li corpi decullati* ("souls of the beheaded bodies"); in Acireale they were called the *beati* ("the blessed"), and in Trapani the *armiceddi* ("little souls"). For centuries Palermo displayed executed bodies at the Sperone outside the city until a 1788 viceregal ban, and a head-pyramid stood at the execution ground from at least 1604 until the Senate moved it in 1779, near the Oreto river church where the dead were buried [X1-207, X1-154, X1-155, X1-156 (Fatta 2016, named)].
- *good thief*: In Luke's Gospel (23:39–43), one of two criminals crucified alongside Jesus rebukes the other, accepts his own sentence as just, and asks Jesus to remember him—receiving the promise of paradise "today." In Sicilian street theology, that single scene turned a repentant felon on the scaffold into a potential intercessor whose execution began his purgatorial purification [X1-236, X1-231].
- *pupi di zùccaru*: Cast and painted sugar figurines set inside a *canistru* (gift basket) with fruit and nuts on 2 November—traditionally a sugar doll (*pupa*) for a girl and a mounted knight (*cavaliere*) for a boy, as oral testimonies collected by Campanella (2023) record. Alongside them often sit almond-paste *frutta martorana*, which Palermitans like to say were invented by Martorana nuns to fake out-of-season fruit for a visiting dignitary, though no historical document backs the tale [X1-162, X1-163, X1-212, X1-166].
- *miraculu*: Sicilian for "miracle," used as the dialect word for a votive offering of thanks. As the Palermo scholar Ignazio E. Buttitta notes, painted tablets depict the crisis, the person spared, and the heavenly protector in a "standing declaration of debt" to the saint; one surviving painted-tin *miraculu* for an escape on 23 November 1890 is catalogued in the Casa-Museo Antonino Uccello, acquired by Sicily's regional government in 1983 [X1-168, X1-169, X1-211].
- *donne di fuora*: Also called *donni di locu* ("ladies of the place") or *patroneddri di casa* ("little masters of the house"). Their grand yearly assembly fell on St John's Eve (23–24 June), summoned by the "Great Fairy of Messina," while in Palermo their meeting place was known as the *Cortile delle sette fate* (Courtyard of the Seven Fairies); in 1656 the bishop of Agrigento barred unmarried girls from leaving home that night, fined their fathers four ounces, and ordered church doors locked at 2 a.m. [X3-52, X3-54, X3-70 (Geremia 2024, named)].
- *ittatura* / *malocchiu*: "Evil-casting" and "evil eye." In Sam Migliore's 1997 study of some 25,000 Sicilian-Canadians in Hamilton, Ontario—mostly tracing ancestry to Racalmuto—informants explained *ittatura* as sitting between *disgrazia* (misfortune) and *sortilegio* (power to disrupt events), cast not only by witches but by ordinary neighbors whose envy turns sharp. Far from uniquely Sicilian, a 2017 Pew survey of 24,599 adults across 15 Western European countries found a 16% median belief in the evil eye, reaching 48% in Portugal and 33% in Spain [X3-90, X3-91, X1-175, X1-179, X3-106].
- *ciarmavermi*: Healers who charm away childhood intestinal worms, which Sicilian belief often links to sudden fright (*scantu*). In 2002–2006 fieldwork across five Sicilian provinces, Maria Concetta Napoli interviewed around 25 practitioners—mostly women over 60 alongside men in their 70s and 80s and one healer nearly 100—who said their prayers could be taught only on specific ritual days (such as seven consecutive Fridays or Christmas midnight Mass), passed to a disciple who could use them only after the master died, and never paid in cash lest the gift be lost [X1-172, X3-99, X3-100, X3-102, X3-103, X3-104 (Napoli 2008, named)].
- *cimaruta*: Literally "sprig of rue": a compound silver amulet shaped like a branching sprig of the herb *ruta*, hung with miniature protective charms—crescent moon, key, fish, serpent, flower or vervain, hand, rooster, or dagger—pinned especially to infants' clothes against *malocchio*. Primarily Neapolitan and southern Italian, it was recorded in early-20th-century Sicily by English collectors who tied it to "Diana Triformis," a classic survivalist leap rather than a documented lineage [X1-268, X3-93, X1-176 (Intorre 2025, named)].
- *chimbangueles*: The ensemble of seven or eight drums—accompanied by flags, maracas, flutes, songs, and dances—that leads San Benito's processions in Venezuela's Zulia, Trujillo, and Mérida from October through 2 February; scholars trace the name to the Bantu *Imbangala* [X3-158, X3-159, X3-160].
