## Summary

- **Agatha (Catania)** has the best-documented relic history. Her body was taken to Constantinople by the Byzantine
  general George Maniakes in 1040 (1039 in some popular accounts). Two soldiers stole it back and brought it to
  Catania on 17 August 1126. The main record is Bishop Maurice's *Historia translationis* (BHL 139). The best English
  scholarly source is Marco Papasidero, *Thefts of Relics in Italy* (Amsterdam UP, 2025, open access), pp. 142–146.
  Catania keeps the head and thorax in a silver bust (1376, by Giovanni di Bartolo), the limbs in separate
  reliquaries, a silver casket (1576), and the veil. Believers hold that the veil stops Etna's lava. Pieces of Agatha
  are claimed in at least 17 other Italian places and 6 other countries (one city-government page; not checked
  elsewhere).
- **Lucy (Syracuse)** is the clearest case of one saint's body claimed in two places. There are two medieval
  traditions. (a) Western, from Sigebert of Gembloux: Lucy's body went to Corfinio and then to Metz in 972. (b) Venetian:
  Maniakes took it to Constantinople in about 1039, and crusaders took it to Venice in 1204 or 1205. On top of that, her
  head was said to be at Bourges from 1513. Venice's body was stolen in November 1981 (all but the head) and
  recovered on 13 December 1981. It visited Syracuse in 2004. Syracuse itself keeps relics in a silver statue (lead
  only).
- **Rosalia (Palermo)** has no medieval relic history: her bones were *found* on 15 July 1624, during a plague. A first
  church commission is reported to have found the bones of three men. A second commission declared them hers on
  11 February 1625, and they were carried in procession on 9 June 1625. The story that the geologist William
  Buckland called them goat bones in 1825 is popular but traced only to a late anecdote, and it is unverified.
- **Monreale** holds the heart and entrails of King Louis IX of France (d. Tunis, 1270), according to a Cornell
  University Press monograph. I saw that only as a citation and did not open the book.
- **Church rules:** the 1983 Code of Canon Law, canon 1190, says "It is absolutely forbidden to sell sacred relics."
  Earlier rules say the same: the Fourth Lateran Council (1215, c. 62), the Council of Trent (1563, "all filthy lucre be
  abolished"), and the 2017 Vatican Instruction. The Vatican's own 2017 text sorts relics into "significant" and
  "non-significant". It does **not** use the popular "first / second / third class" scheme.
- **Critics:** Calvin's *Treatise on Relics* (1543) mocks duplicate relics: "a whole ship's cargo" of the true cross,
  and "every apostle had more than four bodies". His 19th-century translator counts "about a dozen whole heads of St
  John the Baptist". The text I checked does not mention any Sicilian relic.
- **Counts of reliquaries in Sicily (C3):** none found. Not researched, because the budget ran out.

## Disagreements

1. **Date Maniakes took Agatha (and Lucy):** 1040 (Papasidero 2025, scholarly) vs 1039 (NLM 2023), with 1038–39 for
   Lucy (History Walks). Popular pages tie both saints to the same campaign. The difference is probably which campaign
   season each writer counts. Papasidero also notes that the Byzantine historians don't record the removal at all.
2. **Year of Agatha's return:** 1126 (Comune di Catania). Papasidero gives "17 August" under John II Komnenos
   (r. 1118–43) and no year in the part I read. These don't conflict, but 1126 has only the city's page behind it in
   this pass.
3. **Where Agatha's breast was lost or kept:** at Taranto, found by a child (Papasidero, on the medieval account) vs
   Gallipoli 1126–1389, then Galatina (Comune di Catania, on the later local tradition). These may be different layers
   of the legend. Pass 2 should check the Galatina tradition in Italian sources.
4. **Date of Bishop Maurice's *Historia translationis*:** the summary of Papasidero gave "c. 1155–1169", but Maurice
   was bishop 1122–1143/44. Likely a copy's date or a misreading on my side. Re-read the page.
5. **Date of Andrea Dandolo's chronicle:** given in my summary as the "first half of the 13th century"; it is usually
   dated to the 14th century. Check.
6. **Where Lucy's body is:** Metz via Corfinio (Sigebert, 11th–12th c.) vs Venice via Constantinople (Venetian
   tradition, Dandolo), plus a head at Bourges (from 1513, or from Rome). Each tradition is medieval. I found no modern
   scholar in this pass who settles it. Bridge (CE 1910): "the subsequent history of the relics is not clear."
7. **Year Lucy moved to San Geremia:** 1860 (NLM), "1860s" (History Walks), 1861 (Wikipedia citing *The Independent*);

8. **Who led the Rosalia dig:** Geronima La Cattuta (Jay 2021) vs "a woman also named Rosalia" (Anello 2024). Jay
   says the accounts are inconsistent, so the disagreement goes back to the 17th-century sources.
9. **Rosalia's death year:** 1170 (Anello 2024) vs 1166 (Wikipedia lead). Neither gives the medieval evidence.
10. **Year of Calvin's treatise:** 1543 (Musée protestant / Backus edition, and the brief) vs 1553 (Cambridge UL

    Backus-edition source.

## Myths and popular claims

- **"Rosalia's bones are really a goat's" (Buckland, 1825): unsupported as stated / unverified.** It traces to a late,
  second-hand Oxford anecdote: Tuckwell's *Reminiscences of Oxford* (1900), as linked by a 2009 science blog. Gordon's
  1894 *Life and Correspondence of William Buckland* does list "Santa Rosalia" in its chapter IV contents, so Buckland
  did have some Rosalia episode, but I couldn't read the passage. Nothing found says Buckland handled the bones in
  Palermo's cathedral urn, as opposed to bones shown at the shrine. Fine as a labeled anecdote. Don't present it as fact.
- **"Agatha's veil stopped Etna's lava in 252 and since": a belief, recorded and still held.** The University of Teramo
  page and Ruiz Jiménez (2025) record the belief and the 1669 and 1886 processions. The *earliest written record* of the
  252 story isn't traced in this pass. Present it as what Catanians hold, with the events where the veil was carried
  as documented history.
- **"Maniakes stole Lucy along with Agatha": contested / under-sourced.** For Agatha, the Latin sources (Orderic,
  William of Malmesbury, Maurice) support it, and the Greek historians are silent (Papasidero). For Lucy, the pages I
  read give it without a primary source. Sigebert's Western tradition puts her body in Metz by 972, which would mean
  nothing of Lucy was left in Syracuse for Maniakes to take. This is a real, old dispute, not a modern myth.
- **"Relics come in first, second and third class, by Church law": unsupported as a legal claim.** The Vatican's 2017
  Instruction uses only "significant" and "non-significant". The class scheme is a popular devotional classification,
  and its origin isn't traced in this pass.
- **"Calvin said there was enough true cross to fill a ship": supported** by the text (both the 1561 and 19th-c.
  translations). **"A dozen heads of John the Baptist":** a 19th-c. translator's tally in a footnote, not Calvin's own
  count, so attribute it correctly.
- **"Rosalia stopped the plague": belief, documented.** The city's 1625 procession and the reported fall in cases are
  how Palermo told it (Anello; Jay). Report it as what Palermitans held and still celebrate (the Festino).

## Belief notes (for respectful framing)

- Catania: devotees hold that Agatha protects the city from Etna's lava, with the veil as the means. Healing miracles at
  the returned relics are recorded from the 12th century (Blandinus).
- Palermo: in 1624–25 the city held that finding and processing Rosalia's bones would end the plague. The relics'
  procession remains the core of her feast.
- Church teaching (Trent; the 2017 Instruction): relics are venerated, not worshipped, and God grants benefits
  "through" them. They must be authenticated by the bishop, and must never be sold.
