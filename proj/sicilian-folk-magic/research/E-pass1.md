## Short summary

**Verdict: there is real substance for a main section, but it is narrower than "Spain made them both gaudy".**
The documented links are specific and can be told as stories with dates:

1. **Same crown, same machinery.** Sicily and Spanish America were run by the same monarchy and the same Spanish
   Inquisition. Henry Charles Lea's 1908 history treats the Sicilian tribunal (from about 1487 to 1782) and the
   tribunals of Lima (1570), Mexico (1571) and Cartagena (1610) as one family of institutions, in one book.
2. **Sicilian saints really did travel to Latin America**, and this is the strongest direct link. Two Black
   Franciscan hermits of 16th-century Sicily, **Benedict "the Moor" of San Fratello/Palermo** (d. 1589) and
   **Antonio "the Ethiopian" of Noto** (d. 1549/50), became major devotions of enslaved and free Black Catholics in
   Portugal, Brazil and Spanish America, carried by Iberian Black Rosary confraternities (brotherhoods) from the early
   1600s. In Brazil Antonio is still "Santo Antônio de Categeró". **Saint Rosalia of Palermo** was spread by the
   Jesuits across Spain's American viceroyalties in the 17th–18th centuries, reaching New Mexico; a Baja California
   mission bore her name by 1717, and a Palermo-born Jesuit (Píccolo) worked in those missions.
3. **Shared grandparents rather than parent and child.** The cult of **Saint Joseph** was pushed hard by
   Spain after the Council of Trent; one folklorist argues Spanish rule helped root Joseph "in Sicilian soil", and Joseph
   was proclaimed patron of New Spain in 1555. Mexico's **Day of the Dead** is argued by the leading anthropologist
   on it (Brandes) to be largely a colonial, Spanish-derived form of the European All Souls' feast, which makes it a
   cousin of Sicily's Festa dei Morti, not a descendant. The Jesuits even called southern Italy "the Indies down
   here" and used mission methods there that echoed the New World (Selwyn, on Naples).
4. **Migration is a weak link for Sicily specifically.** Sicilian emigrants went mainly to the United States;
   Argentina and Brazil drew more from northern Italy (Ratti 1931). The transplanted feasts I found in Argentina,
   Uruguay and Brazil are Campanian, Vicentine or Calabrian, not Sicilian. The best-documented Sicilian–Latino
   contact is in the **United States** (St Joseph's tables in a "largely Latino parish" in East Los Angeles; New
   Orleans altars adopted by Black Catholics).
5. **The candles and "gaudy" look are resemblance**, explained (where scholars explain it at all) by the shared
   post-Trent baroque Catholicism of the Spanish world and by the pan-Catholic votive tradition (ex-votos), not by
   any Sicily-to-Latin-America transfer. I found no scholar who attributes Sicilian and Latin American candle use to
   one specific shared origin.

## Disagreements

1. **When the Spanish Inquisition began in Sicily.** Lea (1908): first inquisitor appointed and first auto de fe in
   1487. Teramo project: first attempt 1483, working about 1500, permanent from 1543. They are counting different
   things (appointment and first ceremony vs. a funded, housed tribunal vs. a settled one). Show "from the 1480s;
   fully established by the 1540s" or give both.
2. **Benedict's canonization day.** Dell'Aira (2009) gives 24 May 1807 in the text and 25 May 1807 in the abstract.
   Use "1807" until a second source (e.g. the Vatican's own record) settles it.
3. **Antonio of Noto's death year.** Fiume (2009): 15 March 1549, "probably 1550". Dell'Aira (2009): 1550. Show
   1549/1550.
4. **Píccolo's birth year.** Popular article citing Ponce (2012): 25 March 1654, Palermo. Newberry Library name
   heading: 1658–1729. Birthplace Palermo is from the popular article only. Needs a scholarly biography.
5. **Sugar skulls: Aztec or European?** Brandes (1998, via JSTOR Daily) and Lomnitz/Nutini (via Marchi 2018): the
   skull imagery fits European baroque and later caricature better than Aztec mortuary ritual; Lomnitz calls the
   Aztec comparison modern. Hilaire Kallendorf (Texas A&M Hispanic Studies, quoted in a 2021 TAMU College of Liberal
   Arts blog post, https://liberalarts.tamu.edu/blog/sugar-skulls-are-not-halloween-candy/): sugar skulls "find
   their roots" in the Aztec tzompantli (skull racks), with the Spanish bringing the sugar-paste technique
   *alfeñique*. All agree the sugar technique came with the Spanish. The disagreement is over the skull motif.
6. **Pan de muerto's age.** Lomnitz (2005, p. 430, as read by Laudan): this bread appears between 1930 and 1960.
   Brandes (2006 excerpts): bread is the "earliest and most widely reported offering". These may not conflict (bread
   offerings in general vs. today's specific bread), but neither has been checked at the page.

Del Giudice raises the Spanish link as a question, not a finding. Where Sicilian tables meet Latino Catholics, the record is in the US (East LA parish). |
| "St Joseph's table began with a medieval Sicilian famine broken by fava beans." | **Legend, reported as legend.** | Told by practitioners (Green 2016, informants dating it to "the 1500s" or "the Middle Ages"). Del Giudice: "It is not exactly clear when or how the table tradition began in Sicily." Earliest written account in hand: Pitrè 1900. |
| "Mardi Gras Indians took St Joseph's Night from the Sicilians." | **Unsupported (speculation).** | The World (2016) labels it speculation; Gumbo Ya-Ya (1945, via Green) records Black New Orleanians making and visiting altars, which is a documented overlap but not proof of borrowing. |
| "Sicilian immigrants brought their saints to Argentina, Brazil and Venezuela." | **Unsupported for Sicily specifically, in what I found; supported for other southern Italian regions.** | Ratti (1931): Sicilian emigration went chiefly to North America. Transplanted feasts found: San Cono (Campania) in Argentina/Uruguay (Ragone 2019), Achiropita in São Paulo (Schmidt 2016), Vicentine and Pompeian devotions in Buenos Aires (Paravati 2018). The Sicilian saints that did reach Latin America (Benedict, Antonio, Rosalia) went **earlier, through Spain and Portugal**, not with 19th-c. migrants. |

## What this means for the piece (for the editor, not for print)

- A **main section toward the end** is justified, built on two documented stories plus one honest "resemblance" note:
  (a) the Sicilian saints who emigrated before the Sicilians did (Benedict the Moor and Antonio of Noto to Brazil and
  Spanish America via Black Rosary brotherhoods; Rosalia to New Spain via the Jesuits); (b) the shared Spanish
  machinery (one Inquisition; one Joseph cult; the same mission playbook, "the Indies down here"); and (c) why the
  candles and ex-votos look alike: shared baroque grandparents, not a direct line.
- The Day of the Dead belongs here as "cousins, not parent and child".
