## Short summary

1. **Rosalia (Palermo).** Her bones were found in a cave on Monte Pellegrino on **15 July 1624**. She was made the
   city's patron on **27 July 1624**. The archbishop, Cardinal Giannettino Doria, had them recognised as hers in
   **February 1625**, and they were sealed in a silver urn in **1631**. Mariano Smiriglio designed the urn and four
   named Palermo silversmiths made it. The urn was opened on 16 Feb 2024, and inside lay the 1631 cotton wadding
   and a signed copy of the Jesuit Giordano Cascini's booklet. One odd "contact" relic, her prayer-bead counter
   (*contapreghiere*), was split up after 1624 (12 beads to Dominican nuns, the cross to another founder) and put
   back together in one silver reliquary in 2021.
2. **Agatha (Catania).** The Byzantine general George Maniakes took her body to Constantinople in 1039 or 1040
   (the sources differ). Two soldiers, Gisliberto and Goselmo, stole it back in 1126, and it reached Catania on
   **17 August 1126**, 86 years later. Most of the relics are in the silver-gilt bust that Giovanni di Bartolo of
   Siena made in **1376** (date from its inscription; Venturi read 1371). Two femur reliquaries sit in a big
   reliquary chest. Bits of her are also in Palermo (an arm, plus a shared Agatha-and-Lawrence reliquary in the
   Cappella Palatina) and in Apulia (a breast, first at Gallipoli and moved to Galatina in 1389). A 1651 Sicilian
   historian wrote that one breast is shown in Catania and "the other" at Galatina.
3. **Lucy (Syracuse).** Her body went from Syracuse to Constantinople (1039 or 1040, Maniakes again), then to
   **Venice** after 1204 (a Venetian chronicle gives 1206 as the official translation), and has lain at San
   Geremia since 1860. A separate medieval tradition, written down by Sigebert of Gembloux (d. 1112), says the
   Duke of Spoleto had taken it to Corfinium, and that from there it went to **Metz** in the 970s. So at least two
   churches claim the whole body, and Syracuse itself has no permanent body. Since 2004 it gets a visit about
   every ten years (2004, 2014, 2024). In 2024 the Syracuse city council asked for the body back for good, and the
   archdiocese declined politely.
4. **Other Sicilian relics.** Ninfa's head reached Palermo from Rome in 1593. Part of Cristina's relics came from
   Rome in 1154–1166. The **entrails of King Louis IX of France** (d. 1270) were laid in Monreale Cathedral on
   Charles of Anjou's orders. Whether his **heart** is there too is contested: Geoffroy de Beaulieu says yes,
   Jacques Le Goff thinks Saint-Denis is likelier. Messina venerates a lock of the Virgin's hair ("sacri
   capelli") kept with the Sacred Letter.
5. **Rules and critics.** The 1983 Code of Canon Law, can. 1190 §1: "It is absolutely forbidden to sell sacred
   relics." The Vatican's 2017 Instruction splits relics into **significant (*insigni*) and non-significant (*non
   insigni*)** only. The familiar "first / second / third class" labels **do not appear in it**. It bans trade and
   sale (Art. 25) and bars dismembering a body without Rome's consent (Art. 24). Calvin's *Treatise on Relics*
   (Geneva, Jean Girard, 1543) is the classic attack: "a good shipload" of True Cross pieces, and every saint
   with "two or three" bodies.
6. **Counts.** No count was found of reliquaries or relic-holding churches in any Sicilian diocese. The nearest
   are: at least three named reliquaries for Agatha in Catania (the bust and two femur reliquaries), with "vari
   reliquiari" in all and no total given; and "molte reliquie" in Palermo Cathedral's Chapel of Relics, again
   with no number.

## Disagreements

1. **1039 or 1040 for Maniakes taking Agatha and Lucy (rows 13, 29).** LiveSicilia, LiveUniversity, Balarm and
   BlogSicilia give 1040. Avvenire and New Liturgical Movement give 1039. Maniakes campaigned in Sicily from 1038
   to 1040, so the date depends on whether a source counts the capture of Syracuse (1039/40) or the shipment.
   No primary chronicle was opened. Show it as "1039–1040". **Who received the relics** differs too: Emperor
   Michael IV (LiveSicilia) or Empress Theodora (Avvenire). Both are popular retellings with no source given.
2. **1204 or 1206 for Lucy reaching Venice (row 30).** 1204 is the sack of Constantinople. 1206 is the official
   *traslatio* date the Venice sanctuary uses, as BlogSicilia reports. The difference is the city's capture versus
   the ceremonial arrival.
3. **1860 or 1861–63 for the move to San Geremia (row 32).** The body was moved on 11 July 1860 (BlogSicilia, NLM).
   The old church was pulled down in 1861–63 (Comune di Venezia). Both can be true: move first, demolition
   later.
4. **1376 or 1371 for Agatha's bust (row 18).** The inscription read by Mauceri and Riccioni gives 1376. Venturi
   cited 1371 (Di Natale p. 143), which matches the tradition that Bishop Marziale ordered it around 1371. The
   commission (c. 1371) and the finished work (1376–77) are different events.
5. **9 June or 9 July 1625 for the first Rosalia procession (row 6).** Balarm and BlogSicilia say 9 June.
   Fagiolo & Colonnese cite a 1625 printed account dated "9 di luglio". These may be two events: a penitential
   plague procession brought forward to June, and the festive July "triumph". This needs Paruta's *Relatione*
   (1651) or Petrarca (1988).
6. **Louis IX's heart: Monreale or Saint-Denis (row 43).** Geoffroy de Beaulieu (as cited by Costa) and the
   Sicilian sources say the heart is at Monreale with the entrails. Le Goff (as cited by Costa) thinks it was
   buried with the bones at Saint-Denis. What the sources agree on is the entrails at Monreale.
7. **Lucy's body: Venice or Metz (rows 30, 36).** These are two rival medieval traditions. The Venice line goes
   Syracuse, then Constantinople (Maniakes), then Venice. The Metz line, after Sigebert of Gembloux, goes Syracuse,
   then Corfinium (Faroald of Spoleto), then Metz (970s). Neither has been tested scientifically in any source
   found here.
8. **Spellings of the 1126 soldiers' names (row 14).** At least four spellings appear: Gisliberto, Gisilberto,
   Goselmo, Gonselmo, Goselino. Use the diocesan form (Gisliberto and Goselmo, or Gonselmo) and note the others.
9. **Where the Agatha breast relic is (rows 23, 25).** Inveges (1651, as quoted) puts "the other" breast at
   *San Pietro* di Galatina. Today it is in the museum of *Santa Caterina d'Alessandria*, Galatina. This may be a
   naming difference within Galatina or a change of place; it is not settled.
