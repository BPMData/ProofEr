## Short summary

1. **Terms.** Italian scholarship does not talk about "pagan survival". It talks about *religione popolare*
   (popular religion). Its main line runs through Gramsci's notes on folklore as an unofficial "conception of the
   world and of life", then de Martino (magic as a cultural answer to the "crisis of presence", not a leftover),
   then the 1960s–70s *demologi* (folk-culture scholars): Annabella Rossi, Lombardi Satriani, Di Nola, Clara
   Gallini, and in Sicily Elsa Guggino. The historians Gabriele De Rosa and Carlo Ginzburg run alongside them.
2. **The "survivals" idea.** It comes from Tylor's evolutionism, and it is openly rejected. Jean-Claude Schmitt
   (1977): "there is nothing survived in a culture; everything is lived or does not exist at all." Giulio Angioni
   (1972) mocked the "sterile search for the cultural fossil". De Martino called religious folklore read as
   relics a "no-man's-land". Pitrè himself, the founder of Sicilian folklore studies, was shaped by Tylor. He
   sometimes asserted pagan origins without evidence: he called the St Joseph's eve bonfires "certainly pagan"
   and named no reason.
3. **"Syncretism".** The word's own history is a small story. Plutarch used it once, for Cretans uniting against a
   common enemy. Erasmus revived it in 1519. It turned into an insult in 17th-century Protestant quarrels, and only
   in the 19th century came to mean a "mixture" of religions.
4. **Timeline.** Sicily's rulers are dated below from Italian sources: Spatafora (Regione Siciliana), Maurici
   (Palermo), Indelli (Salerno), Di Franco (Messina), the 1816 *Legge fondamentale* text, and others. Britannica
   fills the gaps. Two transitions are still unsourced (see Gaps).
5. **Earliest records (A.3).** These are only partly done: the *donne di fuora* (mid-15th-century confessors'
   manual), the Rosalia relic and procession (1624–25), Pitrè's 1899 statistics on patron saints and his account
   of St Joseph's feasts, and the date temples were turned into churches (late 6th century).

## Disagreements

- **Start of the Inquisition in Sicily.** Geremia (2024) gives 24 March 1486 (Innocent VIII) in note 3 but 1516–1782
  in her abstract. The two may count different things: papal setting-up vs the tribunal actually working, or
  the Spanish *Suprema* (central council) taking control. Not settled here. Group D should get it from Henningsen
  or from Italian archive scholarship (for example on the Steri, the Inquisition's palace in Palermo).
- **Pitrè's *Biblioteca*.** DBI: 1871–1913, but vols 1–2 dated 1870. Both sources say 25 volumes.
- **Pitrè's *Archivio*.** DBI: 1882–1909. Bellantonio: founded 1882. English Wikipedia (a lead, not citable):
  1880–1906. Go with the two scholarly sources.
- **Pitrè's patron-saint sample.** In the same pages he writes "In 150 paesi" ("in 150 towns") and "i 160 da me
  studiati" ("the 160 I studied"). The section heading says 160. Geremia's figure of 172 municipalities
  (1870–1890) is a different sample: his fieldwork overall, not the patron-saint count.
- **When the Norman kingdom began.** One EU heritage page (memolaproject.eu, not cited) wrongly says 1091 made the
  island "an independent kingdom". The kingdom dates from 1130 (Britannica). 1091 is the end of the conquest.

## Myths and claims about "pagan survival"

| Claim | Where it starts | Label | Who argues each side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sicilian popular Catholicism is an "unbroken survival" of pagan cults | The 19th-century "survivals" model of Tylor and his followers, which Pitrè drew on (DBI). Travellers' charges of paganism (Renouard, quoted by Pitrè p. XXIX) | **Contested, mostly rejected in current scholarship** | Against: de Martino (relics as a "terra di nessuno"; magic as a present-day answer to crisis), Schmitt 1977, Angioni 1972, Gallini 1973 (evil eye as social levelling), Boesch Gajano 2020 (a consecrated host put in a beehive read as "alternative" use of a sacred object, not magic). For a long continuity of *some* beliefs: Geremia 2024 on the *donna de fora* (a "long-term continuity of this belief") |
| The St Joseph's eve bonfires are "certainly pagan" | Pitrè, *Feste patronali* (1899/1900), p. XXXIII | **Unsupported as stated** (no evidence or named cult given) | Pitrè asserts it. No source opened argues it with evidence |
| Christian Sicily literally took over pagan sacred places | Physical reuse of temples (Concord at Agrigento, Athena at Syracuse) | **Supported for buildings** (late 6th century: Spatafora; Maurici on Bishop Gregory of Agrigento). Continuity of the *cult* does not follow | Spatafora; Maurici |
| The *donne di fuora* were an archaic fairy cult | Henningsen 1990; Ginzburg, *Storia notturna* (1989) | **Contested** | Henningsen: "nothing more than collective dreaming" with an archaic pattern (per Geremia). Ginzburg links it to the Herodias / nocturnal-goddess complex. Geremia argues the inquisitors' muddled records misled earlier scholars |
| "Syncretism" is a neutral word | Its use in the history of religions | **Contested** | Levinskaya 1993: an insult in the 17th century. Meynell's definition ("usually uncritically") shows it is still judgmental. The Stewart & Shaw (1994) critique was not opened |

## Belief, reported as belief

- In de Martino's account, people who use magic in the South hold that the rite protects them in moments of
  crisis. He describes the "realistic" and "magical" sides of a technique as "non entrano in contraddizione
  soggettiva" ("not in conflict for the person using them") (*Sud e magia*, quoted by Dei 2018, p. 56).
- Rossi records the poor of the South holding that the saints are the "unica forza in grado di risolvere i
  problemi dell'al di qua" ("the only force able to solve the problems of this world") (Russo, p. 293, quoting
  Rossi 1969, p. 5).
