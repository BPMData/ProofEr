## Summary

There **is** real substance for a main section, but it isn't the one the "candles and gaudy stuff" hunch points to.
The documented link runs **from Sicily outward, through the Spanish and Portuguese empires**, not through later
Italian migration. Three things carry it:

1. **The same institutions.** Sicily was ruled from Spain, and its Inquisition was a tribunal of the Spanish Inquisition,
   set up about 1487, more than eighty years before the tribunals of Lima (1570) and Mexico (1571). H. C. Lea's 1908
   study covers Sicily, Mexico, Peru and New Granada in one book as "Spanish dependencies". The same noblemen governed on
   both sides: the Duke of Alburquerque was viceroy of New Spain (1653–60) and later viceroy of Sicily (1667–70).
2. **Sicilian saints went to the Americas and stayed there.** The strongest case is **Saint Benedict the Moor**
   (*San Benedetto il Moro*; Spanish *San Benito de Palermo*; Portuguese *São Benedito*). He was a Black Franciscan
   friar born in San Fratello, Sicily, to formerly enslaved African parents, and he died in Palermo in 1589. Franciscans
   and Iberian Black confraternities (*irmandades*, *cofradías*) carried his cult to Lisbon by 1609, to Brazil within
   decades, and across Spanish America, where it is still alive today. Examples are the drum-and-dance *chimbangueles*
   of Zulia, Venezuela, and São Benedito brotherhoods across Brazil. Historian Giovanna Fiume (University of Palermo)
   calls him the champion of enslaved Africans' religion "throughout the Spanish Empire". In Sicily itself his cult now
   survives only in San Fratello and at his Palermo convent. His fellow Sicilian, the Black Franciscan **Antonio da
   Noto**, travelled the same route and is venerated in Brazil as "Santo Antônio de Categeró". **Saint Rosalia** of
   Palermo also crossed over: an archbishop of Palermo brought her cult to Seville, New Spanish painters (Juan Tinoco,
   Juan Correa) painted her, a Palermo-born Jesuit missionary served at the Baja California mission of Santa Rosalía, and
   an image of her came back to the Canary Islands "from the Kingdoms of the Indies" in 1755.
3. **The Atlantic slave trade is part of the story.** Benedict's parents were enslaved Africans in Sicily. His
   cult was promoted as a model for enslaved and free Black Catholics, and it grew strongest among Afro-descendant
   communities in Brazil, Venezuela and Angola. This is documented, and it is heavier than the essay's light tone. It
   needs respectful handling.

What this pass did **not** establish: whether the look-alike practices (candles, ex-votos, home altars for the dead,
the Saint Joseph table, Day of the Dead sweets) share an origin, developed in parallel, or travelled with migrants. I
ran out of budget before researching them. So for now: the documented links are main-section material, and the
"they both love candles" resemblance is still unverified.

## Disagreements

- **Benedict's birth year: 1524 or 1526.** 1524 is given by Zarattini, Rincón & Carrasquero, Reginaldo (p. 145) and the 2024
  quincentenary. 1526 is given by Reginaldo (p. 136), Oliveira and Casanova. Probable cause: the sources come from
  different hagiographic traditions. None of the sources I opened explains the gap.
- **Beatification: 1743 or 1763.** 1743 (11 May) is given by Vincent, Zarattini and Stella. 1763 is given by Reginaldo,
  and nobody else. Vincent ties Spain's wave of Benedict hagiographies (1744 onward) to the 1743 date.
- **Born free or freed at 18?** Zarattini says born free; Oliveira says freed at 18. Both say his parents had been enslaved.
- **When the cult reached Venezuela.** Casanova (citing Salazar Mays 2001) says the end of the colonial period. Rincón &
  Carrasquero say the early 16th century, which comes before the saint was born, so it can't be literal for his cult.
- **Brazil's first São Benedito fraternity.** Zarattini dates the Rio altar to 1612 and a Salvador fraternity to c. 1630,
  but his other article describes the Salvador foundation as a general Franciscan fraternity. Reginaldo gives Lisbon 1609
  as the first brotherhood anywhere and names Brazilian convent brotherhoods without dates.
- **What survived the 2023 Palermo fire.** The Cabimas diocese says only one femur. BlogSicilia says the friars
  reassembled the bones for public veneration.
- **Píccolo's birth year.** CULCO BCS says 1654; the Newberry authority heading says 1658.

## Belief, reported as belief

- In Zulia, devotees hold that San Benito answers prayers. The Diocese of Cabimas records the oral tradition that he
  stopped the Barroso II oil blowout in 1922 (pastoral letter 2024, §13). Casanova (Universidad del Zulia) studies the
  street devotion of the *sambeniteros* as "lo sagrado en lo cotidiano" ("the sacred in the everyday").
- Fiume (2009, p. 84) and Reginaldo (2016, p. 138) describe Benedict as a saint whom Black Catholics, enslaved and free,
  took as their own. Reginaldo says that devotion "broke free of the friars". Seventeenth-century Angolans claimed him as
  born in Angola.
