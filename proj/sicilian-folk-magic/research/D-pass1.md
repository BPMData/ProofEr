# Group D, Pass 1: the Inquisition, healers, the evil eye and the donne di fuora

Lens: English-language scholarship first (university presses, peer-reviewed journals), with Spanish/Italian
scholarship used where the English record leads to it. Researched Nov 23, 2026 by a research worker
(Claude). Independent of every other report in this folder.

**Working conditions that limit this report (read first).** Partway through, the shared web-search budget
(200 searches per turn, shared by all workers) and then the shared page-fetch budget ran out. Questions D1 and D2
(Inquisition and *donne di fuora*) were covered before that from sources I opened. **Questions D3 (charms, healers,

Spelling. The sources spell the name several ways: *donne di fuora* (Sicilian-Italian), *donne de fora*
(Geremia), *donas de fuera* (the Spanish the inquisitors wrote), *donni di fuora*. All mean "ladies from outside".

## Short summary

- **Dates.** The sources give different founding dates for the Spanish Inquisition in Sicily: a papal act of
  24 March 1486, a working court only from 1500, and "1516–1782" for the Spanish period proper (Geremia 2024).
  It was suppressed by Ferdinand IV's decree of 16 March 1782, at the viceroy Domenico Caracciolo's request. The
  sources disagree on when its archive was burned: 1782 (Henningsen) or by Caracciolo's order of 27 June 1783
  (Geremia). Either way, almost all the original trial files are lost. What survives are the summaries the
  Palermo tribunal sent to Madrid, the *relaciones de causas* ("reports of cases"), now in the Archivo Histórico
  Nacional, plus a few church-court files in Sicilian diocesan archives.
- **How many cases.** 3,188 trials in the surviving *relaciones* for 1540–1700 (Contreras & Henningsen 1986).
  Henningsen's 1993 recount found 4,057 and estimated about 6,300 once missing years are allowed for. Executions in
  person were few after 1540: 25 in the *relaciones*, or 52 by William Monter's fuller count. There were many more
  before 1540 (207 in person and 257 in effigy, per Del Col).
- **Magic.** A count of 456 magic cases for 1547–1701, none of them executed, is attributed to Henningsen 1990.
  I saw it only on a blog and have not checked it against his text.
- **Donne di fuora.** The counts differ: 65 people, 8 of them men, 1579–1651 (attributed to Henningsen via a 1999
  handbook); 70 trials (Henningsen 1990, as a blog cites it); 193 "ladies from outside" (Geremia's 2024 reading
  of Henningsen); 77 (Messana 2007). The defendants were mostly poor women who healed and told fortunes. They said
  their spirits left their bodies at night and travelled in "companies" with fairy women, who blessed tidy houses and
  could cause illness that only a *donna di fuora* could cure. Inquisitors called it witchcraft. Sentences were
  reclusion, prison, exile, and sometimes torture. The documented executions were for heresy, not for this.
- **How scholars read them.** Henningsen calls it a fairy cult and a "white" sabbath: a shared set of cultural
  dreams, not shamanism, because no trance technique shows in the record. Ginzburg links it to a much older
  European night-goddess (Diana/Herodias) complex. Messana and Geremia read the records as layered: the inquisitors'
  demonology, the women's own dream-and-healing world, and the neighbours' accounts. Geremia adds the first full
  surviving trials, from the Monreale diocesan archive.
- **Belief, in their own words.** In 1985 Henningsen interviewed a living Sicilian healer ("Marzia"). She said
  "it is the spirit that flies" while the body stays at home, and her family did not move her body while she was
  "out", so her spirit could find its way back. That is a modern first-hand statement of the belief, reported as
  belief.

## Disagreements

1. **When it started.** 24 March 1486 (papal act), 1500 (court working) and 1516 (start of the "Spanish
   Inquisition" period of 1516–1782) are all in Geremia 2024 (pp. 1–2, 11). They measure different things: papal
   authorisation under Ferdinand of Aragon, the first working tribunal, and the Habsburg period. Henry Charles Lea's
   *The Inquisition in the Spanish Dependencies* (1908) is the classic English account (Gutenberg refused me), and
   Monter 1990 is the standard modern one. Both should be checked, and the piece should give a range or name the
   event it means.
2. **When the archive burned.** 1782 (Henningsen 2020, p. 1064, citing Henningsen 1986, p. 58) against
   Caracciolo's order of 27 June 1783 (Geremia 2024, n. 1). The suppression was 1782 and the burning followed, so
   1783 may be the order's date and 1782 a shorthand. Needs a third source (Renda 1997, Messana 2007, Lea 1908).
3. **How many *donne di fuora*.** 65 (8 men), 1579–1651 (Gijswijt-Hofstra et al. 1999, p. 140, via Wikipedia);
   70 trials (Henningsen 1990, pp. 192–3, via blog); 193 (Geremia's reading of Henningsen); 77 (Messana 2007). The
   likely reasons are different units (defendants, trials, or everyone named), different periods, and different
   archives: Henningsen used the *relaciones* in Madrid, and Messana added other material. **Show it as a range
   with a note, never one number,** until Henningsen 1990 pp. 191–194 is opened.
4. **Executions 1540–1700.** 25 (the *relaciones*) against 52 (Monter's confirmed count). Different
   documentation; neither number is for magic.
5. **Total trials.** 3,188 (1986), 4,057 reported (1993), about 6,300 estimated. Each is a different count of
   surviving versus estimated reports. The estimate must be labelled as one.
6. **Pitrè page.** "Something of a fairy and something of a witch": vol. 4, p. 163 (Geremia) or p. 153 (blog).
   Open Pitrè to settle it.
7. **Henningsen 1990 page range.** pp. 191–215 (most citations) against pp. 191–225 (Henningsen 2020, p. 1066
   text). The bibliography says 215.
