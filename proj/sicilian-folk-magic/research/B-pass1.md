## Short summary

- **Saint Rosalia (Palermo).** Her cult as Palermo's plague saint dates firmly to **1624–25**. The plague arrived
  7 May 1624; her bones were found on Monte Pellegrino on **15 July 1624**; a procession of the relics was held on
  9 June 1625; the city declared the plague over on 15 July 1625. Cascini's *Vita* came out in 1627, and she entered
  the Roman Martyrology in about 1630. Her two feast days are **15 July** (the finding, which is the *festino*) and
  **4 September** (her death day). 2024 was marked as the 400th year of the feast. Fragments of the relics were sent
  to the Pope, to other Sicilian cities and to European courts, but no source I opened gives a count.
- **Saint Agatha (Catania).** The best primary source opened is Bishop Maurice of Catania's letter on the
  translation. It says her body was carried to Constantinople by the Byzantine general Maniakes and brought back to
  Catania in **1126**, arriving on 16 kalends of September (**17 August**), by two men named Gislebertus and
  Goselinus. The modern feast runs **3–5 February**, has **11 *candelore*** (giant carried candle-towers; a 2007 count),
  and the devotees wear the white *sacco*. The claim that the feast inherits the cult of **Isis** comes from Emanuele
  Ciaceri (1905). It was re-examined in 2022–2025: Soraci (2025) finds "no historical foundation" for direct
  continuity. **Contested, leaning unsupported.**
- **Saint Joseph's Tables (*tavulata / cena di San Giuseppe*).** The best English study (Del Giudice 2010) says
  plainly that "it is not exactly clear when or how the table tradition began in Sicily". The earliest account she
  cites is Pitrè's (1900). She ties the spread to Spanish rule but notes there is no equivalent in Spain. The
  well-known **famine-origin story** appears "almost exclusively in diaspora" sources. Pre-Christian spring-rite or
  Demeter readings (Giallombardo) are reported, but treated cautiously.
- **The *decollati* (beheaded souls) of Palermo.** The church of the "Anime dei Corpi Decollati" by the Oreto bridge
  was rebuilt in **1785** and became the cemetery of executed people (Fatta 2016). A head-pyramid with an inscription
  dated 1604 is recorded there. The Bianchi confraternity, which comforted the condemned (1541–1820), assisted 2,127
  of them (Di Bella, cited by Harper). Devotees hold that these souls, in Purgatory, can protect and grant favors, and
  they leave ex-votos marked "V.F.G.A." (*vuto fattu, grazia avuta*, "vow made, grace received").
- **Madonna della Lettera (Messina).** The supposed letter from Mary to Messina is dated 42 CE, on 3 June. Scholarship
  (Franceschini 2019) dates the legend to the **first half of the 16th century**: the first Latin text is in
  Maurolico (1556), and the Holy Office condemned the letter as apocryphal in **1598**. A Jesuit defense of it (1629)
  was censored by the Index. The cult carried on regardless.
- **Counts.** The Regione Siciliana's intangible-heritage register (REI) was set up on **26 July 2005** and held
  **177** items in 2013. My tool counted **about 86** of them in the feasts book (*Libro delle Celebrazioni*); that
  count is approximate. It was replaced by the **REIS** on **5 March 2014**, which has six books. **I found no
  sourced count of patron saints or patronal feasts across Sicily's municipalities.**

## Disagreements

1. **Rosalia: the year she entered the Roman Martyrology.** Bizzarro (2020, fn. 3) gives **1630**. Scalisi (2023,
   p. 196) ties it to the year Antonio Moncada died, which her notes suggest was 1631. Why they may differ: the
   decree and the printed edition can fall in different years. Check a primary source (the Martyrology edition, or
   the *Acta Sanctorum* for 4 September).
2. **Rosalia: her September date.** Most sources give **4 September**: the Vatican City State page gives it as her
   death day in the Martyrology, and the CNA piece places the jubilee's end on 4 September. Bizzarro's footnote 3
   gives **5 September** as the day of death. This is probably a mix-up between the vigil-night pilgrimage (3–4 Sept)
   and the feast itself.
3. **Rosalia: when the plague began.** **7 May 1624** (arrival by ship from Tunis: Vatican page; "early May":
   Bizzarro) versus **early August 1624** (Scalisi: officially "confirmed" when the viceroy died). The first is when
   it arrived, the second when it was officially recognized.
4. **Agatha: when Maniakes removed the relics.** Popular accounts say **1040**. Maurice's 12th-century letter names
   Maniakes but gives no year. Aaron (2015), following Oldfield, says there is no evidence the relics were in
   Catania, or necessarily absent, by 1040, and the removal may have been earlier. **Give it as "11th century, under
   Maniakes", not as 1040.**
5. **The *decollati* church.** Fatta (2016, an archaeology bulletin of the regional heritage office, citing
   Villabianca): the church was **rebuilt in 1785** and became the cemetery of the executed. Harper (a magazine
   essay): the executed were interred there **from 1799**. These may be different events (the rebuilding versus the
   start of burials). Fatta is the stronger source; get a second scholarly source (Di Bella, or Pitrè's *La vita in
   Palermo cento e più anni fa*, which Gutenberg holds but which was refused here).
6. **Madonna della Lettera: when Lascaris translated the letter.** The Stern Center gives **1467** (when Lascaris
   reached Messina). Franceschini gives only "the fifteenth century" and says that is the *supporters'* claim. She
   dates the legend itself to the first half of the 16th century, with the first text in 1556. Present 1467 as part
   of the legend, not as a documented event.
7. **Where the St Joseph famine story comes from.** Canning & Zinni (2018, p. 28) tell it as "the story" of a
   late-13th-century drought in Sicily. Del Giudice (2010, pp. 17–18) says the story appears "almost exclusively in
   diaspora written and oral sources", with "little trace... in homeland Italian literature". She reads it as a
   migration narrative. Del Giudice is the more critical, better-sourced study.
8. **Who sits at the St Joseph table.** In Sicilian practice as recorded, it is three of the poorest people, or poor
   orphans, as *santi* or *virgineddi* (Del Giudice; Valentin). In the western New York diaspora, it is children from
   the family or community (Canning & Zinni). Del Giudice says the role is now largely "honorary" even in Italy. This
   is a change over time and place, not a factual conflict.
