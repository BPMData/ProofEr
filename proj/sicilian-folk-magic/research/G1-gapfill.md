Worker: gap-fill pass G1 (Nov 23, 2026). Lens: primary and Sicilian scholarship first, then English.
Budget used: about 33 web searches and about 48 page fetches, then the session's shared fetch budget ran out
(proxy refusal "web-fetch budget for this session is exhausted"). Two further fetches were refused for that reason.

**A tool limit that matters:** the fetch tool only reads the first ~65,000 characters of an archive.org full-text
file and the first ~94,000 of a Project Gutenberg page. That reaches the *start* of a Pitrè volume and no further.
So Pitrè's *Usi e costumi* vol. IV chapter on the *decollati* (pp. 1–25) **was** read, but Pitrè's evil-eye section in

## Summary

1. **Decollati (Q4): now strong, from Pitrè himself.** Pitrè, *Usi e costumi* vol. IV (1889), ch. I "Le Anime dei
   corpi decollati", opened (OCR). Devotees in Palermo call them *armi di li corpi decullati* (souls of the beheaded
   bodies); in Acireale *beati*, in Trapani *armiceddi*. The Compagnia de' Bianchi (the confraternity that comforted the
   condemned) arose in Palermo in 1541. The church by the Oreto river was called "Madonna del Fiume" before 1785 and was
   rebuilt 1857–1865. Devotees go on **Monday and Friday, "days sacred to them"**, often barefoot, say a rosary to the
   souls, and ask for health, protection from robbers, help in love, the return of someone gone, and even **three
   lottery numbers**. They listen for an omen afterwards: a cock crowing or a dog barking is a good sign. Pitrè counted
   **"about a hundred" painted votive tablets** on the church walls in 1889, showing the souls' miracles. Pitrè's own
   reading: the cult grows from the idea that the repentant executed are **martyrs**. A second, independent scholarly
   source (Davies & Matteoni 2017, open access, citing Hartland 1910 and Di Bella 1994) agrees on the night-walking souls
   "in white garments near the River Oreto".
2. **Worm-charmers (Q2): strong.** Napoli 2008 (*Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*, open access) interviewed
   about 25 *ciarmavermi* in five Sicilian provinces, 2002–2006. Charms walk through **Holy Week** day by day, "cutting"
   the worms; one from Belpasso opens *"U Luni santu, l'addevu è spantu"* ("Holy Monday, the newborn child is
   frightened"). Rules healers state: the charm passes between men and women, a disciple may use it only **after the
   master dies**, it may be learned over **seven Fridays** or recited at **Christmas midnight Mass**, and healers take
   **no money, only gifts**, or they lose the power. Saints Agatha and Laura and the Madonna appear in the charms.
3. **Evil eye (Q1): partial.** Sicilian-sourced: Migliore's *Mal'uocchiu* (Toronto, 1997), on a Sicilian-Canadian
   community from **Racalmuto**, puts the evil eye inside the local ideas of *disgrazia* (misfortune) and *sortilegio*
   (spell-power), with *ittatura* (evil-casting) between them; the caster can be a witch or an ordinary person whose envy
   is strong enough (Antze's review, 1999). Napoli 2008 records a "little plate for the evil eye" (*u piattinu*) used by
   worm-charmers. Amulets: coral, silver bells, the horned-hand gesture (Intorre 2025, from early-20th-c. English
   collectors). **Not sourced from an opened Sicilian text in this pass: the oil-drops-in-water test and the
   Christmas Eve rule for learning the evil-eye prayer.** The only opened source for the Christmas Eve rule is from
   **Abruzzo and Molise** (Giancristofaro 2022), not Sicily. Keep these as the main open gap.
4. **Surveys (Q3): no Sicilian or southern Italian survey exists in anything I could find.** The nearest is national:
   Pew Research Center's 2017 Western Europe survey asked about the evil eye (median 16% across 15 countries; Portugal
   48%, Spain 33%, Denmark and Sweden 9%). Italy's own figure is in a chart image I could not read. Gershman 2022
   (PLOS ONE) pools the Pew waves: 95 countries, 140,000+ people, no regional breakdown. Napoli's 25 healers are a sample
   of practitioners, not a measure of belief. **Say plainly in the piece: no count of belief for Sicily is on record.**
5. **Festa dei Morti (Q5): thin.** One opened scholarly-ish source with oral testimony (Campanella 2023): children waited
   for the dead to bring them **sugar dolls** (*pupi di zùccaru*); the doll for girls, the knight for boys. No earliest
   record was opened. The famous **origin story of the frutta martorana** (nuns of the Martorana making marzipan fruit
   for a visiting archbishop) is **unsourced** everywhere I looked.
6. **Ex-votos (Q6): moderate.** Definitions and Sicilian examples from Ignazio E. Buttitta (Università di Palermo):
   painted tablets show the danger, the person saved and the holy helper; wax, metal or terracotta ones show the healed
   body part. Collections: the decollati church, about 100 tablets in 1889 (Pitrè); the Madonna della Milicia shrine at
   Altavilla Milicia, "about 400" painted tablets from 1837, museum opened 1983 (a local website only: needs a second
   source, the lead is Antonino Buttitta's 1983 catalogue); the Casa-Museo Antonino Uccello at Palazzolo Acreide (state
   ICCD records: a painted-tin ex-voto for a miracle dated 23 Nov 1890; a silver baby-shaped one). Dialect name in the
   ICCD records: *miraculu* ("miracle").

## Disagreements

1. **The decollati church: which date, which bridge.**
   - Pitrè 1889, p. 10: called "Madonna del Fiume" **before 1785**; **rebuilt 1857–1865** on the Oreto bank; named
     "del Ponte" from a bridge Pitrè dates **1113**.
   - Fatta 2016 (B-pass1): rebuilt in **1785**, then the cemetery of the executed.
   - Harper (B-pass1, magazine): the executed buried there **from 1799**.
   - Likely reading: 1785 is when the church took on its role and name for the beheaded souls, 1799 when burials began
     (unconfirmed), 1857–65 a later rebuilding. Print "rebuilt in 1785 and again in 1857–65" only with both sources
     named.
