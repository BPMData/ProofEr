Where G3 opened a better source, its verdict is used. Nothing here comes from memory.

**How the status was decided (strict).**
- "Independent" means not taken from the same original count or document. A scholar repeating another scholar's
  count is a carrier, not a second source. So many exact counts below are `single source` even though several
  opened articles repeat them. Often a rounder claim built from two independent counts is `checked` where the exact
  digit is not. Where that happens, the `checked` row is the one to print.
- *Ereticopedia* (La Motta; Karlikowski) is a signed scholarly dictionary on a wiki platform. It counts as a source
  only for La Motta's own statements. For figures it tabulates from other works (Renda, Contreras & Henningsen,
  Monter), the source is the work itself, and that work was not opened.
- Popular pages (CULCO BCS, *All About History*, BlogSicilia, *Le Ali di Ermes*, Balarm, VisitSicily, the TAMU
  blog, JSTOR Daily summaries, Wikipedia, WordPress blogs) never count toward the two.
- A quote's wording is verified when a worker opened the text that contains it. As a fact claim it is still one
  source, so quotes from a scholar are marked `single source` with "quote verified" in the notes.
- Translations into English made by the workers are **DRAFT**.

Out of scope here and left to the other cross-checks, although they are in G1: the *decollati* and the souls in
Purgatory, the Festa dei Morti, and ex-votos in Sicily.

## Belief statements

Each one is what practitioners or devotees hold, from the source named. The piece reports it as belief
("healers say", "devotees hold"), never as fact.

- **The spirit goes out at night.** *Donne di fuora* said their spirits left their bodies at night and travelled in
  "companies" with fairy women (Henningsen 2020, pp. 1063–1065; Geremia 2024). The companies went out on Tuesdays,
  Thursdays and Saturdays, never Fridays or Sundays (Henningsen 2020, p. 1065).
- **The fairies bless a tidy house.** Clients were told to tidy the house so the fairies would bless it; the
  fairies came in "like a breath of air", played with children and ate and drank (Henningsen 2020, p. 1065).
- **The fairies cause illness, and only one of their company can cure it.** People went to a *donna di fuora* to
  cure "illnesses caused by these fairies" (Henningsen 2020, p. 1063).
- **What the fairies look like.** "They look like people in front and empty on the back like the tiles of the
  roofs" (Gracia la Chiaczisa, 1619, in Geremia 2024, p. 8).
- **A living healer, 1985.** "Marzia" (a pseudonym), a 50-year-old healer from a mountain village south of Palermo,
  told Henningsen "it is the spirit that flies" while the body stays at home; her family did not move her body while
  she was "out", so the spirit could find its way back; "we need to be three to go out". She did not believe in
  fairies but called herself a night traveller (Henningsen 2020, pp. 1072–1074 and n. 9). Single source.
- **Prayer, wool and oil heal.** A healer of the Potenzano family "recitando una orazione e toccando con lana ed olio
  le ferite più gravi, le sanava" (reciting a prayer and touching the worst wounds with wool and oil, healed them)
  (Pitrè 1896, pp. 12–13).
- **Some families are born with a gift.** The Geranio families were held to make venomous snake bites harmless;
  others to heal with the touch of the right hand alone (Pitrè 1896, pp. 12–13).
- **Faith does the healing.** "Fidi mi salva e no lignu di varca" ("Faith saves me, not the timber of the boat")
  (Pitrè 1896, p. 14).
- **Worms come from a fright.** Worm-charmers hold that worms are "shaken" by a fright (*scantu*) and climb to the
  throat, and that the charm "cuts" them (Napoli 2008).
- **A charm loses its power if sold.** Healers must not be paid in money "at risk of losing their powers", only in
  kind (Napoli 2008, p. 5). A charm passes to a disciple only after the master dies (pp. 5–6).
- **Envy can strike.** Migliore's Sicilian-Canadian informants hold that the evil eye can be cast by a witch or by an
  ordinary person whose envy or hostility grows strong enough (Antze 1999, summarising Migliore 1997).
- **Coral and bells protect.** Coral as an amulet, a rattle of silver bells for a child, and a mother's horned-hand
  gesture against a stranger's look (Intorre 2025, reporting early-20th-c. English collectors).
- **San Benito stopped a blowout.** In Zulia, oral tradition holds that San Benito stopped the Barroso II oil
  blowout in December 1922, after nine days of flow (Diocese of Cabimas, pastoral letter 2024, §13).

## Charm texts

Only texts recorded in an opened source. **No Sicilian evil-eye charm text was found in any opened source.**

1. **Worm charm, Belpasso (Etna)**: "U Luni santu, l'addevu è spantu, / lu Marti santu, lu vermu è ntô sô mantu,"
   ("Holy Monday, the newborn child is frightened, / Holy Tuesday, the worm is in his guise,"). The charm goes on one
   line per day of Holy Week to Easter, and the worms are "cut" in numbered steps, often to ten. Saints Laura and
   Agatha and the Madonna appear in other charms. Source: Napoli 2008, p. 10 of 19 (opened). The English is Napoli's.

## Q7 verdict

**Yes: the research supports a main section near the end, but on documented links that are narrower than "Spain
made them both gaudy".** The links run from Sicily outward through the Spanish and Portuguese empires in the 16th to
18th centuries, not through later migration.

Documented links (rows `checked` unless noted):
1. **One Inquisition.** Sicily's tribunal (from 1487) was part of the Spanish Inquisition that later set up Lima
   (1570), Mexico (1571) and Cartagena (1610) (X3-01, X3-115; the American dates are Lea only, X3-117 to X3-119).
2. **Sicilian Black saints went to the Americas before Sicilians did.** Benedict the Moor, born in San Fratello to
   formerly enslaved African parents, died in Palermo in 1589 and was canonized in 1807 (X3-122, X3-124, X3-126,
   X3-128). His cult travelled through Iberian Black confraternities (first São Benedito brotherhood, Lisbon 1609) to
   Brazil and the Iberian New World from the early 1600s (X3-134, X3-136). Antonio of Noto (d. 1549/50) is still
   venerated in Brazil as Santo Antônio de Categeró (X3-148, X3-150). In Venezuela the *chimbangueles* drum the saint
   through the streets (X3-158). Fiume's line that their devotion is now more alive in Latin America than in Sicily is
   one source (X3-146). This story runs through the Atlantic slave trade and needs a serious, respectful tone.
3. **Rosalia crossed too.** Her cult reached New Spain and Spanish America through the Jesuits and the Spanish
   monarchy's networks (X3-166). Mulegé's name was not shown to come from Sicilian Jesuits (X3-180, myth).
4. **St Joseph as a shared "grandparent".** Mexico's first Church council took Joseph as patron of New Spain's
   Church in 1555 (X3-181). That Spanish rule helped root Joseph in Sicily is one scholar's hedged view (X3-183).

Resemblance only (say so in the piece):
- Candles, ex-votos/retablos and the baroque "gaudy" look: a pan-Catholic, post-Trent habit, with no shared Sicily–Latin
  America origin found (X3-201, X3-203).
- The Day of the Dead and the Festa dei Morti: plausibly cousins through All Souls' Day, but no source makes that link
  for Sicily (X3-198). "Mexico got it from Sicily" is a myth (X3-199).
- St Joseph tables: no Latin American *mesas* found (X3-189). The Holy Family pageant and Las Posadas look alike
  (X3-185).
- Migration: Sicilians went mostly to the US (X3-205, single source). Where Sicilian and Latino Catholics really meet
  in the record, it is in the US: an East Los Angeles parish (X3-186) and New Orleans (X3-187). That makes a side note.

Gaps that would strengthen it: Benedict and Antonio in Spanish America outside Venezuela (Rowe 2019; Sandoval,
Cartagena); souls in Purgatory compared with Latin American *ánimas*; Sicilian–Spanish baroque art; any scholar who
explicitly compares Sicilian and Latin American popular Catholicism.

## Decisions for Bryan

Only disputes that change what the page shows.

1. ***Donne di fuora*: how many? (X3-46)** The sources give 65, 77 or 193 because they count different things:
   65 is the people tried in the surviving reports to Madrid, 1579–1651 (eight of them men); 77 is one historian's
   charge category "contact with the dead"; 193 may count every woman named, companions included. Options: say
   "dozens were tried" with no number; or print "one count finds 65, eight of them men" with an "as cited" note; or
   show all three with what each counts. Or send a scan of Henningsen 1990 (below) and it can probably be settled.
2. **Executions, all crimes, 1540–1700 (X3-30).** 25 counts only the surviving reports; 52 adds other papers.
   Options: "25 to 52", "a few dozen", or both with a note. (Neither was for magic.)
3. **Benedict's birth year (X3-123).** 1524 or 1526; no birth record exists. Options: "born in 1524 (some lives say
   1526)", "born in the 1520s", or just "1524" with a note.
4. **Was Benedict born free? (X3-125).** Two scholars say born free; one says freed at 18. Options: give only that his
   parents had been enslaved, or give both versions.
5. **Salvador's first brotherhood, c. 1630 (X3-138).** It's unclear whether it was Benedict's or a general Franciscan
   one. Options: "from the 1600s" with no year, or "1630" with a note.

## Corrections to carry into figures.md
- Henningsen 1990's subtitle is "An Archaic Pattern of the Witches' Sabbath", not "Fairies, Witches, and Poverty in
  Early Modern Sicily" (D2 had it wrong; G3).
- "70 trials" of *donne di fuora* has no source: drop it (G3).
- Rio's "1640" brotherhood is the Rosary brotherhood, not Benedict's (G3).
- Benedict was beatified in 1743, not 1763; canonized on 24 May, not 25 May, 1807 (G3).
- "Categeró = Caltagirone" is not a debunked myth but one scholar's hedged guess (Zarattini 2025, pp. 147–148); only
  the confident popular version gets a myth label.
