## How the status rules were applied

- **Independent** means a different author or institution. The same document read by two reports counts once (for
  example Pitrè's *Feste patronali*, read by A2 and G2; Geremia 2024, read by A1 and A2; the Treccani DBI entry, read
  by A1 and A2; Britannica, read by A1 and A2).
- **Never counted toward the two:** Wikipedia, tourism pages (including the Comune di Catania tourism portal), blogs
  (including the Leicester project blog), aggregators (Tuttitalia, lasiciliainrete.it), news sites (CNA/EWTN,
  LiveSicilia, NewSicilia, Corriere Etneo, Balarm) and popular magazines (*Image*, *Saudi Aramco World*, NPR). A BA
  thesis (Aaron 2015) is treated as a lead, not as scholarship. The Padua MA thesis (Montalto) is counted only as a
  weak second source for a belief, never for a figure.
- **Counted, with a note:** Church institutions (Vatican City State, Cattedrale di Palermo, Santuario di Santa
  Rosalia, dioceses) for their own dates and traditions; the Comune di Palermo's history page (a municipal page, not a
  tourism portal); Britannica, the Met's Heilbrunn Timeline and the 1907–1911 *Catholic Encyclopedia* (CE) as reference
  works. The Cattedrale and the Santuario both belong to the Palermo church, so they are not counted as independent of
  each other.
- **A count inside one primary document** (the REI register, Pitrè's 1899 sample, an ICCD record) can only come from
  that document. Several workers recounting it confirms the reading, not the fact, so these rows are `single source
  (primary)`. They are listed for Bryan under "Decisions" as candidates to accept with a visible note.
- **Quotes:** a quote read in the original text is "original opened"; a quote seen only inside another author's work is
  "seen cited". Translations into English made by a worker are marked DRAFT.
- **Belief** rows say what practitioners hold. They are never figures and are listed again under "Belief statements".

## Belief statements

Reported as what practitioners or devotees hold, never as fact. Each with its source.

1. **Decollati.** Devotees hold that the souls of the executed, in Purgatory, "take the side of the weak" and can grant
   health, protection from robbers, help in love, the return of someone gone, and three lottery numbers (Pitrè 1889,
   pp. 11–19; lottery numbers from Purgatory souls also in Campanella 2023). They hold that the souls walk at night in
   human form, in white, near the Oreto (Pitrè 1889, p. 21; Pitrè, *La vita in Palermo* vol. 1; Davies & Matteoni 2017,
   citing Hartland 1910). A cock crowing or a dog barking after the prayer is a good sign; a cat's mew or a splash of
   water a bad one (Pitrè 1889, p. 20). Monday and Friday are "days sacred to them" (Pitrè 1889, p. 14).
2. **Agatha and Etna.** Catanians invoke Agatha against the eruptions of Etna (Kirsch, CE 1907); her veil is held to
   have stopped lava, and a chronicler reports it held up against lava in 1190 (Aaron 2015, citing Roger of Howden: a
   thesis; Howden not opened).
3. **Rosalia and the plague.** Tradition, as the Palermo cathedral and the Comune report it, holds that the plague
   stopped from the day of the first relic procession, 9 June 1625 (Cattedrale di Palermo; Comune di Palermo;
   Bizzarro 2020: the city "delivered" between 9 June and 15 July 1625).
4. **Rosalia for Tamil devotees.** Tamil Hindus in Palermo find in her a "Great Mother" who protects marriage and
   children (Salerno 2024).
5. **St Joseph.** Families lay the table to keep a vow (ICCD14955029). Guests take a fava bean home; in New Orleans
   these were "lucky beans" carried as charms (Del Giudice 2010, p. 17), the bean recalling the legume that "saved
   people from starvation" (Canning & Zinni 2018, p. 30).
6. **Festa dei Morti.** The dead return on 2 November and bring children gifts (Campanella 2023; Montalto 2022–23). In
   the Messina area, sweets are left out because the dead are believed to eat them (Montalto, p. 42; thesis only).
7. **Worm-charmers.** Healers hold that worms are "shaken" by a fright (*scantu*) and climb to the throat; that the
   charm "cuts" them; that a disciple may use the charm only after the master dies; that it passes man to woman as well
   as man to man; that it may be learned over seven Fridays or recited at Christmas midnight Mass; and that a healer
   paid in money loses the power (Napoli 2008, pp. 5–6). Napoli notes the rules aren't always kept.
8. **Evil eye.** In the Racalmuto-descended community Migliore studied, the evil eye is cast by witches or by ordinary
   people whose envy becomes strong enough to harm (Antze 1999, on Migliore 1997). Coral, silver bells and a raised
   thumb-and-little-finger gesture are held to protect children (Intorre 2025, from early-20th-c. collectors).
9. **Aeolian *maga*.** Afflictions named in the Aeolian Islands: *scantu*, *vranca matri*, *malocchi*, *fatture*
   (spells), *ligamenti* (bindings) (Maffei 2021, via review).
10. **Madonna della Lettera.** Messinesi hold that Mary wrote to their city and blessed it, and that the hair on the
    vara is hers (Campagna 2025; Arcidiocesi di Messina 2026). The 1636 famine ended, the record says, "per prodigio
    di Nostra Donna della S. Lettera" (Ciaramitaro 2005, p. 21). The Holy Office condemned the letter in 1598
    (Franceschini 2019).
11. **The saints in general (southern Italy).** Rossi records the poor of the South holding the saints to be the "only
    force able to solve the problems of this world" (Russo 2019, p. 293, quoting Rossi 1969, p. 5). In de Martino's
    account the "realistic" and "magical" sides of a technique are "not in conflict for the person using them" (Dei
    2018, p. 56, quoting *Sud e magia*). Both are about the South at large, not Sicily alone.

## Where the reports support, complicate or overturn the client's starting idea (Q2)

- **Supports:** Sicily keeps local, dated practices tied to its own saints and places (the 1624 Rosalia cult, the
  1126 Agatha return, the Messina Letter, the decollati, the St Joseph tables "with no counterpart in Spain").
- **Complicates:** "absorbed older traditions" is attested for *buildings* (temples into churches, late 6th c.;
  cathedral on a mosque site), not for cults. Every goddess-underneath claim checked here (Isis, Ceres, Tanit,
  Demeter, the bonfires) traces to a named 1823–1905 author or to an assertion, and the best current work rejects or
  can't back it. The four big city feasts are baroque creations (Benigno).
- **Complicates:** "what the rest of Catholicism does not" is hard to show from these sources. The evil eye is found
  across Europe (Pew) and the rest of the South (Abruzzo, Molise); cults of executed souls are traced to confraternities
  in southern Italy generally (Davies & Matteoni). What is distinctly Sicilian is the local form, names and calendar,
  not the kind of practice. No source in scope makes a "found only in Sicily" claim except Del Giudice on the tables
  (and only as "no counterpart in Spain or its colonies").
