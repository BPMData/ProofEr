## 1. Act 1 — Roman Emperor Decius's Edict of Sacrifice (249/250 CE)

### Findings
- **Accession and motive:** Gaius Messius Quintus Trajanus Decius reigned 249–251 CE after defeating Philip the Arabian. Facing Gothic invasions along the Danube and internal instability, he sought to restore traditional Roman piety and secure the *pax deorum* through empire-wide religious conformity.
- **The edict (early 250 CE):** Issued around January 250 CE (the exact text is lost, known from contemporary Christian accounts and surviving papyrus receipts). It ordered all inhabitants of the Roman Empire (with an explicit exemption for Jews as a *religio licita*) to perform a public sacrifice, pour libations, or burn incense to the Roman gods and for the emperor's well-being before local magistrates/commissions (*comitia sacrificiorum*).
- **Certificates (*libelli*) and Christian refusal:** Compliant citizens received a signed and witnessed papyrus certificate (*libellus*), dozens of which survive from Egypt (e.g., *P.Mich.* III 157, 250 CE). Because Christian monotheism forbade sacrificing to pagan gods, Christians faced imprisonment, torture, and execution (including Pope Fabian of Rome in Jan 250, Bishop Babylas of Antioch, and Bishop Alexander of Jerusalem); others fled or complied/bribed officials for *libelli* (*lapsi*, *sacrificati*, *libellatici*). It marked the first organized, empire-wide persecution of Christians (prior persecutions had been local and sporadic). This supplies the historical backdrop for Agatha's traditional martyrdom under Decius in the 250s (see `X1-82`, `X2-01`).

## 2. Act 3 — Messina's Civic Rivalry with Palermo & the *Vara* Float

### 2a. Messina vs. Palermo Civic Rivalry
- **Contest for *Caput Regni* and privileges:** Throughout the late Middle Ages and especially the 16th–17th centuries, Messina—wealthy from its port and silk trade—fiercely contested Palermo's status as capital (*Caput Regni*) of the Kingdom of Sicily, seat of the Sicilian Parliament and Spanish Viceroy. Under Spanish rule, the rivalry grew so intense that the Spanish viceroy was obliged to divide his official residence between Palermo and Messina, and Messina held extraordinary corporate privileges (including minting coin, exclusive local court jurisdiction for its province, and ambassadorial honors for its *Universitas* / Senate envoys).
- **Patron-saint propaganda (*Madonna della Lettera*):** Messina's Senate and expatriates deployed the cult of the *Madonna della Lettera* (claiming a 42 CE apostolic blessing from the Virgin) as a spiritual charter of civic independence and preeminence, commissioning prominent altarpieces abroad (e.g., Rome 1642; Mattia Preti's 1664 painting in Valletta, Malta) that drew Inquisition scrutiny and jurisdictional pushback. (Meanwhile, Palermo promoted Santa Rosalia after 1624.)
- **Climax and collapse (1674–1678):** Driven by fiscal strain, viceregal friction, and internal factions (*Merli* and *Malvizzi*), Messina revolted against Spain in July 1674 and sought French protection under Louis XIV. After France withdrew at the Peace of Nijmegen (1678), Spain suppressed Messina harshly: dissolving its historic Senate, closing its university, abolishing its centuries-old privileges, and building the *Real Cittadella*, leaving Palermo undisputed capital of Sicily.

### 2b. Physical Description and Mechanics of Messina's *Vara* Float
- **Note on the two *vare* (`X1-131`, `X1-133`, `X1-204`):** Messina's 3 June *Madonna della Lettera* feast carries the hair reliquary on a silver *vara*, whereas the monumental pyramidal *Vara* (dialect for *bara*, bier) belongs to the 15 August Feast of the Assumption (*Ferragosto*), traditionally traced in its mechanical form to 1535 (celebrating Emperor Charles V's entry after Tunis; present baroque/late-16th-c. form per Benigno citing Giorgianni 1995).
- **Physical structure:** A towering, multi-tiered pyramidal votive machine (*macchina votiva*) standing roughly **13.5 to 14 meters** (approx. 44–46 ft; one metropolitan summary says 14–16 m) tall and weighing about **8 tons**. Following a Ptolemaic/cosmological scheme:
  - *Base:* The Virgin's bier / Dormition (*Dormitio Virginis*) surrounded by the Twelve Apostles.
  - *Middle tiers:* Revolving celestial spheres, sun, moon, zodiac/stars, and hosts of angels (in earlier centuries, live children—*camiddhri*—rode the moving tiers before safety concerns replaced them with papier-mâché/wooden statues; internal wooden/iron gears rotate the spheres).
  - *Apex:* Christ holding the soul/figure of the Virgin Mary in his right hand.
- **How it is pulled and worked:** Mounted on heavy iron/steel sleds or runners (*cippu*) with **no wheels or mechanical steering/brakes**. Along a ~3 km route (Piazza Castronovo → Via Garibaldi → Piazza Duomo), fire/water crews continuously wet the asphalt ahead to cut friction while roughly **1,000 devotees (*tiratori*)**—many barefoot by vow, in white shirts and blue sashes—haul **two massive hemp ropes (*gomene*) over 100 meters long**, chanting *"Viva Maria!"* Maritime-named crewmen around the base (*timonieri*, *vogatori*, *capicorda*) steer the sleds with poles, culminating in a sharp pivot (*la girata* / *virata*) into Piazza Duomo. (Afterward, pieces of rope are traditionally cut and distributed as keepsakes.)

## 3. Act 4 — St Joseph's Tables (*Tavolate* / *Altari*): Foods & the Diaspora Fava-Bean Legend

### 3a. Specific Traditional Breads, Pastries, and Foods
- **Ceremonial breads:** Alongside the massive braided *cucciddati* ("often 3 to 7 kg", `X1-146`, Del Giudice 2010, p. 3), bakers sculpt ornate ritual breads into symbolic religious and carpentry forms: St. Joseph's staff, crosses, monstrances, ladders, crowns, carpenter's tools, baskets, fish, birds, animals, and flowers (often dusted with sesame seeds).
- **Pastries and sweets:** *Sfince* / *sfinci di San Giuseppe* (fried dough fritters filled with sweetened ricotta cream, candied fruit, and pistachios), *cuccidati* / *cucciddati* (Sicilian fig-filled cookies/pastries), and *pignolata*.
- **Meatless Lenten dishes & fava beans:** Because 19 March falls in Lent, tables are strictly meatless: *pasta con le sarde* and *pasta con la mollica* (pasta topped with toasted breadcrumbs representing carpenter's sawdust), whole fish, fried artichokes, vegetable omelets (*frittate*), *maccu* (fava bean soup), fresh fruit, and dried **fava beans** (taken home by guests and carried as "lucky beans").

### 3b. Why Sicilian-American Emigrant Communities Tell the Medieval Fava-Bean Famine Legend
- **Historical status (`X1-149`, `X3-191`):** Del Giudice (2010, pp. 17–18) established that the story of a medieval Sicilian drought/famine broken by St. Joseph sending rain and fava beans appears *"almost exclusively in diaspora"* sources, with *"little trace… in homeland Italian literature."*
- **Why the diaspora tells it:**
  1. **A "cultural exorcism of hunger":** Del Giudice (2010) analyzes the overflowing table and the famine/fava story together as a *"cultural exorcism of hunger"*—a way for communities shaped by chronic rural Sicilian poverty, scarcity, and emigration to invert historical deprivation into a public ritual of abundance, charity (*caritas*), and gratitude.
  2. **Re-enacting migration and ethnic identity:** Del Giudice also notes that diaspora tables serve as a "living archive" of cultural memory and ethnic identity in places like Los Angeles, subtly mapping the Holy Family's wandering and search for shelter onto the immigrant experience of displacement and hospitality.
  3. **Explaining the rite and passing down luck:** In Louisiana (Green 2016) and Western New York (Canning & Zinni 2018, p. 30), Sicilian-American families use the famine story to explain *why* an animal-fodder legume (the dried fava bean) sits at the center of a sacred table and why guests pocket a "lucky fava bean" so the household will never go hungry or broke.

## 4. Act 6 — Palazzo Chiaramonte-Steri Pre-1601, the *Cimaruta*, and the Sicilian Oil-in-Water Test

### 4a. Palazzo Chiaramonte-Steri Before 1601
- **Origins (early 14th c.):** Built in Palermo's Kalsa quarter near the port (Piazza Marina) starting in the early 1300s (c. 1307–1320s; from *hosterium*, fortified palace) by the powerful feudal Chiaramonte family (Giovanni Chiaromonte / Manfredi I Chiaramonte). Manfredi III Chiaramonte commissioned the famous painted wooden ceiling of the *Sala Magna* (1377–1380).
- **Confiscation (1392) and royal/viceregal period:** Following the Aragonese restoration under King Martin I, Andrea Chiaramonte was accused of treason and beheaded outside the palace in **1392**; the Chiaramonte estates were confiscated into the royal domain.
- **Before the Inquisition (15th–16th c.):** Served as a royal residence, then as the seat of the **Spanish viceroys** of Sicily from **1468 to 1517**, and later housed the Royal Customs (*Regia Dogana*) before being handed over to the Spanish Inquisition in 1601 (`X3-13`, `X3-14`; restored in the 20th century, now the Rectorate of the University of Palermo).

### 4b. Physical Description of the *Cimaruta* Amulet
- **Name and form:** *Cimaruta* (literally "sprig of rue," *cima di ruta* in Neapolitan/southern Italian dialect) is a traditional Italian silver compound amulet (best known from Naples and southern Italy, and recorded by early-20th-century English collectors in Sicily as well).
- **Physical details:** Cast in **silver**, shaped as a branching (often triple-stemmed) sprig of the rue plant (*Ruta graveolens*) terminating in multiple miniature apotropaic charms hanging from the branches—commonly including a **crescent moon**, a **key**, a **fish**, a **serpent**, a **flower/vervain**, a **rooster**, a **heart**, a **dagger/sword**, or a **hand** (such as *mano fica* or *mano cornuta*). Worn as a pendant/necklace or hung above infants' cradles to ward off the evil eye (*malocchio*). (Early-20th-century collectors linked it to Diana Triformis—a classic "survivals" hypothesis, not a historical record: `X1-176`, `X3-93`.)

### 4c. Sicilian Oil-in-Water Evil-Eye Test (Second Scholarly Source)
Previously in `figures.md`, `X1-248` had **Marco Fragale (Università di Palermo, 2021)** on Gratteri (citing Guggino 2006, pp. 20–21, and Pitrè 1889/1896), while `X3-95` listed the oil-in-water test as unverified pending a second opened Sicilian source. We now have multiple independent scholarly confirmations for Sicily:
- **Procedure & diagnosis in Sicily:** A healer (often an older woman) takes a shallow plate or bowl filled with water (*piatto fondo* / *u piattinu*), sometimes held over the afflicted person's head, makes the sign of the cross while reciting prayers/charms (*orazioni* / *ciarmi*), and drops drops of olive oil (commonly three drops from a fingertip) onto the water's surface. If the oil drops **remain intact/round**, there is no evil eye; if they **spread, break apart, or disperse**, the person is diagnosed with *malocchio* / *ucchiatura* (distinguished in Aeolian fieldwork as fresh vs. chronic, *maluocchiu friscu* vs. *siccu*), and the rite continues (often with salt crosses, cutting gestures, or repeated drops) to dissolve it.

#### Sources
1. **Marco Fragale (Università di Palermo)**, "U ciarmu: apotropaic rites and healing formulas of the people of Gratteri," 31 Mar 2021 (already in `X1-248`, citing **Elsa Guggino**, *Fate, sibille e altre strane donne*, Sellerio, 2006, pp. 20–21, and Pitrè 1889/1896).
2. **Macrina Marilena Maffei**, *La maga e il velo. Incantesimi, riti e poteri del mondo magico eoliano* (Rome: CISU, 2021), documented in publisher/university records (`cisu.it`, `unime.it`) and reviewed by **Mauro Geraci** in *Humanities* 10(1) (2021), pp. 197–199 (already cited in `X1-209`), documenting Aeolian Sicilian *maluocchiu* diagnostics using water in a plate and oil drops.
3. **Giuseppe Pitrè**, *Medicina popolare siciliana* (Torino–Palermo: Clausen, 1896; *Biblioteca delle tradizioni popolari siciliane* XIX), pp. 176–184 ("Jettatura e malocchio" from p. 181; `archive.org` scan summaries confirm Pitrè documents the oil-and-water diagnostic/healing practice alongside olive oil's centrality in Sicilian folk medicine) and **Elsa Guggino**, *La magia in Sicilia* (Palermo: Sellerio, 1978), foundational fieldwork on Sicilian folk magic and healers (*maari*).

## 5. Act 8 — St. Benedict the Moor & Antonio of Noto (Biographical Details)

### 5a. St. Benedict of San Fratello / the Moor (1524/1526–1589)
*(Builds on `X3-122`..`X3-128`: born San Fratello to enslaved African parents; died 4 Apr 1589 at Santa Maria di Gesù, Palermo; beatified May 1743; canonized 24 May 1807.)*
- **Youth & hermitage:** Worked as a shepherd in his youth. Joined an independent community of Franciscan-affiliated lay hermits led by **Girolamo Lanza** (living first near San Fratello and on **Monte Pellegrino** above Palermo), succeeding Lanza as the hermits' **superior**.
- **Santa Maria di Gesù, Palermo:** After Pope Pius IV ordered independent hermit congregations to disband and join established orders (1562/1564), Benedict entered the Observant Franciscan friary of **Santa Maria di Gesù** in Palermo as a lay brother (*converso*).
- **Illiterate cook & reluctant guardian:** Though **illiterate**, Benedict gladly served as the friary's **cook** in the kitchen. Despite his lack of schooling and humble station, his reputation for wisdom, humility, charity, and miracles led the friars to elect him **guardian (superior)** of the convent (1578) and later master of novices; once his term ended, he returned joyfully to cooking.
- **Reputation & civic honor:** Visitors from every level of society, including nobles and viceroys, sought his counsel and prayers, and he was famed for charity toward the poor. The Senate of Palermo proclaimed him patron of the city in **1652** (nearly a century before his beatification).

### 5b. Antonio of Noto / *Antonio l'Etiope* / *Santo Antônio de Categeró* (d. 1549/1550)
*(Builds on `X3-147`..`X3-154`: captive from Barca mountains, Libya, enslaved by Sicilian galleys; died March 1549 or 1550 at Noto.)*
- **Capture, slavery, and shepherd life:** Born c. 1490 in Barce/Barca (Cyrenaica, Libya) to a Muslim family; captured by Sicilian galleys, brought to Sicily (Syracuse), and sold into slavery. Employed as a **shepherd** in the territory of **Avola** (and later Noto), he converted to Christianity, taking the name Antonio.
- **Freedom and hermitage:** Freed by his masters in Noto on account of his exemplary virtue, he lived as a **Franciscan tertiary and hermit** (in the Valle dei Pizzoni / near the hermitage of San Corrado outside Noto), dedicating himself to asceticism and charity—serving the **sick, the poor, and prisoners**—until his death in March 1549 or 1550 (buried in Santa Maria del Gesù, Noto; 38 witnesses testified to his sanctity on 22 April 1549 per Fiume 2009, pp. 56, 58).

## 6. Overture — Tarot Occultists (Verified Life Dates & Roles)

All six figures verified from at least two independent encyclopedic, national-library, museum, or scholarly sources:

1. **Antoine Court de Gébelin (1725–1784):** Swiss-born French Protestant pastor, scholar, and Freemason. In *Le Monde primitif* (vol. 8, **1781**), he became the first to claim publicly that tarot cards preserved an ancient Egyptian sacred book (the *Book of Thoth*) brought to Europe by "Gypsies" (*bohémiens*), launching occult tarot.
   - *Sources:* **Bibliothèque nationale de France** (`data.bnf.fr` authority record); **Helen Farley**, "Out of Africa: Tarot's Fascination With Egypt," *Literature & Aesthetics* 21(1) (2011), pp. 178–180 (already in `X1-216`/`X1-217`); **Morgan Library** Visconti-Sforza essay (`X1-216`); **Decker, Depaulis & Dummett**, *A Wicked Pack of Cards* (1996).
2. **Etteilla / Jean-Baptiste Alliette (1738–1791):** French occultist and cartomancer who reversed his surname ("Alliette" → "Etteilla"). Building on Court de Gébelin, he published tarot divination treatises (1783–86), was the first to assign systematic divinatory meanings to tarot cards, and designed the first deck created specifically for fortune-telling (c. 1789; V&A notes "in the 1770s" for his early cartomancy).
   - *Sources:* **Bibliothèque nationale de France** (`data.bnf.fr` authority record); **Victoria and Albert Museum** (`vam.ac.uk`, "Tarot cards"); **Public Domain Review**, "Etteilla's Livre de Thot" (`X1-218`); **Decker, Depaulis & Dummett** (1996).
3. **Papus / Gérard Encausse (1865–1916):** Spanish-born French physician and prolific occultist; co-founder of the Martinist Order and leading figure of the French Occult Revival, inspired by Éliphas Lévi. Wrote *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* (*The Tarot of the Bohemians*, **1889**), synthesizing Court de Gébelin, Etteilla, and Lévi into a systematic esoteric tarot manual.
   - *Sources:* **Bibliothèque nationale de France** (`data.bnf.fr` authority record); **Encyclopædia Britannica**, "Occultism" (`britannica.com`); **Helen Farley** (2011), p. 187 (`X1-220`); **Decker, Depaulis & Dummett** (1996).
4. **Arthur Edward Waite (1857–1942):** American-born British poet, mystic, and occult scholar; member of the **Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn** (later leading its Christian-oriented Independent and Rectified Rite). Conceived the esoteric design, directed artist Pamela Colman Smith, and wrote *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* for the **Rider-Waite-Smith** deck published by William Rider & Son in **1909** (early printings dated 1909/1910 in V&A records, `X1-222`).
   - *Sources:* **Encyclopædia Britannica** ("Occultism" / Western esotericism, `britannica.com`); **Victoria and Albert Museum** (`vam.ac.uk`); **Yale University Library** archival records (`library.yale.edu`).
5. **Pamela Colman Smith (1878–1951):** British-American artist, illustrator, and Golden Dawn member (nicknamed "Pixie"). Commissioned by A. E. Waite in **1909**, she painted all 78 cards of the **Rider-Waite-Smith** tarot deck, breaking with European tradition by illustrating full narrative scenes on all Minor Arcana pip cards rather than plain suit symbols.
   - *Sources:* **Victoria and Albert Museum** (`vam.ac.uk`); **Yale University Library** archival records (`library.yale.edu`).
6. **Aleister Crowley (1875–1947) & Lady Frieda Harris (1877–1962) / *The Book of Thoth* (1938–1944):** British occultist, writer, mountaineer, and founder of **Thelema**; initiated into the **Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn** in **1898**. Between **1938 and 1943/1944**, Crowley collaborated with British artist **Lady Frieda Harris** (1877–1962), who painted the 78 cards of the **Thoth Tarot** under his direction; Crowley published his companion text, ***The Book of Thoth: A Short Essay on the Tarot of the Egyptians***, on **21 March 1944** (limited edition of 200 copies in *The Equinox* III.5). Harris bequeathed the original paintings, now held with correspondence at the **Warburg Institute** in London.
   - *Sources:* **Encyclopædia Britannica**, "Aleister Crowley" (`britannica.com`); **The Warburg Institute, University of London** (`sas.ac.uk`, holding Harris's original paintings and manuscripts); **Oxford University Press** (`oup.com`, *The Lady and the Beast: The Extraordinary Partnership between Frieda Harris and Aleister Crowley*).
