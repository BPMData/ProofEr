## Short summary

- **The four big city feasts are baroque creations, not ancient survivals, in the best Italian scholarship opened.**
  These are Palermo's Rosalia, Catania's Agata, Messina's Assunta/Vara and Trapani's Misteri. Benigno (Univ. Teramo
  site) calls all four "creations of the baroque" and "urban productions". Petrarca (1986/2008) shows the Rosalia cult
  as a Jesuit- and Senate-shaped "urban rebirth" after the relics were found in 1624. On his account the festino's
  civic, show-off character dates from about 1649.
- **The "pagan goddess underneath" claims can be traced to named authors.** Agata = Ceres/Eleusis goes back to the
  English traveller J. J. Blunt (1823). Agata = Isis goes back to Emanuele Ciaceri (1905). Its 2021 reprint editor in
  *ASSO* praises the method but says Ciaceri's conservative ideology shaped his conclusions. Rosalia's cave = a Punic
  (Tanit) shrine is offered as "probable" by Petrarca and asserted by the sanctuary's own site. Neither cites any
  inscription. Enna = the ancient seat of Ceres/Proserpina is solid ancient testimony (Cicero). That a Marian feast
  "replaced" her there is a modern local claim with no source.
- **The best institutional primary source on San Giuseppe is an ICCD inventory record.** This is the Italian state
  catalogue of intangible heritage (MODI form, 2015). It says the Sicilian rites run January to September and centre on
  19 March. They include bonfires, collecting alms, auctions, a ritual banquet for poor people or children who play the
  Holy Family, and verses in Sicilian, usually to keep a vow. Ethnography (Giacomarra; Giallombardo 2006) records the
  Madonie *virgineddi* meals.
- **The official register is the Regione Siciliana's REI/REIS.** It was set up by D.A. 77 of 26 July 2005 and redone as
  REIS by D.A. 571 of 5 March 2014, in six "Libri". The Regione's page gives 177 elements for the old REI. A copy of
  the September 2013 table held on UNESCO's site lists 177 entries. About 86 of them are in the Celebrazioni book,
  including 11 San Giuseppe entries. Later totals of 201 and 305 come from a wiki and an aggregator.
- **No count of patron saints or patronal feasts across Sicily's 391 comuni was found.** Pitrè's *Feste patronali in
  Sicilia* (1900) is the classic survey but wasn't opened.

## Disagreements

1. **Year Rosalia entered the Roman Martyrology: 1630 vs 1631.** The Cattedrale di Palermo quotes the martyrology entry
   under 1630, and Margiotta (2018, p. 10) says Urban VIII "canonized" her in 1630. Scalisi (2023, p. 195) says 1631.
   This may be the gap between a papal decision or an edition's preparation and a printed edition. Check against a
   dated edition of the *Martyrologium Romanum*. Margiotta's "canonized" is looser than either source supports.
2. **When the festino starts: 1624 vs 1625.** The Comune di Palermo homepage says "Dal 1624" ("since 1624"), counting
   from the find on 15 July 1624. The cathedral, Palazzotto (p. 63) and Margiotta (p. 15) date the first procession or
   festino to 1625 (9 June). Edition numbers follow 1625 = 1st (400th = 2024). Petrarca adds a third view: the festino
   as a civic show really dates from about 1649.
3. **Who found Rosalia's bones.** The Comune names the sailor Vito Amodeo, guided by La Gattuta. The cathedral and the
   Santuario put La Gattuta's visions at the centre. Scalisi says Franciscans and then Jesuits ran the search. These are
   different layers of one story (a visionary, a digging crew, a religious order), not necessarily contradictions.
4. **Bonello's vision and La Gattuta's illness.** The cathedral dates La Gattuta's dream to May 1624. The Santuario has
   her first vision in October 1623 and her climb on 26 May 1624. Both are hagiographic narratives, so treat their dates
   as the tradition's dates.
5. **REI/REIS totals: 177 vs 201 vs 305.** 177 is the old REI to Sept 2013 (Regione page and the UNESCO-held table).
   201 is Wikipedia IT (no footnote, date unclear). 305 is lasiciliainrete.it's count of its own archive posts, not
   register entries. The differences come from date and from what is counted. Only 177 is institutionally sourced.
6. **Number of *candelore* in Catania: 16 vs "11".** 16 comes from Wikipedia EN via *Italy Magazine*. 11 is common in
   popular retellings and wasn't verified. Possibly different counting rules (guild *candelore* only, or also the
   bishop's and others'). Unresolved.
7. **Where the Madonna della Lettera letter came from.** The legend dates the letter to 42 AD. Wikipedia IT also
   claims a translation from Arabic was brought in 1716 by a monk, Gregorio Arena, and that a nun's visions confirmed it
   in 1647. These details are uncited and sit oddly with Benigno's late-16th-century dating of the merged feast. Not
   resolved. Needs the Arcidiocesi di Messina or Giorgianni (1995).

## Myths

| Popular claim | Label | Where it started / who argues each side |
|---|---|---|
| The Palermo festino and Rosalia's cult are an unbroken survival of a pagan goddess cult (Tanit, Persephone) on Monte Pellegrino | **Contested; mostly unsupported as "unbroken survival"** | *For a pre-Christian sacred site:* Petrarca 2008, p. 19 ("probabilmente dedicato a Tanit", "probably dedicated to Tanit"); the Santuario's Mazzola, citing Giustolisi, on the niche-shrine. *Against continuity:* Petrarca himself says the parallels "do not prove identical origins" and reads the 1624 cult as a Jesuit- and Senate-made urban construction. Palazzotto (p. 61) finds a *weak* medieval Christian cult, not a pagan one. Benigno calls the festino a baroque creation. No source opened gives evidence of cult continuity from the Punic period to the 12th century. |
| Sant'Agata's feast is a Christianised Isis festival (the *Navigium Isidis*, the boat-shaped cart, white robes, the veil) | **Contested** | Started with E. Ciaceri, *ASSO* 1905. Revived in local media (Corriere Etneo 2026) and web essays (Barbagallo). His 2021 editor praises the method but calls the frame ideologically conditioned. Benigno dates the white *sacco*, cap and gloves to the 18th century, which undercuts the robe parallel. Archaeological evidence of an Isis cult in Catania is asserted in the web essays but no excavation report was cited or opened. |
| Sant'Agata's feast continues the Ceres / Eleusinian mysteries | **Unsupported (an early travellers' parallel)** | Started with J. J. Blunt, *Vestiges of Antient Manners and Customs* (1823), as analysed by Walendziak-Genco (2018). Blunt admitted he couldn't prove the origins. Part of a 19th-century travellers' habit of reading Sicilian feasts as pagan relics (Forbin 1823 on Messina's Vara and the Panathenaea). |
| Enna's Madonna della Visitazione replaced Demeter/Ceres | **Half supported** | *Supported:* Enna was the ancient centre of the Ceres and Proserpina cult (Cicero, *Verr.* II.4.106-108). *Unsupported:* that the Marian feast (statue 1412, cart 1590) descends from it. The only source seen is an uncited Wikipedia page. |
| The San Giuseppe tables are a pagan spring or Demeter rite | **Contested / framed differently by ethnographers** | Giacomarra and the ICCD record read the rites in terms of the farming calendar, the poor, vows and food exchange. Giacomarra notes the spring link but makes no pagan or Demeter claim. No source opened argues for a direct pagan origin. Treat it as a popular claim, not a scholarly one. |
| The Trapani Misteri are a medieval or ancient rite | **Unsupported** | Benigno: introduced by the Jesuits in the 17th c., replacing the *Cilii*. Earliest known guild concession: 1612 (lead). The "Spanish *casazza*" origin story is common but wasn't verified this run. |
| Sant'Agata is "the world's third-largest religious festival" | **Unsupported** | No institutional source or method found. Wikipedia EN says only "among the largest". |
| Mary wrote a letter to Messina in 42 AD | **Legend (a belief, reported as such)** | Wikipedia IT notes the dating problem and Mommsen's view that the only ancient witness is an interpolation. The belief itself is real and civic: the inscription at the port, the 3 June feast. |
