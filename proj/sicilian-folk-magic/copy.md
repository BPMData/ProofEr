## 0 · Curtain up

**Saints & Spells**

Under every saint in Sicily, the story goes, there's a goddess wearing a disguise. We put that story on stage and
checked it, claim by claim: who said it first, and what the record shows.

A show in eight acts. By Bryan Murphy · November 22, 2026

---

## 1 · Overture: under every saint, a goddess?

You've probably heard some version of it. Catania's Saint Agatha is really Isis, or Ceres. Palermo's Saint Rosalia
lives in a cave that once belonged to the Punic goddess Tanit. Scratch a Sicilian feast and you find a pagan rite
underneath. [X1-94, X1-95, X1-121]

It's a great story. It's also mostly a 19th-century one. [X1-19; premise check]

> **Mulder:** Two thousand years of goddesses hiding in plain sight. I want to believe.
> **Scully:** Then let's see who said it first.

What the record does show is stranger and better: a Church that moved into the island's temples and calendar, and
Sicilians who made its saints, relics and rites entirely their own.

---

## 2 · Primer: how scholars talk about this

**Syncretism.** The word is Greek. Plutarch used the verb for Cretans who stopped feuding and joined forces against
an outside enemy. Today it means beliefs from different sources blending into one practice. [X1-01]

**"Pagan survival."** In the late 19th century, anthropologists such as Tylor treated folk customs as fossils:
leftovers from an older religion, still twitching. That idea shaped how a generation read Sicily. Most scholars now
reject it as a general model, though a few still argue for long continuity in particular beliefs. [X1-17, X1-19]

**Three people to know:**
- **Giuseppe Pitrè**, in Palermo, collected Sicilian customs in 25 volumes and founded a journal for them in
  1882. Palermo created the first university chair in the study of folk belief for him in 1910. He also helped
  start the survival story. [X1-21, X1-26, X1-27; premise check]
- **Antonio Gramsci**, in his prison notebooks, argued that folklore was a worldview of its own, "to be taken
  seriously". [X1-10]
- **Ernesto de Martino** studied magic in Lucania, on the mainland, in the 1950s. He refused to call it a
  pre-Christian leftover and tied it to Catholic practice instead. His book *Sud e magia* came out in 1959.
  [X1-12, X1-13, X1-15]

> **Scully:** So the man who wrote down the customs also wrote the theory about them.
> **Mulder:** Someone has to start the file.

## 3 · Who ruled Sicily, and what they left

Before the colonists came, the island belonged to the Sicani, the Sicels and the Elymians, with Phoenicians trading
on the coasts. Greeks founded colonies from the 8th century BC. [X1-47, X1-50]

Then the island changed hands, again and again:
- **535:** Byzantium takes the island. [X1-56]
- **831:** Arabs take Palermo. [X1-58]
- **1061–1091:** the Normans conquer the island; Palermo falls in 1071–72. [X1-60, X1-61]
- **1130:** Roger II is crowned, and the Kingdom of Sicily begins. [X1-62]
- **1194–1266:** the Hohenstaufen. **1266–1282:** the Angevins. [X1-64, X1-66]
- **1282:** the Sicilian Vespers, and Aragon takes over. [X1-67]
- **1443:** one king for Naples and Sicily again. [X1-70]
- **1713:** Savoy. **1720–1734:** Austria. **1816:** the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies. [X1-74, X1-75, X1-77]

The Church didn't need to hide inside the old gods. It moved into their buildings. In the late 6th century the Temple
of Concord at Agrigento and the Athenaion at Syracuse became churches. Palermo's cathedral was rebuilt, from about
1170, on the site of a mosque. [X1-42, X1-43]

And in 1487 the Spanish Inquisition sent its first inquisitor to Sicily. Hold that thought. [X3-01]

## 4 · Act 1 · Catania: Agatha goes away, and comes back

Agatha is Catania's own saint. Around 1040, the Byzantine general George Maniakes took her body to Constantinople.
[X1-84, X2-02; **OPEN D4**: "about 1040, during Maniakes's campaign of 1038–40"]

On 17 August 1126 two soldiers, Gislebertus and Goselinus, brought it home. The clergy met it "nudis pedibus et in
albis vestibus": barefoot, in white. That makes 2026 the 900th year. [X1-86, X2-08, X2-10, X2-12, X1-97]

Catania still celebrates her from 3 to 5 February, and again on 17 August for the return. Devotees wear a white robe,
the *sacco*. One story says it recalls the nightshirts people ran out in when the relics came home in 1126. The
historian Benigno says the robe is an 18th-century creation. [X1-90, X2-35, X1-201, X1-93]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · the *sacco* remembers 1126 · no historical support [X2-34]

Her silver bust was made by Giovanni di Bartolo of Siena; its inscription dates it to 1376. Her crown is not a gift
from Richard the Lionheart, whatever you've heard: it was made in the late 15th century. [X2-18, X2-21]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · Richard the Lionheart's crown · made in the late 1400s [X2-21]

Devotees hold that her veil protects the city from Etna's lava. When Etna erupted in 1669 the veil was carried in
processions from March to July; the lava reached Catania on 30 April. [X2-28, X2-29]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · Agatha is Ceres in disguise · first claimed 1823 [X1-95]
The English writer J. J. Blunt said it first, and admitted he couldn't prove it. The Isis version comes from
Emanuele Ciaceri in 1905. A 2025 study's title says it plainly: "Né Demetra, né Iside" (neither Demeter nor Isis).
[X1-94, X1-95]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · "the world's third-largest religious festival" · no source found [X1-96]

> **Mulder:** A saint who stops a volcano.
> **Scully:** A city that walked into the lava's path carrying her veil. Both of those are true.

## 5 · Act 2 · Palermo: Rosalia and the plague

In May 1624 the plague reached Palermo. [X1-98]

Rosalia was a local hermit of the 12th century, as tradition tells it, with a small cult and not much else.
[X1-106, X1-107]

Then, on 15 July 1624, bones were found in a cave on Monte Pellegrino, after a sick woman's visions led the search.
[X1-99, X2-67, X1-100; **OPEN D7**: name Geronima La Gattuta, or keep her nameless]

Here is the timeline, and it is the punchline:
- **15 July 1624:** bones found. [X1-99]
- **27 July 1624:** the Senate elects Rosalia patron of Palermo. [X2-70]
- **November 1624:** the Church sets up a commission to check whether the bones are hers. [X2-72]
- **February 1625:** the commission says yes. [X1-102, X2-76]
- **9 June 1625:** the relics' first procession. [X1-104, X2-78]
- **15 July 1625:** the plague is declared over, one year to the day after the finding. [X1-105, X2-80]

Palermo made her its patron seven months before anyone confirmed whose bones they were. Palermitans credit her with
ending the plague; that is their belief, and they have kept it for four centuries. [X2-70, X1-104]

A *Life* of Rosalia came out in 1627, and her name entered the Roman Martyrology about 1630. Her relics have lain in
a silver urn in the cathedral since 1631. [X1-108, X1-109, X1-113, X2-85]

[**CARE NOTE X2-75**: the Bonelli vision of 4 February 1625 is left out here; its cathedral version has a suicide
theme.]

Today the *festino* runs 10 to 15 July, with the great cart on the evening of the 14th and the urn procession on the
15th. The 400th festino was in 2024. On the night of 3 to 4 September comes the *acchianata*, the climb up the
mountain to her cave. [X1-116, X1-117, X1-118, X1-202, X1-203]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · Rosalia's cave was Tanit's shrine · no finds, no inscription [X1-121]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · a geologist proved the bones were a goat's · a late, second-hand anecdote [X2-93]

> **Scully:** Elected first, authenticated later.
> **Mulder:** And the plague still ended. Just saying.

## 6 · Act 3 · Messina: a letter from Mary

Messina's feast on 3 June honors a letter. The legend says the Virgin Mary wrote to the people of Messina in the year
42, from Jerusalem, and tied the letter with a lock of her hair. [X1-123, X2-98, X1-130, X2-99]

The legend arose in the 16th century. The oldest manuscript copy is from 1599. [X1-124, X2-100, X2-103]

In 1629 a scholar named Inchofer published a defense of the letter. The Church's own censors, the Congregation
of the Index, objected; a corrected edition came out in 1632. [X1-126, X2-106]

The hair relic still rides the city's silver *vara* in procession. [X1-131]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · found in Greek and translated in the 1400s · the supporters' claim, unsupported [X1-127]
**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · "venerated since the Middle Ages" · oldest firm date: 1522 [X1-128]

> **Mulder:** A letter from the Virgin Mary.
> **Scully:** Censored by the Vatican for overselling it.

## 7 · Act 4 · St Joseph's tables

On 19 March, Joseph's feast, Sicilian families build tables of bread and food and feed three of the poorest people
they can find, often children, dressed as Mary, Joseph and Jesus: the *virgineddi*. [X1-138, X1-144, X1-205]

The night before, bonfires: the *vampata*. [X1-147, X1-206]

Pitrè recorded these meals for the poor by 1900 at the latest. Nobody knows exactly when they began. The folklorist
Del Giudice found nothing like them in Spain or its colonies. [X1-142, X1-141, X1-148 (single source, named)]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · a medieval famine ended by fava beans · told mostly by emigrants, with no source [X1-149]
**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · a Christianized Demeter rite · parallels, not descent [X1-150]

> **Mulder:** Feeding the poor as if they were the Holy Family.
> **Scully:** No myth required.

## 8 · Act 5 (night) · The dead come back

_The stage goes dark; the candle wall lights._

In Palermo, by the Oreto river, a church honors the *decollati*: the beheaded, executed criminals whom devotees
believe can intercede for the living. The church was rebuilt in 1785 and again later; executed people were buried
there. [X1-154]

In 1817 a man named Francesco Frusteri was beheaded at Paceco, and people made him a popular saint. [X1-159]

## 9 · Act 6 (night) · Witches, healers and the evil eye

From 1487 to 1782, Sicily had its own court of the Spanish Inquisition, answering to Madrid, not Rome. From 1601 it
sat in the Steri palace in Palermo. Its archive was burned in 1783. [X3-01, X3-09, X3-115, X3-13, X3-10]

**The numbers:** it tried about 6,000 to 7,000 people. About 1,000 were charged with magic or witchcraft. Between
1540 and 1700 it executed 25 to 52 people in person, for any charge, and almost no one for witchcraft. No woman is
known to have been executed as a witch. [X3-24, X3-33, X3-30, X3-36]

_[Unit chart: one square = 10 people tried; one dot = one person executed.]_

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · "thousands of witches burned in Sicily" · two men handed over in nearly three centuries
[X3-109]

**The ladies from outside.** Dozens of people stood trial for dealing with the *donne di fuora*. They were above all
healers: people went to them to cure illnesses the fairies were said to have caused. [X3-46, X3-53]

The historian Gustav Henningsen read the trials as a Sicilian fairy cult with a "white" sabbath, a shared set
of cultural dreams. Was it shamanism? Henningsen says no, because no trance technique is described; others still
compare it to shamanic cults. [X3-72, X3-78]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · an unbroken pagan cult from antiquity · the trials show a 16th–17th-century cult, nothing
older [X3-110]
**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · the magic trials were all about women · men were tried too, and the only two handed over for
witchcraft were men [X3-114]

**The evil eye.** Sicilians call it *ittatura* or *malocchiu*. It isn't only Sicilian: belief in it runs across
southern Europe. [X1-210, X1-179]

[**OPEN D3**, single-source rows X1-172 to X1-176: worm-charmers (*ciarmavermi*), who also lift the evil eye; the
amulets (coral, the raised-finger gesture, the *cimaruta*). Shown only with the source named, if Bryan accepts that.]

[**Left out until sourced:** a Sicilian evil-eye charm text and the oil-in-water test (Pitrè 1896, pp. 176–184).]

> **Mulder:** Fairy women who cure the sick.
> **Scully:** The Inquisition's own records say so. That's the strange part.

## 10 · Act 7 · The relic cabinet opens

How many fingerbones can one dead saint have? In Sicily's case, the honest answer is disappointing: we found no saint
with two of the same finger. [fingerbone tally]

What we did find:
- **Lucy's body:** two places claim the whole thing, Venice and Metz. Syracuse, where she was buried, has the empty
  tomb. [X2-47, X2-59, X2-65]
- **Lucy's head:** Venice and Bourges each have a claim, by our own comparison of the sources. [tally T2]
- **Agatha:** spread, not doubled. One tourism list names 31 places outside Catania with a piece of her, and where
  parts can be counted, the numbers add up. Palermo Cathedral holds her arm. [tally T4–T9, X2-41]
- **Louis IX of France,** who died on crusade at Tunis in 1270: his entrails were buried at Monreale. His heart may
  be there too, by one medieval account. [X2-115, **OPEN D5**: X2-116]

Palermo Cathedral's Chapel of the Relics holds 17 reliquaries. No one has counted them for the island. [X2-97, single
source named]

**The critics.** In 1543 John Calvin wrote a *Treatise on Relics*. Collect every piece of the True Cross, he joked, and
you'd fill a good big boat. Every apostle had more than four bodies, and each saint at least two or three.
[X2-137, X2-140, X2-141]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · Calvin counted a dozen heads of John the Baptist · that was his translator [X2-145]

**The rules.** Canon law says: "It is absolutely forbidden to sell sacred relics." The Vatican's 2017 rules ban
trading them too, and forbid cutting up a saint's body without Rome's consent. [X2-121, X2-125, X2-127, X2-128]

**Stamp:** MYTH-CHECK · first-, second- and third-class relics are Church law · not in the current rules [X2-132]

## 11 · Act 8 · To the Americas

Sicily and Latin America both love candles and baroque gold. Is there a real connection? Some, through one empire.
[brief Q7]

**Benedict the Moor.** Born at San Fratello to African parents who had been enslaved, he died at a convent
in Palermo on 4 April 1589. He was beatified in 1743 and made a saint in 1807. [X3-122, X3-123, X3-124, X3-126,
X3-127, X3-128; **OPEN D7**: born free or freed at 18]

His cult traveled with Black Catholic brotherhoods: a confraternity in Lisbon by 1609, and across Brazil and the
Spanish and Portuguese New World from the early 1600s. In Venezuela his feast is danced to the *chimbangueles* drums.
[X3-134, X3-136, X3-158]

**Antonio of Noto,** another Sicilian holy man honored by the same brotherhoods, died at Noto in 1549 or 1550. In Brazil he is still venerated
as Santo Antônio de Categeró. [X3-148, X3-150]

**Rosalia** crossed to New Spain with the Jesuits and the Spanish crown's networks. And in 1555 the first Mexican
Church council made **Saint Joseph** patron of "this new Church". [X3-166, X3-181]

The shared look, the candles and the gold? No scholar we found traces it to one source. Votive candles are a habit of
Catholics everywhere, and the baroque turned up the volume in both places. [X3-203]

**Stamps:**
- MYTH-CHECK · Mexico got the Day of the Dead from Sicily · no transfer found either way [X3-199]
- MYTH-CHECK · Sicilian immigrants carried these saints to Argentina, Brazil and Venezuela · they went earlier,
  through Spain and Portugal [X3-211]
- MYTH-CHECK · St Joseph's tables are a Latin American tradition too · not found [X3-189]

> **Mulder:** Two Sicilian saints in the drums of Venezuela.
> **Scully:** Carried by Black brotherhoods through Lisbon, not by emigrants.

## 12 · Curtain call

The goddesses under the saints are mostly stories from the 1800s. What's real is better: a Church that moved into
Sicily's temples and calendar, and Sicilians who made it their own, with a hermit elected patron before her bones
were checked, a letter from Mary, tables for the poor, gifts from the dead, and healers the Inquisition couldn't quite
bring itself to burn.

> **Mulder:** I still want to believe.
> **Scully:** Good. Now you know what you're believing.

## Glosses (tap to open)
- *sacco*: the white robe worn by Agatha's devotees in Catania [X1-201]
- *festino*: Palermo's July feast of Rosalia [X1-202]
- *acchianata*: the night climb up Monte Pellegrino to Rosalia's cave, 3–4 September [X1-203]
- *vara*: a processional float; Messina's is silver [X1-204]
- *virgineddi*: "little virgins", the guests of honor at a St Joseph table, usually poor children dressed as the Holy
  Family [X1-205]
- *vampata*: the St Joseph's eve bonfire [X1-206]
- *decollati*: "the beheaded", executed people venerated as souls who can help the living [X1-154, X1-160]
- *donne di fuora*: "ladies from outside", fairy women in Sicilian belief, and the healers who said they went out
  with them [X3-53]
- *ittatura* / *malocchiu*: evil-casting / the evil eye [X1-210]
- *scantu*: fright, held to cause worms and other ills [X1-209]
