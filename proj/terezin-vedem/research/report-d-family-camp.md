# Follow-up research: family camp, Rossel, Vrba–Wetzler (report D)

> Pasted by Bryan in chat, Nov 22, 2026 ("Worked for 7m 49s"). Tool: _not yet named_. Links did not survive the paste.

The clearest documented link is between the May deportations and preparations for the June 23 inspection of Terezín. The SS removed prisoners to reduce overcrowding before the visit. Whether the SS also kept the Birkenau family camp ready for a second inspection, and whether the success of the Terezín visit determined the timing of the July killings, remain disputed. The surviving May authorization names an unspecified Jewish labor camp. The later, explicit request to visit Auschwitz and Birkenau was refused.

## A. The Theresienstadt family camp at Auschwitz-Birkenau

### 1. Transport dates and counts

The Terezín Initiative Institute's List of All Transports from Terezín is an undated database table that gives departure dates and deportee counts. Danuta Czech's Auschwitz Chronicle, 1939–1945 gives arrival and registration figures from Auschwitz records. The page numbers below refer to the linked Italian transcription of her chronicle, first published in German in 1989 and in English in 1990.

Miroslav Kárný attributes the December gap of 43 to possible deaths in transit and to some arrivals being in such poor condition that the SS did not register them. The sources cited here do not establish the reason for the full May gap. Kárný's 1994 Chronology of the Terezín Family Camp in Birkenau lists 2,504 in its May 15 entry, while his companion essay's note 19 gives 7,503 for May as a whole, matching the Institute's three rows. His May 15 entry and the Institute's Dz row therefore differ by one. The Auschwitz Museum's exhibition notice rounds the overall total to about 18,000, and its 2004 anniversary notice gives about 20,000 without a transport breakdown that would explain the higher estimate.

There was also a documented exception to the seven mass transports. Dx left on March 20, 1944, with 42 psychiatric patients and three accompanying staff. Terezín Memorial historian Tomáš Fedorovič reconstructs its destination as Auschwitz and identifies nurse Kurt Andermann as reaching BIIb, drawing on testimony from Yakov Tsur and Gottfried Bloch. Bloch recalled that the patients were sent to the gas chambers. Fedorovič gives no verified arrival date or complete count of staff admitted to BIIb. His Story of the Psychiatric Transport Dx (2022), opening paragraphs and notes 11–15, explains why earlier accounts gave Bergen-Belsen as the destination.

### 2. Who came from outside Terezín?

Virtually the entire deportee population came through Terezín. The qualification matters if counting everyone held in the compound. Auschwitz assigned functionary prisoners from elsewhere, including camp elders Arno Böhm and Wilhelm Brachmann. The published transport figures give no complete denominator for such assignments, so a precise percentage is unavailable. "From Terezín" describes the deportation route. The three May transports included Jews previously deported to the ghetto from Germany, Austria and the Netherlands as well as Czech Jews. Sources are the Auschwitz Museum's Oś, no. 75, "Zdarzyło się w KL Auschwitz" (2014), and Kárný's Terezínský rodinný tábor v konečném řešení (1994), note 19.

### 3. The March killing

The SS moved the surviving September deportees earmarked for killing to the adjacent quarantine sector and murdered them in the gas chambers on the night of March 8–9, 1944. Kárný gives 3,792 in Otázky nad 8. březnem 1944 (1999), pp. 11–39. Czech gives 3,791 as her calculation and 3,792 as the figure attributed to prisoner physician Otto Wolken, Chronicle, Jan.–June 1944, March 8–9 entries, p. 37 n. 1. Fedorovič found 3,752 in the vernichtet column of Wolken's surviving quarantine register and argues the higher figure attributed to it was a transcription error. That establishes what the register says and leaves the exact total disputed. The USHMM's 1944: Key Dates, March 8–9 entry, uses almost 3,800. The April escapees' report dated the killing March 7, an early dating error compared with the camp chronology and the later archival studies.

### 4. The July liquidation

The SS selected prisoners for forced labor in late June and early July. It murdered the remaining prisoners on the nights of July 10–11 and 11–12, 1944. Published figures differ in the population being counted and in the estimated camp strength before the killings. The counting basis is explicit in Czech, Kárný and Brod. The Auschwitz Museum's 2004 notice does not give a roster or calculation for its transfer figure.

How many July victims had arrived on Dz, Ea or Eb? I could verify no reliable total for the three May transports. Kárný explicitly identifies many May deportees among those left to be murdered. Brod's note 6 estimates 7,250 May deportees and 3,250 December deportees alive in late June, then subtracts 3,500 transfers overall, without distributing them by transport. On Brod's assumptions alone, the arithmetic permits roughly 3,750–7,000 May victims. That is a bound derived from his aggregate estimates, with extreme assumptions about who was transferred. It is too broad to use as a historical victim count.

### 5. Survivors

Kárný's 1994 essay, note 33, identifies 1,167 people from the seven transports who lived to liberation, broken down as 38 from September, 728 from December and 401 from May. His 1996 essay Zpráva Maurice Rossela o prohlídce Terezína, pp. 188–195, note 10, qualifies 1,167 as the number he was able to establish. The Terezín Initiative Institute's English overview, updated in 2021, gives 1,294 and supplies no matching roster or counting note. The difference cannot be confidently assigned to a particular group or counting rule from these sources. Selection for work was a separate event from survival to liberation.

## B. Why the camp existed and what the Red Cross did

### 6. Purpose and evidence for a possible show camp

Documented facts. The main transports bypassed the usual arrival selection. Families stayed in one sector, with separate sleeping quarters, and the SS permitted censored correspondence. Before the March killing, prisoners were ordered to postdate reassuring postcards. The escapees described the special-treatment and six-month-quarantine entry in the camp records. Kárný also cites the letter in which prisoner Leo Janowitz reported the creation of a purported labor camp at Birkenau near Neu-Berun, and the buried eyewitness account of the March killing by Sonderkommando prisoner Salmen Gradowski. These sources establish deception about the prisoners' fate. They leave the identity of the proposed inspection site open. Sources are Vrba and Wetzler's April 1944 report, §§13–15, and Kárný's 1994 essay, text and notes 8–13 and 20–26.

Historians' interpretations.

- Otto Dov Kulka, Miroslav Kárný and Nili Keren identify BIIb as the likely show site or describe it as having been prepared for that purpose. They point to the May authorization, the postcards and labor-camp cover name, and the unusual camp arrangements. Keren goes furthest in saying that the delegation was scheduled to go on to Birkenau. Her notes cite Kulka and Leni Yahil. The surviving authorization does not name Birkenau, and the sources reviewed here supply no dated BIIb itinerary. See Kulka, Ghetto in an Annihilation Camp (1984), pp. 315–330, especially 329–330, as quoted and checked by Kárný in 1996, and Keren, The Family Camp (1994), pp. 428–429 and notes 1–3.
- Miroslav Kryl proposed Płaszów as the unnamed camp and stressed the lack of direct evidence for Birkenau. Toman Brod, himself a BIIb survivor, found an on-site inspection hard to reconcile with the March killings, the crematoria nearby and the risk that prisoners would tell visitors what had happened. He considered deception through postcards more plausible and left the founding purpose unresolved. Tomáš Fedorovič emphasizes the cover story of deportation for labor and a possible aim of weakening resistance at Terezín. He allows that prisoners might have been held in reserve to show a delegation elsewhere, but reads the surviving correspondence as weighing against an inspection inside the Auschwitz-Birkenau complex. See Kryl, Osud vězňů terezínského ghetta (1999), p. 93, quoted in Fedorovič, Terezín's Propagandist Role and the Terezín Family Camp (2018), pp. 32–37, and Brod's Reflections (1994), opening discussion.

### 7. Formal requests and plans

- Spring 1944. The German Red Cross asked Eichmann's office on March 14 for access to an unnamed Jewish labor camp alongside the promised Terezín visit, and followed up on April 26. In May the Reich Security Main Office conveyed Himmler's permission for the German Red Cross and an ICRC delegate to visit one such camp. Kárný dates the decision to May 17. The surviving letter to the German Red Cross is dated only "May 1944," was initialed on May 18, and bears a note that the decision was telephoned to Roland Marti's ICRC delegation in Berlin on May 19. Kárný reports that Favez and ICRC editor François Bugnion found no record that Berlin passed the labor-camp authorization on to Geneva. The offer was unused. There is no documented pre-July ICRC appointment to inspect BIIb or cancellation of one. See Kárný, Chronology, March–May entries, and his 1994 essay, notes 20 and 23–26, citing Bundesarchiv R 58/89.
- Autumn 1944. The ICRC did formally seek access to Auschwitz and Birkenau by name. Its archive files the attempt under October 18–December 6. A November 23 Foreign Office record says the Swiss envoy repeated the request for an ICRC representative. On November 28, Eberhard von Thadden told State Secretary Adolf Steengracht that the Reich Security Main Office ruled out access. Steengracht set the matter aside. See ICRC Archives, inventory B G 59, p. 41, file 12-367.02, and von Thadden's Letter to Steengracht, 1944, PAAA R 99 337, reproduced as document 155, p. 512, in Das KZ Auschwitz 1942–1945 und die Zeit der Todesmärsche 1944/45 (2018).
- Other parties. Slovak prime minister Vojtech Tuka sought a commission visit to a Jewish camp in German-occupied Poland. In February 1944 Eichmann held out Terezín as an option and said access to camps in the General Government was difficult. That correspondence does not identify BIIb as the destination. Separately, a German Reich Ministry of Justice delegation did visit Auschwitz on June 28, 1944. Its protocol mentions passing a crematorium and describes guides limiting what the delegation saw. See Fedorovič (2018), pp. 34–36 and notes 29–38, and Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum, International Red Cross Visit, undated, final paragraph.

### 8. Rossel at Auschwitz

September 27, 1944 is the date given by the ICRC and Auschwitz Museum for Rossel's stop at the Auschwitz I commandant's office. September 29 is the date of his written report. Favez's older account, followed by Kárný in one essay, dates the visit itself to September 29. Rossel called his interlocutor the commandant. Richard Baer held that post, but the documentary editors and the museum leave open whether Baer or a staff officer received him. Rossel saw guarded work parties on the road and could see camp buildings from outside the prisoner enclosure. He accepted SS assurances about parcel distribution as credible, while acknowledging he had no proof. His report also records a British POW representative at Teschen telling him about a rumored gas chamber. Rossel had no access to the prisoner compound, Birkenau or the killing facilities. The sources identify him as the sole documented ICRC delegate to make such a stop at Auschwitz in 1944. See Rossel, Visite au K.Z. Auschwitz (1944), pp. 1–3, ICRC file B G 59 12-367.01, reprinted as document 143, pp. 478–480, and Piotr Setkiewicz, Setting the Record Straight (2022), pp. 250–251.

### 9. Did the June visit or Rossel's report determine the timing?

Kulka, Kárný and Keren argue that the staged inspection and the claim that Terezín was a final destination made the proposed Birkenau exhibit dispensable. A crucial qualification comes from Kárný himself. In 1996 he showed that the German Red Cross official von Heydekampf read the report on September 10 and briefed SS officer Ernst Möhs on September 11. The letter Kulka used therefore records a briefing after the July killings. Kárný retained his interpretation, citing Heydekampf's June 27 assessment that the visitors had reacted favorably, and Rossel's reassuring July 1 letter to von Thadden. Those documents support an inference that German officials already considered the visit a success. They do not establish an order tying it to the liquidation. Margalith Shlain likewise interprets the SS as waiting for the visit and then disposing of a camp it no longer needed.

Danuta Czech suggested that publicity about the threatened June 20 killing might have delayed it. Gabriel Milland, Some Faint Hope and Courage (1998), pp. 217–218, takes that possibility seriously and points to the BBC broadcasts and German monitoring records. Kárný, The Vrba and Wetzler Report (1994), pp. 558–559, says the effect of the publicity cannot be documented and questions whether a June killing of the whole December cohort had actually been ordered. Brod gives greater weight to German demand for forced labor as the explanation for the July selections. Fedorovič disputes the premise that BIIb was the intended inspection site. Farré and Schubert, L'illusion de l'objectif (2009), pp. 72–75, document the staging, reporting and circulation of photographs. Those pages establish no causal link to the July decision.

## C. The Vrba–Wetzler report and the use of Rossel's report

### 10. Who received the report, when it became public, and its effect

The April Vrba–Wetzler report circulated in several versions and summaries. The collection later known as the Auschwitz Protocols also included the separate testimony of Jerzy Tabeau and the later report by Arnošt Rosin and Czesław Mordowicz. Receipt of a warning, receipt of a complete text and publication are different milestones.

Yad Vashem records a shortened version reaching a Swiss representative of the Orthodox rescue committee in mid-May. See Szabó, The Auschwitz Reports: Who Got Them, and When? Effect on Hungary. The reports and press coverage helped build international pressure on Regent Miklós Horthy. Szabó dates appeals from Pius XII to June 25, Roosevelt to June 26 and Gustav V of Sweden to June 30. The Budapest Holocaust exhibition's The Suspension of the Deportations dates Horthy's communication of his decision to Prime Minister Sztójay to July 6. USHMM's The Holocaust in Hungary (updated 2025), section on the halt, dates his order to July 7, with the main deportation wave continuing through July 9. USHMM also identifies Germany's worsening military position and pressure from Horthy's inner circle. The report contributed to the halt that temporarily spared most of Budapest's Jews. The evidence supports several causes for that decision.

### 11. Did the report warn about the family camp?

Yes. Sections 13–15 of Vrba and Wetzler's Report (late April 1944) describe the September deportees' special treatment, the March gassing, forced postdating of mail and the December prisoners' preparations to resist. The report says the December group's six-month quarantine would expire on June 20 and warns that the March fate would recur. That was a contemporaneous prediction based on the first group's fate. The text predates the May transports and could give no account of the actual July liquidation. The later BBC warning expressed the threat as a German order, a stronger claim than the original report could establish.

### 12. How German officials used Rossel's report

- Foreign Office and Swedish diplomats. Farré and Schubert, L'illusion de l'objectif (2009), p. 74 and notes 50–52, date typing to June 26, dispatch to ICRC headquarters to June 27, and describe a report and photographs being sent to von Thadden on July 1. Kárný's transcription of the July 1 covering letter explicitly identifies photographs and promises a reassuring report. It securely establishes the photo shipment and leaves the report enclosure less certain. Rossel also said he was sending sets to Rolf Günther in Eichmann's office. On July 26 von Thadden thanked Rossel and said he would use the photographs against foreign allegations about Terezín. An archival annotation records a set going to the Swedish legation in Berlin. Von Thadden invoked the inspection in discussions with Swedish diplomat Göran von Otter concerning prisoners with Swedish citizenship. See Kárný's Zpráva Maurice Rossela (1996), pp. 188–195, note 15, films 14173 and 15463.
- Foreign press and SS officials. Reich deputy press chief Helmut Sündermann cited the favorable inspection to foreign journalists to rebut reports of mistreatment of Jews. Kárný's 1994 Chronology and an October 7, 1944 Foreign Office memorandum place the press conference on July 21. Kárný's undated English text The Genocide of the Czech Jews, section on the Red Cross visit, gives July 19. The October memorandum independently backs July 21. In September, Heydekampf read the report, briefed Möhs, and prepared an extract. Niehaus requested a copy for Günther. The extract addressed claims that prisoners had been transferred onward from Terezín and stressed the persuasive value of the photographs. See Kárný (1996), notes 16–18, Yad Vashem Archives JM 1700/4 and Bundesarchiv R 58/89, pp. 23–24.
- The specific claim about Vrba–Wetzler. Rossel's portrayal of Terezín as a final destination directly contradicted the escapees' account of deportation to Birkenau. Kárný says that contradiction could cast doubt on their wider disclosures. The German records cited above document rebuttals of reports about conditions and onward deportations. They do not identify the Vrba–Wetzler report by name as the target. There is also direct evidence of an Allied official reading the two against each other in the opposite direction. On October 26, War Refugee Board representative Roswell McClelland forwarded Rossel's report confidentially to John Pehle and cited the Auschwitz-Birkenau report to challenge Rossel's final-destination claim. See Kárný, The Vrba and Wetzler Report (1994), pp. 559–560, and McClelland, Letter to John W. Pehle (1944), pp. 1–3, JDC Archives. "Rossel's report buried Vrba–Wetzler" overstates the evidence.
