# **Canine Evolutionary and Historical Trajectories: A Comprehensive Archaeozoological and Genetic Analysis of the Chihuahua and Pomeranian**

The evolutionary and cultural histories of domestic dogs offer a profound lens into the societies that bred, utilized, and transported them. Among the myriad of modern canine breeds, the Chihuahua and the Pomeranian represent two radically different trajectories of human-animal interaction. The Chihuahua's lineage is deeply entangled with the pre-Columbian civilizations of Mesoamerica, involving millennia of genetic isolation, utilitarian consumption, and profound spiritual significance, followed by near-total genomic replacement following European colonization. Conversely, the Pomeranian represents a lineage of Arctic working dogs that underwent aggressive morphological miniaturization driven entirely by the aesthetic preferences of the British aristocracy during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. This report provides an exhaustive, multi-disciplinary analysis of these two breeds, synthesizing paleogenomics, zooarchaeology, isotopic chemistry, and ethnohistoric documentation to separate empirical reality from enduring breeder folklore.

## **1\. Chihuahua Origins: Pre-Columbian Ancestry, Genetics, and Archaeological Evidence**

Understanding the precise origins of the modern Chihuahua requires delineating the deep archaeological record of Mesoamerica from the highly complex, and sometimes conflicting, findings of modern paleogenomics. The archaeological evidence unequivocally establishes the presence of small, morphologically distinct canids in pre-Columbian Mexico, while genetic studies reveal the extent to which European colonization erased and overwrote the native canine genome.

### **Archaeological and Artistic Evidence of Pre-Columbian Origins**

Zooarchaeological excavations and art historical analyses provide physical confirmation that the ancestors of the Chihuahua were bred and maintained by the indigenous civilizations of Mesoamerica long before European contact. The most prominent morphological ancestor associated with the Chihuahua is the Techichi, an extinct breed of small, sturdy, short-limbed dog originally bred by the Toltec civilization beginning in the 9th century C.E., as detailed in the encyclopedia entry "Techichi" (Wikipedia, 2022, https\://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techichi)1.  
The physical appearance of these early Mesoamerican dogs is heavily corroborated by pre-Columbian art and effigy ceramics. The Barakat Gallery, in its analysis "Colima Vessel in the Form of a Dog, 300 BCE \- 300 CE" (Barakat Gallery, https\://barakatgallery.eu/artworks/categories/980/72411-colima-vessel-in-the-form-of-a-dog-300-bce-300-ce/), documents that the animal most frequently depicted in Colima art is the Techichi or its hairless counterpart, the Escuincle2. These effigy pots and wheeled toys—dating from 100 BCE to 300 CE—depict a small, portly dog with pronounced physical wrinkling, a short stature, and rounded, alert, upright ears that bear a striking resemblance to the cranial morphology of the modern Chihuahua1.  
Furthermore, osteological remains recovered from the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlán (modern Mexico City) confirm the continued presence of these dogs into the Postclassic period. According to the article "Unique Aztec Dog Burials" by Mark Stuart (Aztlan Development, October 2, https\://aztlandevelopment.com/unique-aztec-dog-burials/), archaeologists discovered a unique mass burial containing the remains of twelve dogs6. Morphological analysis of these 15th-to-16th-century skeletal remains confirmed they were native Mexican breeds, specifically identifying the osteological markers of the Techichi and the Xoloitzcuintli6.

### **The Paleogenomic Debate: Conflicting DNA Methodologies**

While archaeology proves the existence of the Techichi, determining how much of this pre-Columbian ancestry survives in the modern Chihuahua is a subject of intense scientific debate. This disagreement within the academic community is entirely methodological, stemming from the differences between mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) tracking and nuclear (autosomal) DNA sequencing.  
One scientific camp asserts that the Chihuahua maintains significant, unbroken pre-Columbian lineage. In the peer-reviewed study "Pre-Columbian origins of Native American dog breeds, with only limited replacement by European dogs, confirmed by mtDNA analysis" (Proceedings of the Royal Society B, Vol. 280, No. 1766, 2013, https\://royalsocietypublishing.org/rspb/article/280/1766/20131142/), lead author Barbara van Asch and her colleagues analyzed the maternal lineages of several American breeds9. The van Asch study concluded that pre-Columbian maternal lineages survived the European conquest intact. Specifically, they found that the modern Chihuahua, alongside the Xoloitzcuintli and Arctic dogs, shares a distinct mitochondrial haplotype with ancient, pre-contact Mexican canine samples9.  
However, a conflicting version of the Chihuahua's genetic history is presented by researchers analyzing the whole nuclear genome. In the 2018 study published in *Science* by Ní Leathlobhair et al., the authors reported that pre-contact American dogs left a practically nonexistent genetic legacy, arguing that the ancient lineages were almost completely replaced by European dogs after 1492 (referenced in the summary "Ancient American Dogs," Dogs Atlas, https\://dogsatlas.com/families/ancient-american-dogs)12.  
The most definitive and exhaustive synthesis of this genomic admixture is provided by Anders Bergström et al. in the 2020 peer-reviewed study "Origins and genetic legacy of prehistoric dogs" (*Science*, Vol. 370, Issue 6516, pp. 557-564, 2020, https\://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33122379/)13. Bergström and his team sequenced 27 ancient dog genomes dating back 10,900 years13. Utilizing principal component analysis (PCA) and exhaustive *f4*\-statistics to test 135,285 admixture graph models, the researchers traced the global divergence of canids13. The Bergström study concluded that, while indigenous American dogs formed a distinct genetic clade separated from Eurasian dogs for thousands of years, modern descendant breeds are overwhelmingly European in their nuclear DNA. According to Bergström's admixture models, only a minute fraction of pre-colonial autosomal ancestry survives today: approximately 4% in the Mexican Chihuahua, and roughly 3% in the Xoloitzcuintli16.  
**Why the Sources Disagree:** The discrepancy between van Asch (2013) and Bergström (2020) does not represent a failure of science, but rather highlights the mechanics of genetic introgression. Mitochondrial DNA (studied by van Asch) is passed down exclusively through the maternal line without recombination. If a single indigenous Techichi female was bred with a European male, and her female descendants continued to breed with European dogs for five centuries, the modern descendant would retain 100% pre-Columbian mtDNA. However, their nuclear DNA (studied by Bergström and Ní Leathlobhair) would reflect every generation of European admixture, resulting in the 4% indigenous nuclear retention observed today12. Thus, both studies are factually correct within the parameters of their specific methodologies: the Chihuahua possesses a surviving pre-Columbian maternal line, but its genome has been virtually swamped by European introgression.

### **Legend versus Breeder Lore**

In the context of the Chihuahua's origins, it is critical to separate archaeological reality from enduring breeder lore. A pervasive legend claims that the Toltec Techichi was entirely mute and physically incapable of barking, as noted in the encyclopedia entry "Techichi" (Wikipedia, 2022, https\://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techichi)1. There is no anatomical or zooarchaeological evidence to suggest that the Techichi lacked vocal cords or the capacity for vocalization. This legend likely stems from early European misinterpretations of unfamiliar indigenous animal behaviors—such as dogs bred for specific temperaments or suppressed vocalization for hunting or ceremonial purposes—rather than a biological inability to bark. Furthermore, modern breeder lore frequently positions the Chihuahua as a direct, untainted descendant of the Techichi. As the nuclear genomic data provided by Bergström (2020) explicitly proves, this is mathematically false; the modern Chihuahua is a highly admixed European-indigenous hybrid, not a pure genetic continuation of the pre-Columbian dog.

## **2\. Small Dogs as Food in Pre-Columbian Mexico**

The proposition that small dogs were raised, traded, and consumed as a primary protein source in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica is an incontrovertible historical reality. This practice is documented through a robust triangulation of early colonial ethnohistoric accounts and modern isotopic chemistry derived from zooarchaeological remains.

### **Ethnohistoric Documentation by Spanish Chroniclers**

Sixteenth-century Spanish texts provide the most direct observational data regarding the status of dogs as a managed food source. In his "Second Letter" to Emperor Charles V, dated October 30, 1520, Spanish conquistador Hernán Cortés provided a meticulous description of the massive urban market at Tlatelolco, adjacent to Tenochtitlán (translated in *Letters From Mexico* by Anthony Pagden, Yale University Press, https\://www\.scribd.com/document/652548611/Letters-From-Mexico-by-Hernan-Cortes-Anthony-Pagden-Dr-Anthony-Pagden-Z-lib-org)18. Within this letter, Cortés explicitly documented that the Aztecs actively raised, castrated, and sold little dogs specifically for human consumption18.  
Further ethnographic detail is provided by the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún in the *Florentine Codex* (Book 11, "Earthly Things"), a seminal 16th-century compendium of Aztec culture and natural history translated by Charles E. Dibble and Arthur J. O. Anderson (University of Utah Press, https\://vdoc.pub/documents/animals-and-their-relation-to-gods-humans-and-things-in-the-ancient-world-5pd8c0r8q950)23. Sahagún delineated the specific typologies of dogs utilized by the indigenous populations, explicitly naming the *tlalchichi* (a coated, short-limbed dog widely considered synonymous with the Techichi), alongside the *xoloitzcuintli* (hairless), the *tepezcuintli*, and the *itzcuitepotzontli*23. The text confirms these dogs were a vital part of the culinary and sacrificial economy25.  
**Was the Techichi itself eaten?** Yes. Historical accounts confirm that the coated Techichi was consumed alongside the hairless breeds. In 1578, the Spanish explorer and physician Francisco Hernandez formally documented the existence of the Techichi. He noted that the native populations ate these dogs as commonly as they ate rabbits. Furthermore, European explorers, frequently facing starvation during their expeditions, rapidly adopted this indigenous practice. Hernandez estimated that Spanish explorers ultimately consumed as many as 100,000 Techichis, a staggering predation rate that served as a primary catalyst for the breed's eventual extinction by the 19th century (Wikipedia, "Techichi", https\://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techichi)1.

### **Zooarchaeological Evidence and Stable Isotope Analysis**

The historical accounts of canine consumption are independently verified by advanced stable isotope analysis of pre-Columbian animal remains. In the peer-reviewed study "Stable Isotopes and Zooarchaeology at Teotihuacan, Mexico Reveal Earliest Evidence of Wild Carnivore Management in Mesoamerica" (PLOS One, 2015, doi: 10.1371/journal.pone.0135635, https\://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0135635), researchers Nawa Sugiyama, Andrew D. Somerville, and Margaret J. Schoeninger analyzed animal remains recovered from dedicatory contexts at the Moon Pyramid in Teotihuacan, dating to the Classic Period (A.D. 1–550)27.  
This methodology relies on the fact that stable carbon isotope ratios (![][image1]) in bone collagen and apatite reflect the photosynthetic pathway of the plants consumed by the animal during its life (specifically distinguishing between C3, C4, or Crassulacean acid metabolism \[CAM\] plants). The natural, wild biome of Central Mexico is dominated by C3 plants. However, Sugiyama's team discovered that the domestic dogs analyzed at Teotihuacan displayed anomalously high ![][image1] values, heavily indicative of C4 plant consumption27. Because the primary C4 plant in the region is human-cultivated maize (*Zea mays*), these high isotopic signatures prove that the dogs were not wild scavengers; they were intentionally provisioned and fattened on a diet dominated by agricultural maize, explicitly linking them to specialized food production29.  
This system of intensive canine husbandry was widespread. In the study "Isotopic Evidence for Maya Patterns of Deer and Dog Use at Preclassic Colha" (Journal of Archaeological Science, Vol. 28, 2001, pp. 89-107, https\://christinawarinner.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/02/DietomeUKAS2009-6-4.pdf), Christine D. White, Mary E. D. Pohl, H. P. Schwarcz, and F. J. Longstaffe demonstrated identical isotopic patterns in the Maya region32. Through carbon and nitrogen (![][image2]) isotopic modeling, White et al. concluded that Maya dogs were regularly and intentionally fed human-grown agricultural products, confirming their status as a managed, domesticated food source long before the Aztec empire or the arrival of the Spanish32.

## **3\. Small Dogs as Bed or Body Warmers, Healing, and Religious Roles**

The functional roles of the Chihuahua's ancestors extended beyond sustenance, penetrating the spiritual, ritualistic, and domestic spheres of Mesoamerican peoples. However, analyzing these roles requires a strict boundary between documented ethnohistoric/archaeological evidence and pervasive modern folklore.

### **Documented Religious Roles and Psychopomps**

There is extensive, incontrovertible documentation that small Mesoamerican dogs served a central role in pre-Columbian religion, specifically functioning as psychopomps—spiritual entities responsible for guiding the souls of the deceased through the treacherous trials of the afterlife.  
According to Aztec cosmology recorded in the 16th-century *Florentine Codex* by Bernardino de Sahagún, souls of the dead were required to undertake a multi-year journey to Mictlan, the nine-leveled underworld (Zoë Saadia, "Who Goes to Mictlan?", https\://zoesaadia.com/historia-en-el-calmecac/who-goes-to-mictlan/)36. To successfully complete this journey, the deceased eventually had to cross a wide, turbulent subterranean river known as the Chignahuapan, or Apanohuaya, located in the ninth region of Mictlan36. Sahagún documented the religious belief that humans could not cross this river alone; only a dog could guide the human soul safely across the water36. Consequently, native dogs—particularly the coated Techichi and the reddish, hairless Xoloitzcuintli—were frequently ritually sacrificed and buried alongside their human owners to serve as eternal guides1. The mass burial of twelve Aztec dogs discovered in Tenochtitlán, unassociated with human remains but located in a highly ritualized precinct, further underscores the profound ceremonial and mortuary function these canids held within the state religion6.

### **Modern Folklore versus Historical Documentation: Bed Warmers and Healers**

In contrast to their heavily documented role as psychopomps, the concept of the Techichi or modern Chihuahua functioning specifically as a therapeutic "bed warmer" or magical healer requires critical scientific nuance.  
From a broad, paleoanthropological perspective, researchers theorize that the earliest domesticated dogs across the Pleistocene globe may have generally served as sources of warmth for migrating humans in harsh climates. In "New Evidence of the Earliest Domestic Dogs in the Americas" (American Antiquity, 2018, https\://www\.researchgate.net/publication/329936071\_NEW\_EVIDENCE\_OF\_THE\_EARLIEST\_DOMESTIC\_DOGS\_IN\_THE\_AMERICAS), researchers Darcy F. Morey and Michael D. Wiant postulate that early migrating Pleistocene populations arriving in the Americas "may have used dogs to transport goods and people, work as hunting aids, serve as bed-warmers, warn people of potential danger..."39.  
However, taking this generalized, 10,000-year-old Pleistocene survival theory and applying it specifically to the Toltec Techichi or the modern Chihuahua is largely an artifact of modern breeder folklore. There are absolutely no primary colonial texts—neither from Cortés, Sahagún, nor Hernandez—that document the Techichi being explicitly bred, marketed, or utilized as literal bed warmers.  
Similarly, pervasive modern legends claim that Chihuahuas were kept by the Aztecs to cure human respiratory ailments, such as asthma, by supposedly drawing the illness out of the human body and absorbing it into the dog. This is unequivocally a post-colonial myth and modern folklore. There is zero pre-Columbian archaeological, isotopic, or ethnohistoric documentation supporting the use of Techichis as asthma curatives or biological illness sponges. The documented realities of these dogs in Mesoamerica were companion, food source, and spiritual psychopomp1.

## **4\. Modern Chihuahua Breed Naming and Recognition**

The transition of the indigenous Mexican dog from a culturally localized, heavily decimated remnant population into a globally recognized, standardized breed occurred primarily during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, driven entirely by American canine enthusiasts.  
Following the near-extinction of the original Techichi breed due to mass Spanish consumption (estimated at 100,000 individuals) and centuries of unchecked genetic dilution via the importation of European dogs, the indigenous canine lost its distinct historical identity1. It was not until the mid-1800s that American travelers, tourists, and early dog fanciers began to take note of the small, admixed descendants of these dogs living in border regions. Americans primarily encountered these diminutive canines in the northern Mexican state of Chihuahua (Wikipedia, "Techichi", https\://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techichi)1. As tourists purchased these animals and transported them back to the United States, the dogs lacked a formal taxonomic designation and thus colloquially adopted the geographical name of the region, becoming known universally as the Chihuahua1.  
The formal standardization, codification, and breeding of the modern Chihuahua is fundamentally an American achievement. The American Kennel Club (AKC) officially recognized and registered the Chihuahua breed in the year 1904 (AKC Chihuahua History, https\://www\.akc.org/expert-advice/dog-breeds/chihuahua-history/)40. The very first Chihuahua to be entered into the American Kennel Club stud book was a dog named "Midget" (AKC registration number 2291), who was owned by H. Raynor, a resident of El Paso, Texas21. Shortly thereafter, the breed secured its first national champion, a dog named "Beppie" (registration 85317), owned by Mrs. L. A. McLean of New Jersey41.  
To further develop the genetic lines and community surrounding the breed, the Chihuahua Club of America was founded in 1923 by figures including Mrs. Henrietta Proctor Donnell and Ida H. Garrett42. Early breeders, such as Anna B. Vinyard of La Oro Kennel and Mrs. Harry S. Peaster, heavily influenced the modern physical standard45. Today, the AKC breed standard demands a dog weighing no more than 6 pounds, featuring a rounded "apple-dome" skull (though the older "deer-head" type persists in pet populations), and recognizes both smooth and long coats, completely separate from the original, slightly larger, exclusively short-coated Techichi of the Toltecs22.

## **5\. Pomeranian Origins: Ancestry, Miniaturization, and Royal Patronage**

While the Chihuahua traces its highly diluted roots to the agricultural and spiritual practices of indigenous Mesoamerica, the Pomeranian is a direct descendant of the robust, cold-weather working dogs of the Arctic and Europe. The breed represents a profound example of extreme morphological miniaturization, driven strictly by the aesthetic and status-oriented selections of the British royal family.

### **Spitz Ancestry and Early Typology**

Taxonomically, the Pomeranian is classified within the Spitz family of dogs. Its direct ancestors were the large, heavy-coated working, sled, and sheepdogs of the Arctic regions and Central Europe, specifically the German Spitz (Preussen Spitz, "The Toy Spitz/Pomeranian", https\://www\.preussenspitz.de/the-german-spitz/the-toy-spitz-pomeranian-1/)47. Historically, the breed took its name from the region of Pomerania (an area spanning parts of modern-day northern Germany and Poland along the Baltic Sea), which is where these larger Spitz-type dogs were first selectively bred into a smaller, standardized companion form.  
The historical morphology of the early Pomeranian was vastly different from the modern 3-to-7-pound toy breed. Up until the late 1880s, the standard acceptable weight for a Pomeranian was approximately 20 pounds (9 kilograms), a size commensurate with a medium-sized herding or working dog rather than a lapdog (Hepper, "Orange Pomeranian", https\://articles.hepper.com/orange-pomeranian/)49. This larger size is heavily documented in early natural history texts. As depicted in the seminal 1790 publication *A General History of Quadrupeds* by the English engraver and naturalist Thomas Bewick (https\://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/Ancillary/BeagleLibrary/1793\_Pennant\_A897.1.html), the "Pomeranian Dog" was recognized as a distinct, larger canine type with wolf-like characteristics well before the Victorian era48.

### **Royal Patronage and Extreme Miniaturization**

The radical reduction of the Pomeranian to its modern size is directly attributed to the patronage of the British monarchy, functioning as a primary catalyst for selective dwarfism in canine breeding. The initial introduction of the breed to the British aristocracy occurred in the year 1761, when Queen Charlotte, the Queen-consort of King George III, brought a pair of Pomeranians with her from her native Germany to England (Kaihu Valley History, https\://kaihuvalleyhistory.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/1-from-the-sea-we-came.pdf)53. These two dogs, named "Phoebe" and "Mercury," were relatively large compared to modern standards. Their existence and physical stature were immortalized by the renowned English portrait artist Thomas Gainsborough, whose paintings confirm the breed's originally robust, 20-pound Spitz morphology53.  
However, the aggressive miniaturization of the breed was catalyzed over a century later by Queen Charlotte's granddaughter, Queen Victoria. In 1888, during a visit to Florence, Italy, Queen Victoria discovered and acquired a remarkably small red Pomeranian named "Marco"49. Marco was reported to weigh a mere 12 pounds (5.4 kg), representing a dramatic physical reduction from the standard of the era49.  
Queen Victoria possessed immense cultural influence across Europe. She actively participated in conformation dog shows with Marco, instantly igniting a massive public demand among the Victorian aristocracy for progressively smaller Pomeranians. To emulate the Queen's favored dog, European and British breeders aggressively selected for smaller stature over the following decades, rapidly shrinking the breed down from 20 pounds to the modern toy dimensions of under 7 pounds.

## **6\. Kennel Club Recognition of the Pomeranian**

The formal, bureaucratic recognition of the Pomeranian by international kennel clubs tracks alongside its surge in popularity driven by Victorian aristocratic trends.  
In the United Kingdom, The Kennel Club (UK) was originally founded in 1873, and British dog shows began evaluating Pomeranians during this era heavily influenced by royal breeding programs (Dogs Atlas, "Ancient American Dogs", https\://dogsatlas.com/families/ancient-american-dogs)12.  
In the United States, the American Kennel Club (AKC) saw its first Pomeranian registration just as Queen Victoria was popularizing the miniaturized breed in Europe. In 1888, a Pomeranian named "Dick" became the very first of his breed to be entered into the AKC stud book (DogTime, "Pomeranian Cockapoo", https\://dogtime.com/dog-breeds/pomeranian-cockapoo)57. The breed had made early appearances in American dog shows, notably being recognized at a New York show as early as 1883, as documented in James Watson's 1906 text *The Dog Book* (http\://archive.org/download/dogbookpopularhi02wats/dogbookpopularhi02wats.pdf)61. Despite Dick's early entry into the stud book in 1888, the American Kennel Club did not officially grant full breed recognition to the Pomeranian until the year 190059. The American Pomeranian Club was formally organized shortly thereafter, in 1901, cementing the breed's standardized rules and toy classification in the United States61.
