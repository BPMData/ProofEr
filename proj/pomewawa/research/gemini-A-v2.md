# **Research Report on the Origins, Historical Roles, and Pedigree Recognition of the Chihuahua and Pomeranian Breeds**

## **Question 1: Chihuahua Origins: Pre-Columbian Ancestry, Archaeology, and DNA**

### **Genetic Evidence: Maternal Continuity versus Nuclear Genome Replacement**

The evolutionary history of the modern Chihuahua (*Canis lupus familiaris*) has been clarified through ancient DNA (aDNA) analysis, whole-genome sequencing, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) phylogenetics1. Genetic research demonstrates a clear divergence between maternal lineage persistence and overall nuclear genome inheritance1.  
Mitochondrial DNA studies, which trace an unbroken maternal line, provided the initial molecular evidence linking modern Mexican dogs to pre-Columbian canids1. A study by Dr. Barbara van Asch and colleagues (2013), published in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences* (Vol. 280, No. 1766, doi:10.1098/rspb.2013.1142), analyzed a global dataset of modern and ancient canid mitogenomes4. Van Asch et al. identified specific, rare mitochondrial haplotypes—namely A185 (subclade a4) and C16—shared exclusively between modern Chihuahuas and pre-Columbian archaeological remains recovered in Mexico1. Their analysis revealed geographic continuity over millennia, confirming that modern Chihuahuas retain direct maternal lineages from ancient Mexican dogs3.  
Subsequent whole-genome and nuclear DNA analyses refined this narrative by demonstrating that maternal continuity exists alongside near-total nuclear genome replacement1. Dr. Máire Ní Leathlobhair and colleagues (2018), writing in *Science* (Vol. 361, No. 6397, pp. 81–85, doi:10.1126/science.aao4776), sequenced 71 mitochondrial and 7 nuclear genomes from ancient North American and Siberian dogs spanning 9,000 years2. Their findings established that Pre-Contact Dogs (PCDs) formed a monophyletic clade that entered the Americas alongside human populations from Siberia approximately 14,600 years ago (95% Highest Posterior Density \[HPD\]: 12,965–16,484 cal BP)2. Ní Leathlobhair et al. revealed that following European contact after 1492, indigenous American dog populations were almost completely replaced by European dog breeds, leaving a minimal nuclear legacy in extant American breeds1.  
This genomic legacy was further quantified by Dr. Anders Bergström and colleagues (2020) in *Science* (Vol. 370, No. 6516, pp. 557–564, doi:10.1126/science.aba9572)4. Using high-coverage global ancient and modern canine nuclear genomes, Bergström et al. modeled ancestry contributions and determined that modern Chihuahuas carry approximately 4% pre-Columbian indigenous nuclear ancestry, with the remaining \~96% of their nuclear genome deriving from European dog breeds introduced over the past five centuries11.  
The discrepancy between the conclusions of van Asch et al. (2013) and Ní Leathlobhair et al. (2018) / Bergström et al. (2020) is methodological rather than contradictory1. Mitochondrial DNA tracks only female-line inheritance; thus, a modern Chihuahua can carry an unbroken pre-Columbian maternal haplotype while undergoing extensive backcrossing with male European dogs over centuries, resulting in an overwhelmingly European nuclear genome1.

### **Archaeological and Material Evidence**

The material record of Mesoamerica provides extensive evidence of small indigenous canids across multiple cultural horizons13. Zooarchaeological remains and iconographic representations indicate that small dogs were integrated into elite, ceremonial, and domestic spheres long before European contact13.  
Ceramic effigies provide visual evidence of canine morphology in pre-Columbian Mexico13. In Tres Zapotes, Veracruz, ceramic canine sculptures—including small wheeled dog figurines—date from the Late Formative to Classic periods (c. 100 BCE–600 CE)13. In West Mexico, shaft tomb cultures of Colima, Nayarit, and Jalisco (c. 100 BCE–300 CE) produced thousands of hollow ceramic dog effigies13. Zooarchaeologists, including Dr. Raúl Valadez Azúa and colleagues (2000, 2009, 2013\) at the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (UNAM), have categorized Mesoamerican canid remains into distinct morphological types14:

* The *Techichi*: A small to medium-sized, fully coated dog with long hair and erect ears, associated with the Toltec civilization of Tula (c. 900–1150 CE) and later the Aztecs14.  
* The *Tlalchichi*: A small, coated dog exhibiting achondroplasia (dwarfed, short limbs), documented in West Mexican ceramic effigies and Postclassic central Mexican codices14.  
* The *Xoloitzcuintli*: A hairless, naturally occurring mutant canid variety14.

Zooarchaeological excavations at Teotihuacan (State of Mexico, c. 100–650 CE), the Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan (1325–1521 CE), and lowland Maya sites (e.g., Ceibal, Preclassic to Classic periods) have yielded physical bones confirming the existence of small-statured dogs16. Skeletal morphometrics demonstrate that pre-Columbian small dogs were generally larger and heavier than modern toy Chihuahuas, typically weighing between 5 and 15 pounds (2.3–6.8 kg)16.

### **Legend, Ethno-historical Lore, and Breeder Hypotheses**

Numerous popular assertions regarding the origin of the Chihuahua derive from 19th- and 20th-century breeder literature rather than empirical history or genetics17.  
A central narrative in breeder lore asserts that the Techichi was kept by the Toltecs as a sacred pet at Tula (Hidalgo) starting in the 9th century CE, and was later adopted by the Aztecs after their migration into the Valley of Mexico in the 13th and 14th centuries17. While archaeological effigies and canid bones exist from Tula and Tenochtitlan, the direct, unhybridized linear descent of the modern Chihuahua from the Techichi is an oversimplification1. Breeder literature from the early 20th century, such as works by Ida H. Garrett (1933), asserted that the modern Chihuahua was created by crossing the native Techichi with Chinese Hairless or European toy dogs brought via Spanish galleons across the Pacific19. Genomic sequencing has disproven the theory of Asian toy dog admixture, confirming that European companion breeds account for the vast majority of non-indigenous Chihuahua nuclear DNA1.

## **Question 2: Small Dogs as Food in Pre-Columbian Mesoamerica**

### **Ethnohistorical Accounts from Colonial Chroniclers**

Primary historical documents written by early Spanish chroniclers establish that small indigenous dogs were systematically bred, fattened, and consumed across Mesoamerica prior to and during the Spanish conquest4.  
In his *Second Letter to Emperor Charles V*, written in October 1520 from Mexico, the conquistador Hernán Cortés described the market of Tlatelolco in Tenochtitlan4. Cortés wrote that small dogs were raised, castrated, and sold specifically for food in the market stalls4.  
In the *General History of the Things of New Spain* (the *Florentine Codex*), compiled between 1545 and 1590 by Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún with indigenous Nahua informants, Book XI detailed the domestic dog varieties of the Aztecs13. Sahagún documented that the short-limbed, coated *Tlalchichi* and small coated dogs were specifically raised for culinary purposes, noting that they were fed maize dough (*nixtamal*) and slaughtered for elite feasts or market exchange13.  
The 16th-century Tlaxcalan chronicler Diego Muñoz Camargo (*Historia de Tlaxcala*, c. 1592\) similarly documented the slaughter and ritual consumption of dogs, including the hairless *Xoloitzcuintli*, during agricultural rites and periods of drought to implore divine intervention14.

### **Zooarchaeological and Isotopic Evidence**

Archaeological excavations across Mexico and Central America confirm the consumption of small dogs through direct physical markers, including butchery cut marks on long bones, disarticulation patterning, burning, and bone breakage for marrow extraction14. At Teotihuacan, zooarchaeological analysis of canid remains from civic-ceremonial structures (e.g., Templo Redondo Q-152 and Hall H-15) by Dr. Raúl Valadez Azúa et al. (2006) revealed high concentrations of front limb bones (humeri, radii, ulnae) and cranial fragments displaying clear cut marks consistent with butchery for human consumption16.  
Stable isotope geochemistry provides definitive proof of captive provisioning and dog husbandry21. Stable carbon (![][image1]) and nitrogen (![][image2]) isotope analyses conducted on bone collagen by researchers such as Dr. Christine D. White et al. (2001, *Journal of Archaeological Science*, Vol. 28, pp. 89–107) and Dr. Aurélie Manin et al. (2018, *Scientific Reports*) demonstrate that domestic dogs in Mesoamerica consumed diets exceptionally enriched in ![][image3] plants—specifically maize (*Zea mays*)21.  
While wild canids display ![][image4]\-dominated isotope signatures consistent with hunting wild herbivores, archaeological domestic dogs from Maya sites (e.g., Ceibal, Preclassic period c. 750–300 BCE; Colha, Belize) and central Mexican sites show ![][image1] values ranging from ![][image5] to ![][image6], proving they were intentionally fed human agricultural products20. Furthermore, strontium isotope (![][image7]) analyses conducted by Dr. Elizabeth Paris et al. ### **Identification of Consumed Canine Varieties**

Mesoamericans did not restrict dog consumption to a single breed14. Zooarchaeological and ethnohistorical records demonstrate that three main varieties were eaten14:

> 1. *Tlalchichi*: The short-limbed, coated dog, specifically preferred for table meat because its dwarfism hindered movement, allowing rapid weight gain on a maize diet14.  
> 2. *Techichi*: The small to medium coated dog, raised for domestic companionship, religious sacrifice, and food14.  
> 3. *Xoloitzcuintli*: The hairless dog, whose flesh was consumed at ritual banquets and sold in markets alongside coated dogs14.

## **Question 3: Functional, Thermal, Healing, and Religious Roles of Small Pre-Columbian Canids**

### **Body Warmers and Thermal Properties**

The functional use of small dogs as body warmers in pre-Columbian and early colonial Mexico is rooted in biophysical reality18. The hairless *Xoloitzcuintli* radiates intense surface heat directly onto skin due to the lack of an insulating fur layer, though its internal body temperature (![][image8]–![][image9]) is identical to that of coated dogs18. Primary ethnohistorical sources, including Sahagún’s *Florentine Codex* (Book XI), record that indigenous peoples placed hairless dogs against their bodies, knees, or abdomen to alleviate joint stiffness, stomach distress, and cold conditions18.

### **Medicinal Lore versus Modern Folklore**

Popular modern folklore has expanded this historic body-warming practice into claims that the Techichi or Chihuahua possessed supernatural healing abilities, such as "absorbing" asthma, rheumatism, arthritis, or organ failure from human patients into their own bodies17. Ethno-botanical and veterinary historical analyses confirm that these claims are mid-to-late 19th-century folk belief systems rather than documented pre-Columbian medical science22.

### **Religious, Mortuary, and Psychopomp Roles**

The primary documented spiritual role of small dogs in ancient Mesoamerica was serving as a psychopomp—a guide for the human soul through the underworld13.  
According to Aztec mortuary theology recorded in the *Florentine Codex* (Book IV and Book III) and depicted in colonial pictorial manuscripts such as the *Codex Magliabechiano* (Folio 72r) and *Codex Vaticanus 3738*, the deceased had to navigate the nine layers of Mictlan (the underworld)13. A critical obstacle was crossing the *Chignahuapan*, a wide, turbulent underworld river18. To cross, the deceased required the assistance of a dog that had been sacrificed and buried alongside them13. Sahagún recorded specific color symbolism: a white dog would refuse to carry the soul, stating it had just washed itself, while a black dog would refuse, stating it had stained itself black; only a yellow or reddish dog (*tlalchichi* or *techichi*) would carry the soul across on its back13.  
Archaeology corroborates this mortuary duty13. In West Mexico (Colima, Nayarit, Jalisco), shaft tombs (100 BCE–300 CE) routinely contain ceramic dog effigies placed next to the head or feet of human interments to facilitate transit to the afterlife13. At the site of Moxviquil in Chiapas, zooarchaeological excavations of a funerary cave context revealed extensive canid remains, primarily juveniles aged 4 months to 3 years, buried alongside human skeletons as funerary companions14.

## **Question 4: Naming and Official Kennel Club Recognition of the Chihuahua**

### **Geographic Naming and Border Trade Origins**

The modern Chihuahua breed derived its name from the northern Mexican state of Chihuahua24. Following the Mexican-American War (1846–1848) and the expansion of rail travel in the mid-to-late 19th century, American tourists and soldiers traveling near the Texas-Mexico border (specifically around El Paso, Texas, and Juárez, Chihuahua) began purchasing tiny indigenous companion dogs from local traders4. These dogs were variously called "Arizona dogs," "Texas dogs," or "Mexico dogs," before standardizing as "Chihuahua dogs" by the late 1880s4.

### **Official Pedigree Recognition and Milestones**

The formal pedigree standardization and recognition of the Chihuahua proceeded through major kennel organizations as follows:

* American Kennel Club (AKC):  
  * First Registration: The AKC officially recognized the Chihuahua breed in 19044. The first individual registered was a short-coated dog named **Midget** (AKC Registration Number 2291), owned and bred by H. Raynor of El Paso, Texas4.  
  * First Champion: In 1905, the bitch **Beppie** (AKC Registration Number 85317), owned by Mrs. L. A. McLean of New Jersey, became the breed's first official AKC champion23.  
  * Parent Club: The Chihuahua Club of America (CCA) was founded in 1923 by Mrs. Henrietta Proctor Donnell, Ida H. Garrett, and Clara L. Dobbs to draft the official breed standard23.  
  * Coat Variety Split: Originally, smooth-coated and long-coated Chihuahuas were shown together in a single class23. On **August 1, 1952**, the AKC Board of Directors officially separated the breed into two distinct show varieties: Smooth Coat and Long Coat23.  
* The Kennel Club (UK):  
  * The first Chihuahuas exhibited in Britain appeared at the Richmond show in 1902 (a dog named "Chaco")24. The Kennel Club (UK) recorded its first official breed registrations in **1907** with the entries "Topsy" and "Prince," imported by Mrs. Powel of London24.  
* United Kennel Club (UKC):  
  * The UKC officially recognized the Chihuahua breed in **1948**37.

## **Question 5: Pomeranian Origins, Quantitative Size Reduction, and Historical Fanciers**

### **Ancestry and Geographical Origin**

The Pomeranian is a miniaturized member of the Spitz family (*Canis lupus familiaris*), descended from larger, cold-climate working, sled, herding, and watchdogs of Northern Europe and the Arctic38. The breed's ancestral lineage includes Arctic and European landraces such as the *Deutscher Spitz* (German Spitz), *Wolfspitz* (Keeshond), *Samoyed*, *Schipperke*, and *Norwegian Elkhound*38.  
The breed takes its name from the region of **Pomerania** (*Pommern*, meaning "on the sea"), situated along the southern coast of the Baltic Sea in modern-day northwest Poland and northeast Germany38. In this region, indigenous Spitz-type dogs were utilized for sheep herding, farm guarding, and cargo protection on river barges38.

### **Chronological Size Reduction and Weight Evolution**

The reduction of the Pomeranian from a medium-sized working Spitz to a toy companion occurred over two centuries through selective breeding38. During the 18th century in Pomerania, ancestral dogs weighed between 30 and 50 pounds (14 to 23 kg)38. Continental German breeders initiated size reduction during the early-to-mid 19th century, reducing working Spitz specimens to between 30 and 40 pounds (14 to 18 kg)38. By the time the breed gained popularity in Victorian show rings in the 1870s, English show specimens weighed approximately 18 pounds (8.2 kg)38. Further selective miniaturization by British kennels during the late 19th and early 20th centuries established the modern toy standard of 3 to 7 pounds (1.4 to 3.2 kg), with an ideal show weight of 4 to 6 pounds (1.8 to 2.7 kg) as codified by the AKC and UK Kennel Club standards39.

### **Documented Royal and Historical Fanciers versus Apocryphal Lore**

The history of the Pomeranian is notable for high-profile royal patrons, though several popular claims regarding historical figures are unverified or apocryphal39:

* Queen Charlotte (Wife of King George III of Great Britain):  
  * *Status*: **Fully Documented**38. In 1761 and 1767, Queen Charlotte brought two Pomeranians, named "Phebe" and "Mercury," from Mecklenburg-Strelitz to England38. These dogs were depicted in royal portraits by Sir Thomas Gainsborough (1727–1788)38. Gainsborough's paintings document dogs weighing 30–50 lbs with white/parti-color coats, pointed muzzles, heavy double coats, and curled tails over the back38.  
* Queen Victoria:  
  * *Status*: **Fully Documented**39. Granddaughter of Queen Charlotte. In 1888, while visiting Florence, Italy, Queen Victoria acquired a small red Pomeranian weighing approximately 12 pounds named "Marco"39. Victoria established her own royal breeding kennels at Windsor, showing "Marco" and other Poms (including "Turi," who was brought to her deathbed in 1901\)39. Her preference for smaller specimens directly drove the worldwide reduction of the breed to toy size39.  
* James Boswell:  
  * *Status*: **Fully Documented**38. The Scottish biographer recorded an encounter with a Pomeranian named "Pomer" in Mainz, Germany, on November 2, 1764, in his private journals, providing the earliest written reference to the breed name in British literature38.  
* Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart:  
  * *Status*: **Fully Documented**39. Mozart owned a small Spitz named "Pimperl" (or "Bimperl"), frequently mentioned in family correspondence between Mozart and his sister Nannerl39.  
* Martin Luther:  
  * *Status*: **Documented**39. The Protestant reformer made written references in his *Table Talk* notes to his dog "Belferlein"39.  
* Sir Isaac Newton ("Diamond"):  
  * *Status*: **Apocryphal / Popular Legend**39. A widely repeated anecdote claims Newton's Pomeranian "Diamond" knocked over a lit candle onto his desk, burning twenty years of scientific manuscripts and causing Newton a nervous breakdown39. Modern historians regard this story as 18th-century folklore, noting Newton never owned a dog in his Cambridge quarters39.  
* Michelangelo Buonarroti:  
  * *Status*: **Breeder Lore / Legend**39. Popular dog literature asserts that Michelangelo’s pet Pomeranian sat on a satin pillow watching him paint the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–1512)39. No 16th-century primary sources, contemporary biographies (Giorgio Vasari or Ascanio Condivi), or personal letters substantiate this claim39.  
* RMS Titanic Survivors:  
  * *Status*: **Fully Documented**39. Of the twelve dogs aboard the ocean liner *RMS Titanic* when it sank on April 15, 1912, three survived39. Two were Pomeranians: one carried into Lifeboat No. 7 by Margaret Bechstein Hays of New York, and another rescued by Elizabeth Barrett Rothschild in Lifeboat No. 639.

## **Question 6: Official Kennel Club Recognition of the Pomeranian**

### **Recognition by The Kennel Club (United Kingdom)**

* Founded in **1873**, The Kennel Club officially recognized the Spitz/Pomeranian among its initial cohort of breeds in its inaugural stud book published in 187438.  
* Special competitive show classes for Pomeranians were introduced at London shows in **1863** and the Altrincham Show in **1870**41.  
* The **English Pomeranian Club**, the first parent specialty club for the breed, was established in **1891**, drafting the modern toy standard41.

### **Recognition by the American Kennel Club**

* The AKC officially recognized the Pomeranian breed in **1888**40.  
* The American Pomeranian Club (APC) was accepted as an AKC member parent specialty club in **1909**.
