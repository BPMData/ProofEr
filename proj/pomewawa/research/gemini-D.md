To answer your question directly: No, I did not find any historical evidence that the Aztecs used hairless dogs as living hot-water bottles. The claim is a modern myth retroactively projected onto ancient history.
Here is the summary of what the evidence actually shows:
 * The Primary Source Says the Opposite: The claim is overwhelmingly credited to Fray Bernardino de Sahagún's 16th-century Florentine Codex. However, his Spanish text explicitly states that because the dogs had no hair, the indigenous people covered the dogs with blankets at night to keep the animals warm. It never says the humans used the dogs to warm themselves.
 * No Other Colonial Records Found It: The foundational medical and natural histories of early colonial Mexico, such as the Badianus Manuscript (1552) and Dr. Francisco Hernández's Historia natural, meticulously document Aztec medical remedies and indigenous dogs. Yet, they never mention hairless dogs being used for warmth or to cure aches and rheumatism.
 * The True Origin is Modern Folk Medicine: Using hairless dogs as a "living hot-water bottle" is a very real, well-documented modern folk practice in rural Mexico and Peru (with the Peruvian hairless dog) to treat ailments like rheumatism and asthma. In the mid-20th century, breed revivalists like Norman Pelham Wright documented this modern practice. Over time, breed clubs and popular media merged this modern rural reality with the dog's ancient Mesoamerican origins, effectively rewriting history.
 * The Biological Reality: Veterinary infrared thermography confirms that hairless breeds do have significantly higher skin surface temperatures than coated dogs because they lack insulating fur. This makes them excellent heat conductors for modern folk medicine, but it is also the exact biological reason why the Aztecs had to wrap them in blankets to help them survive cold nights.
