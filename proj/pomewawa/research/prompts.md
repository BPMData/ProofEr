## Prompt A: Where these dogs come from (brief Q1, Q2, Q3)

```
I'm making a sourced infographic about Chihuahuas and Pomeranians. Research their origins and history. Answer each
question separately.

1. Chihuahua origins. Is the Chihuahua descended from pre-Columbian dogs of Mexico (for example the Techichi kept by
   the Toltecs, Aztecs or others)? What does genetic (DNA) research show about how much pre-Columbian ancestry
   modern Chihuahuas carry, compared with European or other dogs? Separate what DNA studies show, what archaeology
   shows (burials, remains, art), and what is legend or breeder lore. Give dates or periods for each.
2. Small dogs as food in pre-Columbian Mexico. Were small dogs (Techichi or similar) raised and eaten in Mesoamerica?
   By whom, when, and how do we know (colonial-era written accounts such as Spanish chroniclers, archaeological
   remains, isotope studies)? Was the Techichi itself eaten, or a different dog?
3. Small dogs as bed or body warmers. Is there documented evidence that Chihuahuas or their ancestors were kept for
   warmth, or for healing or religious roles (for example accompanying the dead)? Clearly mark what is documented
   and what is modern folklore.
4. When and how the modern Chihuahua breed was named and recognized (place, date, first registration by the American
   Kennel Club or other kennel clubs).
5. Pomeranian origins. Which larger Spitz-type dogs is the Pomeranian descended from (including any sled, herding or
   working dogs), what did those ancestors weigh, and when and how was the breed reduced to its modern size? Include
   documented royal or famous owners (for example Queen Victoria) and the evidence for each.
6. When the Pomeranian was recognized by kennel clubs (The Kennel Club UK, American Kennel Club), with dates.

Rules for your answer:
- For every figure, date, name or claim, give the source: title, author or institution, year, page or table if any,
  and a link. Prefer primary sources (peer-reviewed studies, archaeological reports, kennel club records, historical
  documents) over websites and breeder pages.
- Where reputable sources disagree, list each version with its source and explain why they differ. Don't pick one
  for me.
- Flag estimates as estimates, and legends as legends. No figures without a source. No unsourced round numbers.
- End with a table: claim or figure | value or range | unit | source | year | notes (agree / disputed / legend).
```

## Prompt B: What they're good at, and how they compare (brief Q4, Q5, Q6, Q8)

1. Lifespan. What is the typical lifespan of Chihuahuas and Pomeranians, according to large studies (for example

   Give the study's sample size and years.
2. Size. Breed-standard weight and height for Chihuahuas and Pomeranians (American Kennel Club, The Kennel Club UK,

## Prompt C: Dogs as food around the world (brief Q7)

```
I'm making a sourced, non-judgmental infographic about the documented history of people eating dogs, worldwide. The
point is that it has happened in many cultures, often out of hunger or necessity, not that any one people is
strange. Treat every culture in the same plain, factual way.

1. List documented cases of dog meat being eaten, across all continents and periods: for example pre-Columbian
   Mexico, Switzerland, Germany (including periods of hunger such as the World Wars and sieges), France, Poland,
   the Arctic, Pacific islands, East and Southeast Asia, Africa, and North America (including expeditions and
   famines). For each: where, when, who, why (custom, ritual, medicine, famine, war, siege, expedition), and how
   common it was.
2. Switzerland specifically: what is documented about dog meat eating in Swiss regions, how common it is or was, and
   what Swiss law says about it today (selling vs. eating at home).
3. Today: in which countries is eating dog meat legal, restricted or banned, with the law and its year? Include
   recent changes (for example South Korea's ban on breeding and slaughtering dogs for meat).

Rules for your answer:
- For every case, figure, date or law, give the source: title, author or institution, year, page if any, and a link.
  Prefer primary sources (historical documents, scholarly histories, archaeological reports, official legal texts)
  over news features and listicles.
- Where sources disagree (for example on how common a practice was), list each version with its source and why
  they differ.
- Flag estimates as estimates, and anecdotes as anecdotes. No figures without a source.
- End with a table: place | period | who / why | source | year | notes.
```

```
I need to settle one historical claim with primary evidence, not repetition. The claim: in Aztec and early colonial
Mexico, people used hairless dogs (xoloitzcuintli) to warm themselves at night or to soothe aches and illness, as
"living hot-water bottles". It is repeated by National Geographic, the Smithsonian's National Museum of the
American Indian and many dog-breed sites, often credited to Bernardino de Sahagún's Florentine Codex.

The idea is physically plausible: the dog has no coat, its body runs about 38-39 °C, so dog and person would warm
each other. I'm not asking whether it is plausible. I'm asking what is actually recorded, by whom, and when.

1. The Florentine Codex, Book 11 ("Earthly Things"), the section on dogs. Quote the passage on the
   xoloitzcuintli in full: the Nahuatl text and the English of the Dibble & Anderson translation (University of
   Utah Press / School of American Research), with volume, chapter, paragraph and page. Also give the Spanish
   column (Sahagún's Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España) for the same passage. Then answer plainly:
   does it say people used the dog to warm THEMSELVES, or that people covered or wrapped THE DOG (for example
   with a blanket or cape, tilmatli) to keep the dog warm, or neither? If the passage mentions a blanket, quote
   exactly who covers whom.
2. Search the rest of the Florentine Codex and Sahagún's other works (Primeros Memoriales, Book 10 on bodily
   ailments and medicine, Book 11 on medicinal plants and animals) for any mention of dogs used for warmth,
   healing, rheumatism, joint pain or the sick. Quote what you find, with locations.
3. Other early sources: check Francisco Hernández (Historia natural de Nueva España, the "xoloitzcuintli" or
   "Mexican dog" entries), the Libellus de medicinalibus Indorum herbis (Badianus/Cruz manuscript, 1552), Diego
   Durán, Motolinía, Hernán Cortés's letters, Bernal Díaz del Castillo, Francisco López de Gómara, Gonzalo
   Fernández de Oviedo, and Francisco Javier Clavijero (Storia antica del Messico, 1780). For each: does it
   mention hairless dogs, and does it mention warmth or healing? Quote it, or say plainly "not found".
4. Later evidence: modern ethnography or folk-medicine studies (19th-21st century) recording people in Mexico, or
   in Peru with the Peruvian hairless dog, holding hairless dogs against aching joints, the stomach or the chest,
   or sleeping with them for warmth. Who recorded it, where, when. Include the work of Raúl Valadez Azúa (UNAM)
   and Norman Pelham Wright on the xoloitzcuintli if relevant.
5. Trace the "hot-water bottle" claim: the earliest printed source you can find that says it, in English or
   Spanish, and what that source cites. Then follow the National Geographic and Smithsonian NMAI articles' own
   citations back as far as they go.
6. Any measurements of hairless dogs' skin-surface temperature compared with coated dogs (veterinary studies).

Rules for your answer:
- Quote only text you have actually read in that source. If you only saw a source cited by someone else, say
  "cited secondhand by X" and do not paraphrase it as if you read it. If you cannot access a source, say so.
- For every claim give: title, author or institution, year, volume/chapter/page, and a link. Primary sources
  (the codices, colonial chronicles, peer-reviewed ethnography) before museum pages, magazines and breed sites.
- Where sources disagree, list each version with its source and why they differ.
- "Not found" is a useful answer. Don't fill gaps with plausibility.
- End with a verdict, choosing one: (a) recorded in a 16th-century source (quote it); (b) recorded only in later
  colonial sources; (c) recorded only in modern folk practice or ethnography; (d) no traceable source: a modern
  story. Say how confident you are and what evidence would change the verdict.
- Then a table: source | date | what it says (quoted or "not found") | warmth for people / warmth for dog /
  healing / none | link | read directly or secondhand.
```
