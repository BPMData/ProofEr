## Q1. Gauguin in Arles: dates, length, money

**Arrival: 23 October 1888.**
- Art Institute of Chicago, "Van Gogh and Gauguin: The Studio of the South" site (archive.artic.edu/vangogh/studiosud.html, 2001): "October 23, 1888" marks the start of the Studio of the South.
- u3a Oliva pdf "Van Gogh and Gauguin; Arles and the Aftermath" (amateur, secondary): 23 Oct 1888.
- vangoghstudio.com (secondary): 23 Oct 1888.
- Wikipedia "Vincent van Gogh" (lead only): 23 Oct 1888.
- Agreement: strong. The Art Newspaper (Bailey, 2020) says only "late October". Joint letter to Bernard dated 1 or 2 Nov 1888, "about a week after" (Art Newspaper, 5 June 2020).

**Departure: disputed by a day or two. Not firmly settled.**
- Went to a hotel the night of 23 Dec; Gauguin did not return to the Yellow House to live. (Bailey, Art Newspaper 7 June 2019; Gauguin's Avant et après says he took a room in "un bon hôtel d'Arles".)
- Version A, 25 Dec 1888 evening, travelling with Theo on the night train: Van Gogh Museum Journal 2001 (dbnl.org/tekst/_van012200101_01/_van012200101_01_0008.php, summary) says Gauguin "travelled back to Paris with Theo on 25 December 1888"; u3a pdf says Theo took Gauguin back that evening; vangoghstudio.com says 25 Dec; AIC chronology lists 26 Dec as "After Arles" (consistent with arriving Paris on 26th).
- Version B, 24 Dec 1888: vangoghsear.com/perpetrator.html (the "Pact of Silence" thesis site; a minority argument that Gauguin was involved, NOT Murphy's view) says he left on 24 Dec and quotes his words to Bernard, "The night before my departure". It also says Theo left Paris on the evening of 24 Dec and reached Arles on the morning of 25 Dec, and cites a Theo letter to Jo Bonger, "already left on Tuesday evening". (25 Dec 1888 was a Tuesday, which fits version A, not B. I flag this as an internal tension in that site's argument.)
- Biography.com (2020s) says he "left by train two days before Christmas": this contradicts the other sources and is likely wrong (LOW).
- Wikipedia "Vincent van Gogh": gives no exact departure date.
- Best working claim: left Arles the evening of 25 Dec 1888 (arrived Paris 26 Dec), LOW-MEDIUM confidence. Safer wording for the essay: "left Arles within two days of the night of 23 December".

**Length of stay:** about nine weeks (23 Oct to c. 25 Dec = 9 weeks 1 day). Wikipedia "The Yellow House" (lead): "lived for nine weeks from late October 1888"; Van Gogh's own letters call it "two months". AIC site quotes Van Gogh describing the two months as "excessively electric" (summary; no letter number given).

**Money and the deal.**
- Theo's deal: 150 francs a month in return for one painting a month. Sources: Biography.com (Theo promised 150 francs a month if he moved to Arles); Wikipedia "Vincent van Gogh Painting Sunflowers" (lead: "stipend of 150 francs in return for one painting a month"); webexhibits 538 [WE], Vincent to Theo c. 17 Sept 1888 (probably VGL 683, 18 Sept; the number match is unconfirmed): per the summary Vincent writes of Theo "paying Gauguin 150 francs a month for a picture". Theo also bought Gauguin's Mango Trees, Martinique for 400 francs (The Collector, secondary).
- Primary text (VGL letters from Sept 1888) not read directly. The "one painting a month for 150 francs" is therefore two secondary sources plus one summary of a primary. MEDIUM.
- Fare and debts: Vincent in the Sept 1888 letter [WE 538 summary] suspects Gauguin might leave Pont-Aven without paying his debts. I found NO source saying who paid the Pont-Aven debts or the fare to Arles. u3a pdf says Theo gave "financial assistance for the trip" (secondary, no terms). Verdict: CAN'T VERIFY who paid fare/debts.
- Gauguin's Dec 1888 request: asks Theo to send him part of the money from sales of his canvases so he can go back to Paris ([WE] Gauguin to Theo, "about 12 December 1888"; u3a pdf).

## Q2. What Gauguin wrote to Theo about leaving

- Source: [WE] webexhibits.org/vangogh/letter/18/etc-Theo-1888-s.htm, "[Letter from Gauguin to Theo]", dated there "about 12 December 1888". Wording (short excerpts from the page, English translation): "Everything considered, I am obliged to return to Paris"; "Vincent and I simply cannot live together without trouble, due to the incompatibility of our characters". Content: both need quiet to work; calls Vincent highly intelligent; says he leaves with regret; thanks Theo.
- Same wording confirmed in Wikipedia "The Painter of Sunflowers" (cites Rewald 1956, Post-Impressionism, pp. 241-268; lead only), and u3a pdf ("incompatible temperaments").
- Original French (from memory, NOT verified on a page I opened, do not quote): Gauguin uses "incompatibilité d'humeur et de caractère". Do not use unless checked.
- Date: "about 12 Dec" per webexhibits. Vincent's own letter VGL 724, c. 11 Dec 1888, says Gauguin seems discouraged by Arles and the Yellow House (vangoghstudio.com summary). So Gauguin wrote c. 11-12 Dec, before 23 Dec. Wikipedia says the letter was "written after the incident": that is wrong or at least unsupported by the dates above. Flag.
- "The work is unsalable": I found NO such wording in any source I opened. The webexhibits page contains no "unsalable/unsaleable/unsellable". The draft's quote could not be traced. VERDICT: WRONG / cannot be verified. Do not use it.
- Withdrawal: Not found. Sources only say he stayed on after writing and left after 23 Dec. Vincent's 21 Jan 1889 letter to Gauguin (VGL 739) exists (and Gauguin's reply, not read). I found no source that he formally retracted. CAN'T VERIFY. (Lead worth chasing: VGL 724 notes and VGL 726-728 notes on what Gauguin told Theo after his first letter.)
- Related, later: Gauguin's January 1889 letter, "Cher Monsieur", almost certainly to Albert Dauprat (Merlhès research), says he planned "to spend a year in the south" with a painter friend who "went raving mad", and speaks of a month of dread of a "fatal and tragic accident" (Martin Bailey, The Art Newspaper, 7 June 2019, "Gauguin blamed Van Gogh over ear incident"). Useful because it shows Gauguin framing it as Vincent's madness, and appears to disclaim responsibility.

## Q3. The night of 23 December 1888

**Sequence agreed by Bailey (Art Newspaper 2019), Murphy (via PBS/Penguin pieces) and others:** a row at the Yellow House; Gauguin left and walked toward the centre of town; Vincent cut off his left ear (most of it), wrapped it, took it to the brothel and gave it to a young woman (Bailey, 7 June 2019). Found next morning by police and taken to the hospital where Dr Félix Rey treated him (Wikipedia "Vincent van Gogh" lead).

**Gauguin's razor account (Avant et après, written 1903).**
- Avant et après (BeQ pdf, beq.ebooksgratuits.com/vents/Gauguin-avant.pdf, opened): Gauguin walks across the Place Victor-Hugo, hears Vincent's quick footsteps behind him, turns and sees him rushing at him "un rasoir ouvert à la main". Exact line (from the summary): "Vincent se précipitait sur moi un rasoir ouvert à la main." Gauguin's stare stops him; Vincent runs back to the house. Gauguin goes to a hotel, sleeps badly, and the next morning finds a crowd and the police commissioner, who says Vincent cut off his ear.
- Why doubted: (1) written about 15 years later (museovangogh.org, secondary); (2) nobody corroborates it (same); (3) Bailey says the later account "may have been embellished" (Art Newspaper 2019); (4) Gauguin's own versions shifted: an earlier letter to Bernard also has Vincent running after him; the "Pact of Silence" authors say he told the self-mutilation story to Dr Rey and later invented details (vangoghsear.com; minority view that Gauguin himself cut the ear, which mainstream scholarship does not accept). Murphy's own detailed assessment I could not read.
- A 1928 reference names the hotel as Hôtel Thévot (Apollo magazine piece, secondary).

**Ear: part or whole?**
- Evidence is split. Martin Bailey: "much and perhaps most of the ear, but certainly not all of it" (Art Newspaper 17 Apr 2020, summary).
- Witnesses (Bailey summary): Jo Bonger 1914 "a piece of his ear"; Paul Signac 1921 "the lobe of the ear (not the ear)"; Paul Gachet Jr "a good part of the outer ear"; Émile Bernard "cut his ear clean through"; policeman Alphonse Robert 1929 "the entire ear"; Doiteau and Leroy 1936: upper part, perhaps just over half.
- Dr Rey drawing: a 1930 note by Rey for Irving Stone (researching Lust for Life), with a sketch of the wound and a caption about "what remained of the lobe" (Art Newspaper 13 July 2016 summary). Held in the Irving Stone papers, The Bancroft Library, UC Berkeley. Found by Bernadette Murphy with archivist David Kessler; discovery in 2010 (UC Berkeley Library news page, lib.berkeley.edu/about/news/van-Gogh-ear): shows "a clear incision across the base of the ear", i.e. nearly all the ear gone, leaving a sliver of lobe. A 1955 letter at the Van Gogh Museum had pointed to the note. Also Gustave Coquiot (met Rey 1922) wrote that Rey said the ear was removed "leaving only the tragus" (Art Newspaper 2016). Smithsonian summary says "nub of the earlobe": slightly different reading of the drawing, so give the Berkeley/Art Newspaper wording.
- Caution from Bailey: Rey's later recollections contained errors (e.g. thumb-painting), so the sketch is not fully reliable.
- Van Gogh Museum's stated position: per Bailey (Art Newspaper 2020), the museum's letters website says Van Gogh "did indeed cut off his entire ear". Taken second-hand; I could not open the museum page. MEDIUM. The 2016 VGM exhibition "On the Verge of Insanity" lent the Rey note. Bailey's own view is not "entire".
- Recommended essay wording: "most or nearly all of his left ear" with the Rey drawing, with a note that the museum says "entire" and Bailey says "most, not all".

**Who received it.**
- "Rachel": name in the 1888 press and in biographies. Art Newspaper (Bailey, 20 July 2016): newspapers called her "Rachel", "possibly her nickname"; a policeman in 1936 named her "Gaby".
- Gabrielle Berlatier: Murphy identified a woman named Gabrielle; her surname was withheld at the family's request. Bailey published "Gabrielle Berlatier", a farmer's daughter, 18, living at the Mas de Faravelle in Moulès, bitten by a rabid dog on 8 Jan 1888, treated at the Institut Pasteur (Art Newspaper 20 July 2016). Murphy argues she was a maid/cleaner in the brothel, not a prostitute (fsgworkinprogress.com piece by Murphy's publisher; Van Gogh Museum shop/blog summary).
- Age disagreement: 17 (museovangogh.org), 18 (Bailey), 19 (Apollo, Murphy blog). Use "a teenager". Bailey 2019 and Wikipedia name her Gabrielle Berlatier (Wikipedia footnotes 151, 156).
- What Vincent said (Murphy, on different press versions): Gauguin's version: "Here you are ... a memory of me"; Le Forum républicain: "Take this and keep it preciously"; Le Petit Provençal: "Take this, it will be of use to you" (fsgworkinprogress.com). u3a pdf: "keep this object carefully, in remembrance of me".
- Murphy suggests "Rachel" may have come from a mix-up with "Gabrielle". This is Murphy's suggestion; tag as hypothesis.

**Theo's arrival.**
- Theo took the night train on 24 Dec and arrived in Arles on Christmas Day, 25 Dec 1888; he stayed briefly and went back to Paris that evening (Wikipedia "Vincent van Gogh", footnote 164; museovangogh.org; vangoghsear.com). Three agreeing secondary sources, none primary. MEDIUM. A webexhibits note on VGL 724/565 says Theo "stayed over Christmas", which differs; unclear what it refers to.

## Q4. The petition

- Count: 30 names. Sources: Wikipedia "Vincent van Gogh" and "Hospital in Arles" (30 townspeople; both cite Pickvance 1986 Chronology pp. 239-242 and Tralbaut 1981 pp. 265-273); cosmopolis.ch (30 residents); hanta.nl (Dutch review of Murphy, "door dertig mensen"); Smithsonian (Murphy: "only about 30 people signed", not hundreds); museovangogh.org (30 neighbours; Ginoux family among them). Strongly agreed: "about 30".
- Real signatures vs names: mainlymuseums.com (blog; lead only, LOW CONFIDENCE) says the petition held 30 names, three signers were illiterate and had their names written by a third party, so 27 actually signed; Gustave Coquiot had claimed about 100. Needs a better source (Murphy's book, ch. on the petition).
- Date: sources disagree. Wikipedia says March 1889 (unreliable on this: Salles letter of 2 March 1889 to Theo [WE] says Vincent was already interned on police orders after the petition). Cosmopolis says c. February 1889. Vincent was readmitted to hospital about 26 Feb 1889 (not confirmed on a page I opened). So: signed in February 1889, before 26 Feb, exact day not found. VERDICT: date can't be pinned.
- What it asked: that Van Gogh be detained and kept from returning to his house, "not in full possession of his mental faculties" (cosmopolis.ch, wording is the site's); hanta.nl says it asked that he be sent back to the Netherlands or committed. The Salles letter [WE, 2 Mar 1889, to Theo] says it called him "a lunatic" and warned of "a real danger to leave him at liberty". Wikipedia: signers called him "le fou roux" ("the redheaded madman").
- Police: Central Commissioner (Joseph d'Ornano per cosmopolis.ch) held the petition and questioned signatories, taking written depositions. Salles [WE, 1 and 2 March 1889]: the commissioner "still believed his measures were justified", he took depositions that Salles found "exaggerated" (children following Vincent, women saying he had "seized some by the waist", excess drinking disputed by the café owner). D'Ornano's report backed detention in a special asylum, "madman [who] has moments of lucidity" (cosmopolis, summary). Outcome: Vincent kept in the Arles hospital with Rey and Salles arguing against an asylum; the house was closed by police (Wikipedia); Vincent left for Saint-Rémy on 8 May 1889 (date not verified here; The Art Newspaper says he gave up the lease on 30 Apr 1889 and moved to the asylum).
- Where held: Murphy's research used Arles municipal sources. The Art Newspaper (13 July 2016) says the petition had never been displayed before the 2016 VGM show. The holding archive is not stated in anything I opened. CAN'T VERIFY (likely Arles archives, not confirmed).
- Murphy's finding on motives: Murphy used a database of Arles residents to link signers and concluded commercial interests, not Van Gogh's behaviour, lay behind it (hanta.nl, a review, LOW-MEDIUM). Also reported by the hanta review: the Yellow House was rented to someone else while he was in hospital (unverified).

## Q5. The Yellow House (2 Place Lamartine)

- Rented: lease signed 1 May 1888, right-hand wing (Art Newspaper, "Renting the Yellow House", 1 May 2020, quoting his letter "Today I rented the right-hand wing of this building..."). Wikipedia "The Yellow House": 1 May 1888, four rooms. Van Gogh Museum painting page (s0032v1962): "In May 1888 Van Gogh rented four rooms ... on Place Lamartine". Agreement: strong. He moved in around Sept 1888 (beds bought; Art Newspaper).
- Gave up the lease on 30 April 1889 (Art Newspaper).
- Damaged: Allied bombing raid on Arles, June 1944; Wikipedia gives 25 June 1944 ("severely damaged in a bombing raid by the Allies on June 25, 1944"); Art Newspaper confirms June 1944, Van Gogh's bedroom destroyed, Gauguin's room partly survived, house not rebuilt and demolished, plaque lost. The exact demolition date was not given in either. Verdict: June 1944 bombing holds; exact day 25 June has one source (Wikipedia): LOW-MEDIUM; demolition year CAN'T VERIFY ("afterwards").
- A plaque from 1922 had wrong dates (1887-88). Not needed.

## Q6. Portraits

**Vincent's Fogg self-portrait (Harvard Art Museums 1951.65).**
- Harvard record (hvrd.art/o/299843): "Self-Portrait Dedicated to Paul Gauguin", Vincent van Gogh, 1888, oil on canvas, 61.5 x 50.3 cm, Bequest from the Collection of Maurice Wertheim, Class of 1906. Inscription upper left in red: "a mon ami Paul G[auguin, Arles]" (barely legible); signed "Vincent/Arles". Provenance: Gauguin sold it for 300 francs; Hugo von Tschudi; removed from the Neue Staatsgalerie, Munich, 1938; Theodor Fischer Gallery Lucerne 1939; Wertheim 1939-51. The Harvard page, as relayed, does not use "bonze". Harvard's own descriptions of the background differ ("aqua green" vs "pale veronese green"): record both, do not pick.
- Letter to Gauguin, VGL 695, 3 Oct 1888 [WE 544a, summary]: portrait "all ash grey", reddish-brown jacket, pale malachite background; he aimed for "the character of a bonze, a simple worshipper of the eternal Buddha". (Quoted phrases came through a summary: check the exact spelling in the translation.)
- Letter to Theo, VGL 697, 4 or 5 Oct 1888 (CL 545; [WE] dates it 7 Oct): sent self-portrait to Gauguin; "a simple worshiper of the eternal Buddha", eyes "slightly slanting like the Japanese"; tone "ashen gray against pale veronese (no yellow)"; brown coat with blue border; thick impasto, almost no shadow. He called Gauguin's portrait remarkable but "a prisoner. Not a shadow of gaiety." and Bernard's "inner vision of a painter". (All via [WE] summary.)
- Art Newspaper 16 June 2023 (summary): Vincent says he "slightly slanted the eyes in the Japanese manner" so that Gauguin would look like a Buddhist monk (garbled: the letter says he made himself look like a bonze). Treat as lead.
- "Orange/red hair": Harvard record says auburn hair, green eyes.

**Gauguin's "Les Misérables" self-portrait (Van Gogh Museum, 1888; Self-Portrait with Portrait of Émile Bernard).**
- Gauguin to Van Gogh, VGL 692, 1 Oct 1888 (VGM catalogue cat. 57, Contemporaries of Van Gogh 1): "We've satisfied your desire; in a different way, it's true." "Our 2 portraits." He presents himself as Jean Valjean, "By doing him with my features, you have my individual image", and "mask of a thief, badly dressed and powerful like Jean Valjean". (Exact wording via summary; the catalogue page should be re-read.) The VGM collection page (s0224V1962) adds that he spoke of "poor victims of society".
- Bernard: Gauguin put a schematic portrait of Bernard on the opposite edge of the canvas; Bernard's own self-portrait carries a small sketch of Gauguin in the background and a Hiroshige Tokaido print in the lower right (VGM cat. 57; Art Newspaper 2023). Inscription "les misérables" lower right.
- Vincent to Theo VGL 697 [WE]: "a prisoner. Not a shadow of gaiety"; later he told Theo "my portrait is equally serious but less desperate" (The Collector quoting a letter, no number).
- Letters: 680 (Vincent's request for exchange, c. 11 Sept), 692, 695, 697, 699 (VGM cat. 57).

**Gauguin's "Christ in the Garden of Olives" (1889, Norton Museum of Art).**
- Norton / Getty page (getty.edu/art/exhibitions/business/lhote.html): Gauguin, 1889, oil on canvas, 28 1/2 x 36 in, gift of Elizabeth C. Norton (46.5). Andre Lhote wrote "The Christ resembles Gauguin" (letter to Gabriel Frizeau, 11 Nov 1908).
- NGA brochure (nga.gov, gauguin_brochure.pdf): Gauguin painted himself as Christ, "deserted by all of his disciples", "surroundings ... as sad as his soul"; head askew, eyes down.
- Wikipedia: 1889, Norton; Gauguin quoted by Huret, L'Echo de Paris 1891: "symbolize the failure of an ideal, the suffering which was both divine and human".
- "Red hair": NOT found for Gauguin's picture. The "purple Christ with red hair" in the Van Gogh Museum Journal 2001 text is Theo's description of Bernard's painting (and Vincent's own redheaded Pieta). The draft must not say Gauguin's Christ has red hair unless a source is found. Gauguin's own description (a letter to Vincent c. Nov 1889 is cited by some; not opened): CAN'T VERIFY.
- Van Gogh was "deeply offended" by Gauguin's and Bernard's Christ pictures (Van Gogh Museum Journal 2001, summary): lead.

## Q7. Sales

- Only one Van Gogh painting is generally said to have sold in his lifetime: The Red Vineyard, painted Nov 1888 at Arles (Art Newspaper, "first 12 Van Gogh paintings ever sold", 23 Sept 2022), bought by Anna Boch for 400 francs, shown in Brussels (Les XX), March 1890. The sale date/amount are debated; Art Newspaper's wider reporting ("only painting sold ... ended up in Russia", 2022, 2025 versions exist) and the Pushkin Museum hold it.
- The other Arles-period works in that article sold after his death: Three Sunflowers (April 1891, Mirbeau, 300 francs), Harvest in Provence, Farmhouse in Provence, White Cottages at Saintes-Maries, Barges (Nov 1891, Willy Gretor, part of 2,200 francs for six works), Peach Trees in Blossom (June 1891, Boch, 350). These are Aug-Oct 1888 works, not the Gauguin weeks.
- No source I opened shows any work painted during the Gauguin weeks (23 Oct to 25 Dec 1888) other than The Red Vineyard being sold in his lifetime. Whether the Studio of the South works were unsold: CAN'T FULLY VERIFY; consistent with the account that the Red Vineyard was the only sale. Needs the VGM catalogue entry for The Red Vineyard.
- Gauguin's side: Gauguin sold Vincent's Fogg portrait later for 300 francs (Harvard provenance); Theo had earlier bought Gauguin works. Gauguin took no sale from the Arles weeks that I could find. LOW.

## Q8. Gauguin's later life

- Tahiti 1891: Wikipedia (lead): set sail 1 April 1891, first three months in Papeete (Thomson 1987, p. 127). National Gallery, London: "arrived in Papeete ... in 1891". Arrival June 1891: not verified.
- Marquesas 1901: National Gallery says he moved to Hiva Oa in 1901; Wikipedia says he settled in Atuona on 16 Sept 1901 (footnote, source not shown). MEDIUM.
- Death: 8 May 1903, Atuona, Hiva Oa, age 54: Britannica (opened) and Wikipedia (infobox). Agreement.
- Syphilis: Wikipedia: "there is no direct evidence that Gauguin suffered from syphilis"; Mathews (2001) suggests early cardiac symptoms may have been early signs; some biographers read the leg sores as syphilis (Thomson 1987, pp. 222-223). Artnet (Oct 2019) calls him "the syphilitic artist". Hyperallergic: "long been believed" he was wrecked by syphilis; his teeth were found in a well (reassessment possible; Art Newspaper 2007). So the draft must say "believed to have had syphilis, but the evidence is indirect". Two sources disagree on certainty.
- Teha'amana: "a 13-year-old girl" (Wikipedia, Mathews 2001 pp. 179-182); Noa Noa calls her "a child of about thirteen" (Art Newspaper, Bailey, 7 Oct 2019); Artnet: "probably 13". Mathews notes Gauguin stressed her youth for effect, and Elizabeth Childs questions whether she is a real person or a composite. Say: "reported as about 13, in a text Gauguin wrote himself".
- Vaeoho (Pau'ura's?) the 14-year-old: Wikipedia: "14-year-old daughter of a native couple" (Danielsson 1965, pp. 240-241). Single secondary source on a page I opened; no museum source. LOW CONFIDENCE.
- Museum framing, National Gallery London 2019 ("Gauguin Portraits", 7 Oct 2019 to 26 Jan 2020, after Ottawa): Artnet reports curator Christopher Riopelle: "Formal analysis [of paintings] cannot occlude the real issues"; a wall text says Gauguin "undoubtedly exploited his position as a privileged Westerner"; Artnet and Art Newspaper note the show addresses his behaviour with young girls and criticise some captions as euphemistic. The Gallery's own web page frames the Polynesian portraits as products of imagination and cultural contradiction, "a visiting French artist who had both encountered, and yet did not fully understand" his subjects (nationalgallery.org.uk), and does not give the girls' ages.
