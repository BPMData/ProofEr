It's All in the Wall

Römertopf or La Chamba, VitaClay or Magnifique: nearly everything a clay pot does at dinner, from how slowly it heats to whether it cracks on a burner to whether Tuesday’s garlic turns up in Thursday’s porridge, comes down to three things the box almost never prints, namely how hot it was fired, how open its pores are, and how much it swells when it heats.[[8]]

1The wall

What is actually different about a glazed and an unglazed clay wall?

Short answerUnglazed walls are full of open pores that drink water, oil and soap; a glaze is a layer of glass that shuts them all out; and La Chamba, which looks glazed, isn’t.

Why does “water absorption” come out smaller than “open porosity”?

Water absorption divides the water gained by the dry clay’s weight; open porosity divides the pore space by the whole pot’s volume. Fired clay (about 1.6–2.5 g/cm³) is denser than water, so a terracotta pot that absorbs 15% by weight routinely has 24–30% of its volume open to water.[[2]]

What’s a Zisha “dual pore”?

Fired Zisha, the purple clay from Yixing that VitaClay uses, has two families of pores at once: gaps of roughly 0.2–0.5 µm between grains, and nanopores under 10 nm inside the glassy matrix between them, which is why it holds a broth without soaking yet still picks up fats and aromas.[[3]]

What does the box tell you, if it won’t print any of this?

The care rules give it away: “soak 10–20 minutes, cold oven only” means low-fired, wide-open terracotta (Römertopf);[[14]] “never soak the outside, no oven or stovetop” means a semi-vitrified body that relies on an electric base’s gentle heating (VitaClay);[[17]] “gas OK, diffuser on electric coils” means crack-stopping mica or a low-expansion flameware body (La Chamba, flameware);[[11]] and “glazed; sear, stovetop and oven OK” means a glass-sealed body tuned to resist shock (Magnifique), even if the maker won’t say how.[[7]]

2The names

Does it matter whether a pot is German, Colombian, Chinese or “volcanic”?

Short answerMuch less than the kiln: where the clay came from matters less than how hot it was fired, how much open pore space is left, and how its thermal expansion was tamed.[[8]]

Myth check“Volcanic clay” is molded lava

Fresh basalt, pumice and lava have no plasticity at all, so you can’t throw a pot from them. Real “volcanic” pottery either uses weathered volcanic soils, mixes crushed basalt or scoria in as grit (Jeju), or, in the modern Korean ttukbaegi that claim 0% absorption and no cracking, blends in imported lithium-bearing petalite and fires above 1,250°C. Traditional Korean onggi fired at 1,100–1,200°C measures 10.8–38.2% porosity.[[9]] Verdict: a marketing label; the crack resistance comes from petalite.

Myth checkClay gives off special “far-infrared” heat

Every surface at 100–250°C radiates infrared at roughly 5–20 µm, metal included, and those waves reach only fractions of a millimeter into food. What clay really has over shiny metal is emissivity: it radiates at about 0.90–0.95 of the theoretical maximum against under 0.1 for polished aluminum or steel, plus a low conductivity that keeps hot spots from scorching.[[10]] Verdict: real effect, ordinary physics.

Myth checkLa Chamba is shiny, so it must be glazed

Protected by Colombia’s SIC Resolution No. 72993 (2019), La Chamba blends three Magdalena River clays: plastic arcilla lisa, sandy arcilla arenosa, and a fine red slip, arcilla roja, all rich in mica.[[5]] Potters rub the leather-hard pots with smooth river stones (bruñido), which lays the clay’s flat platelets parallel so they reflect like a mirror, then smother the hot pots in organic chaff (ahumado), which drives carbon into the surface and turns the iron black. Since the pores stay open, importers say to cure liquid-holding pots before first use: three-quarters full of water, uncovered, into a cold oven, 400°F (200°C) for 30 minutes.[[11]] Verdict: 0% glaze.

What are petalite, spodumene and cordierite, and what is “thermal expansion”?

Thermal expansion (CTE) is how many millionths of its length a material grows for each degree it heats; when one patch of a pot is hotter than the next, the difference in growth is the stress that cracks it. Petalite and spodumene (lithium minerals) and cordierite (a magnesium mineral) form crystals that barely expand, so “flameware” built on them has a CTE of roughly −0.5 to 3.5 millionths per °C, against 4.5–8.0 for ordinary earthenware.[[23]] Banko-yaki donabe contain about 40–50% petalite.[[9]]

3Heat

Why does clay cook so slowly, and how much hotter should the oven be?

Short answerClay lets heat through at roughly 1% of aluminum’s rate, so it’s slow to heat and slow to swing; and for a soaked clay baker, the US Römertopf booklet says +100°F and 30 minutes less, starting from a cold oven.[[12]][[14]]

Myth checkCast iron sears well because it has a “high specific heat”

Backwards, per kilogram: gray cast iron stores 460–505 J/(kg·K), about half of aluminum’s 880–904 or clay’s 750–1,000. Cast iron wins per volume because it’s so dense (7,100–7,300 kg/m³), so every millimeter of wall holds 3.27–3.69 MJ/(m³·K) of heat against about 2.4 for aluminum.[[12]] That stored heat is what keeps the pan from crashing when a cold, wet steak lands on it.

What do W/(m·K), MJ/(m³·K) and mm²/s actually measure?

Conductivity, W/(m·K): how many watts flow through a one-meter slab for each degree of difference between its faces; higher means a hot burner spot spreads out faster. Volumetric heat capacity, MJ/(m³·K): how much energy a cubic meter of wall takes up per degree, density times specific heat. Diffusivity, mm²/s: conductivity divided by that heat capacity, which says how fast a temperature change sweeps across the wall; aluminum evens out about a hundred times faster than terracotta.[[12]]

Why do the German and US Römertopf instructions differ?

The US booklet prints “+100°F (~50°C)” and minus 30 minutes against an ordinary recipe; the German site (roemertopf.de) skips conversions entirely and just prescribes 180–200°C with convection, or about 220°C top and bottom heat, maximum 250°C, from a cold oven.[[14]]

If the lid traps the steam, why does slow-cooked meat still come out drier?

Because the water was never the pot’s to keep. A 1983 University of Illinois study (Peters et al., Journal of the American Dietetic Association) found that meat loaves and chickens cooked in an electric slow cooker lost more drip and total weight than oven-cooked ones:[[19]] a sealed pot stops evaporation, but it doesn’t stop muscle proteins from squeezing out their own water over hours at temperature.

What’s an extension service?

The public outreach arm of a US land-grant university (Michigan State, Purdue, Texas A&M and others), which publishes tested home-cooking and food-safety advice; their slow-cooker guidance is where the “30–50% less liquid” and “each lid lift costs 20–30 minutes” rules come from.[[15]]

5Cracks

Why do clay pots crack, and why do multi-cooker inserts almost never do?

Short answerClay is strong when squeezed and weak when stretched; heat one patch faster than the wall can spread it, and the hot part pulls the cold part apart. Electric bases avoid that by wrapping the pot in heat and ramping it slowly.[[20]][[22]]

  • Cast-iron insertThe iron won’t crack, but the enamel can craze if you quench it violently.[[29]]
Myth checkDense, high-fired stoneware is tougher on a flame than porous pottery

Not on a flame. Vitrified stoneware is strong, so it shrugs off small stresses right up until it fails, and then the crack runs through the whole glassy wall. Thermal-shock damage theory and archaeological tests (Tite et al. 2001; Müller et al. 2010) show that coarse grit, open pores and flat mica flakes deflect and stop small cracks before they split the pot, trading impact strength for burner survival.[[20]]

Why does a wet pot crack in a hot oven?

Two ways at once: the outside heats and swells faster than the inside, and the soak water in the pores flashes to steam with nowhere to go. Starting a soaked pot in a cold oven lets both happen slowly.[[21]][[14]]

How does an electric multi-cooker keep a clay insert from cracking?

From the first slow cooker patent (Naxon, US 2,187,888) to modern ones, the heating element wraps around the sides rather than sitting under one spot, and controllers pulse the power to ramp the temperature instead of slamming it on, which keeps the difference across the wall small.[[22]]

6Stovetops

Can it go on my stove, and what about induction?

Short answerGas is the friendliest burner for clay that’s rated for it; coils and glass tops need a diffuser; and no clay pot works on induction by itself, while the steel adapter plates that would make it work are banned by some cooktop makers.[[24]]

7Browning and flavor

Will it brown, will it stick, and will it remember last week’s curry?

Short answerBrowning needs a dry surface at 140–165°C, which a lidded clay pot full of steam won’t give you until the lid comes off; and unglazed clay soaks up fats and aromas that a glaze simply wipes clean of.[[25]][[27]]

What is “water activity”?

A 0-to-1 scale of how much free, chemically available water is at the food’s surface (pure water is 1.0); it isn’t the same as total moisture, and browning slows when it’s below about 0.3 or above about 0.85.[[25]]

Plate 7b · Why food stickssurface energy, mN/m · bars start at zero

The stickier the surface, the more the meat grabs it.

04008001,200

Glazed ceramic is covered in polar silanol groups that bind wet proteins unless the surface is oiled; bare iron binds hardest of all until polymerized oil (seasoning) covers it.[[26]] Surface energy is the energy it takes to make a square meter of new surface; higher means liquids and proteins cling harder.

Plate 7c · Flavor memoryhow deep it gets in

Fats go 5–400+ µm into unglazed clay.

Mass spectrometry of unglazed Yixing pots and La Chamba cooking experiments find fatty acids (palmitic, stearic), waxes and hydrocarbons, not water-soluble broth, migrating into the wall.[[27]][[6]]

When it helps

One pot for one tea (yi tu yi cha) rounds off astringency; a pot kept for the same savory stew deepens it.[[27]]

When it hurts

Garlic, curry or fish ghosts turn up in porridge or yogurt; and since clay can’t be baked at cast iron’s 450–500°F seasoning heat, absorbed oils don’t polymerize and can go rancid in storage.[[26]]

  • RömertopfSteams while lidded; take the lid off for the last 15–20 minutes to brown. Its pores keep oils and aromas.[[14]][[27]]
  • La ChambaAbsorbs cooking fats into the wall over repeated use, so it builds a flavor history.[[6]]
  • VitaClayCan’t sear in it: brown meat in a separate skillet. Its pores trap fats; mild soap is allowed if rinsed well.[[17]][[27]]
  • MagnifiqueSears and sautés in the pot on its base; glaze grabs wet protein unless oiled, but keeps no flavor and goes in the dishwasher.[[7]][[26]]
  • Aluminum insertThe easiest release while the coating lasts; PTFE starts breaking down at 260°C (500°F), sol-gel ceramic coatings at 250–300°C.[[26]][[28]]
  • Cast-iron insertThe best sear, thanks to all that stored heat; the enamel keeps no flavor.[[12]][[29]]
Myth checkOne drop of dish soap ruins an unglazed pot

Half true. Soap surfactants (SDS, LAS) do get pulled into clay pores and cling to the clay minerals electrostatically, which is why dishwashers and harsh detergents stay banned and traditional guides say to scrub with warm water, coarse salt or baking-soda paste.[[26]] But newer Römertopf and VitaClay manuals allow a little mild dish soap once the pot is cool, as long as you rinse thoroughly with hot water; VitaClay suggests a soak with a quarter-cup of baking soda for odors.[[17]][[14]]

Why does a sealed, wet pot refuse to brown?

While there’s free water on the food’s surface, it can’t get much hotter than water’s boiling point, 100°C (212°F); browning only takes off once that water is gone and the surface climbs to 140–165°C.[[25]]

9Glaze safety

Should I worry about lead in the glaze?

Short answerNot with intact commercial cookware, which is tested against the limits below; the real, documented hazard is uncertified, low-fired (about 900–1,000°C) lead-glazed folk pottery, especially with hot, acidic food.[[30]]

Plate 9 · Leachable lead limitsmg/L after 24 h in 4% acetic acid · bars start at zero

The stricter the standard, the shorter the bar.

00.51.01.5 mg/L

The test: fill the pot with 4% acetic acid (about the strength of vinegar), leave it 24 hours at room temperature, and measure the lead that comes out. 1 mg/L is 1 part per million.[[30]]

Myth checkYou can “cure” a lead-glazed pot by boiling vinegar, milk or garlic in it

FDA testing says no: home curing doesn’t seal or neutralize lead. And unglazed La Chamba has no glaze to leach in the first place.[[30]]

About these numbers

Every figure on this page comes from at least two independent sources, except where it says otherwise; where sources disagree, the page shows the range. What’s single-sourced or missing:

  • Single source: Zisha’s thermal conductivity, 1.3 W/(m·K), comes from one peer-reviewed modeling study (Lee & Attinger 2016).
  • Not measured anywhere we could find: a 2/4/8-hour water-loss table for clay against metal; the grams of water a soaked Römertopf holds; a lab porosity figure for modern La Chamba; thermal expansion for the Zisha subtypes; conductivity for La Chamba itself.
  • Not disclosed by the maker: Magnifique’s slow-cook temperatures and any low-expansion additives; VitaClay’s “480°F shock resistance” is in web and video copy but not in its PDF manuals; the 2-qt VitaClay’s wattage conflicts between listings.
  • Drawings: the wall strips, the cracking pot and the flavor ruler show how things work, not measurements; the quench test and the browning map use the published thresholds.
