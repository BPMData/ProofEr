## Headline findings (what changes or contradicts the brief)

1. **Beddoe's index measures HAIR only, not overall "darkness."** The formula is `D + 2N − R − F` (dark-haired + twice
   black-haired − red-haired − fair-haired). Eye colour is classed separately (light / neutral / dark) and is not in the
   index. The brief's "scoring peoples of the British Isles by darkness" needs tightening to "by hair colour."
2. **Beddoe's own data did NOT make the Irish uniformly "dark."** He concludes that the Irish are "considerably lighter
   in eye, but yet more considerably darker in hair, than the English as a whole". He also says Connaught has "less of
   dark eyes and dark hair than any other province of Ireland". The highest index he reports on the page searched is
   in **Devon, England** (Dartmouth district, "exceeding 50"). The essay can't say that Beddoe ranked the Irish as the
   darkest people in Britain.
3. **Beddoe built class and colonial ancestry into his readings.** The Dublin and Cork upper classes scored 14 and 21.4,
   against 33 and 34.2 for the lower class in the same cities. He put the lighter hair of the upper class down to
   English ancestry among the landed and professional classes. This is a stronger and more honest beat for the essay
   than "the Irish scored dark."
4. **"Africanoid" is a separate claim about skull and face type, not part of the nigrescence index.** In Ch. II
   Beddoe "provisionally" names an "Africanoid" type and says Ireland "is apparently its present centre." The essay
   must not merge this into the hair index.
5. **O'Connell's "Over the broad Atlantic … come out of such a land" quote: source, date and venue are UNVERIFIED.**
   The brief's "1845, to Americans" is not confirmed, and searching was impossible this session. Don't print it as
   dated 1845 until it's traced to a newspaper report (*The Liberator*, *Freeman's Journal*, *The Nation*, or
   O'Connell's published correspondence).
6. **"Inbetween peoples" isn't Roediger's term for the famine-era Irish.** It comes from James R. Barrett and David
   Roediger, "Inbetween Peoples" (*Journal of American Ethnic History*, 1997). It is about the "new immigrants" of
   about 1890–1945 (southern and eastern Europeans). Applying it to the 1840s–60s Irish takes it out of context.
7. **The legal answer stands as the brief has it:** no court ever ruled the Irish non-white under the 1790 "free white
   person" clause. One nuance to add: the Naturalization Act of 1798, which raised the residence term to 14 years, was
   aimed partly at Irish radicals. It was a nativist, political barrier, not a racial one. (Not re-read this
   session.)
8. **Douglass's well-known description of Irish poverty may come from his Montrose letter (Feb. 1846), not from the
   Victoria Hotel, Belfast letter (1 Jan. 1846).** Before quoting, check which letter each line comes from (worker's
   recollection, UNVERIFIED).

### Q9-1 — Theodore W. Allen, *The Invention of the White Race*: thesis, the Irish analogy, critics
- CLAIM (brief): Allen is one of the chapter's scholarly anchors, "stated with their theses *and* their critics."
- FINDING (NOT RE-READ THIS SESSION): Vol. 1, *Racial Oppression and Social Control* (London: Verso, 1994); Vol. 2,
  *The Origin of Racial Oppression in Anglo-America* (Verso, 1997); 2nd ed. Verso 2012 with introduction by Jeffrey B.
  Perry. Allen's thesis is that the "white race" was a ruling-class invention for social control in the Chesapeake
  colonies. It came in the late 17th and early 18th centuries, after Bacon's Rebellion (1676), when laws gave poor
  European-descended laborers privileges over Africans so as to split a cross-racial laboring class. Vol. 1 makes the
  Irish case central. Allen calls English and Anglo-Norman rule in Ireland, and above all the Protestant Ascendancy and
  its Penal Laws, **"racial oppression"** without any colour line. He uses it to argue that racial oppression is a
  social-control system, not a response to phenotype. His defining hallmark, in substance: everyone in the oppressed
  group is reduced to one undifferentiated status below every member of the oppressor group, whatever their class. He
  then sets this against the Irish in America, who as "whites" moved into the oppressor group. Strongest criticisms
  (summarized; confirm before attributing):
  (a) Methodological critics of whiteness studies, notably Kolchin (2002) and Arnesen (2001) (see Q9-2), say
  "invention" language and stretching "race" to cover religious and national oppression make the term so loose that
  it explains little. The Irish analogy is the clearest case of that stretch.
  (b) Historians of early Virginia (the "origins debate": Winthrop Jordan, *White over Black*, 1968; Alden T. Vaughan,
  "The Origins Debate," *Virginia Magazine of History and Biography*, 1989) argue that English anti-Black prejudice
  came before the legal hardening of slavery, against Allen's "no white race before c. 1700" chronology.
  (c) Irish historians note that Penal-era discrimination ran on religion: a Catholic who conformed could escape it,
  which a racial status doesn't allow. That weakens the "undifferentiated status" test. The worker could not name a
  specific published review making this point; it needs sourcing.
- SOURCES:
  - Allen, *The Invention of the White Race*, vols. 1–2 (Verso 1994, 1997; 2nd ed. 2012). Not opened this session.
  - Kolchin 2002 and Arnesen 2001 (full citations at Q9-2). Not opened this session.
  - Vaughan, "The Origins Debate: Slavery and Racism in Seventeenth-Century Virginia," *VMHB* 97 (1989). Not opened.
- STATUS: CONTESTED (thesis); bibliographic details PROBABLE, from memory.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none. No Allen wording was read this session. His hallmark definition must be quoted from
  vol. 1, ch. 2–3, with a page number.
  **R6 update:** Allen's wording is now read via Bryan's NotebookLM pass: the hallmark definition (vol. 1 ch. 1
  p. 32; vol. 2 p. 239), racial vs national oppression (vol. 1 p. 273 n. 11) and "Irish history presents a case of
  racial oppression without reference to alleged skin color" (vol. 1 p. 22). See AL1–AL3 in
  `../quote-verification.md` and `r6-notebooklm.md`.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Present Allen's Irish analogy as an *argument* ("Allen calls this racial oppression"), not as
  settled fact. Pair it with the religion-not-race objection.

### Q9-2 — Ignatiev, *How the Irish Became White*, and the pushback
- CLAIM (brief): "The Irish became white by joining the oppression of others" → CONTESTED; Ignatiev at full strength,
  then the pushback (Roediger, Kolchin).
- FINDING (NOT RE-READ THIS SESSION): Noel Ignatiev, *How the Irish Became White* (New York: Routledge, 1995). Thesis:
  the Irish, an oppressed "race" in Ireland, arrived in America at the bottom of the labour market next to free Black
  people. They "became white" by taking part in anti-Black exclusion and violence and by entering the pro-slavery
  Democratic Party and the job trusts. They turned down O'Connell's 1841–42 abolitionist appeal (the book opens with
  the Irish Address). The key word is choice: whiteness was a privilege they took up, not something they always had.
  Pushback:
  - **Peter Kolchin, "Whiteness Studies: The New History of Race in America," *Journal of American History* 89, no. 1
    (June 2002): 154–173.** Sympathetic but critical. "Whiteness" is used loosely and with shifting meanings. The
    field tends to treat a single "whiteness" as the master key, plays down change over time, and makes claims about
    contemporaries' consciousness from thin evidence.
  - **Eric Arnesen, "Whiteness and the Historians' Imagination," *International Labor and Working-Class History* 60
    (Fall 2001): 3–32,** with responses in the same issue (incl. James Barrett, David Brody, Barbara J. Fields, Eric
    Foner, Victoria Hattam, Adolph Reed Jr.). Arnesen's sharpest point for this essay: whiteness scholars have shown
    no evidence that contemporaries thought the Irish *non-white*. Insults like "white negroes" and simian caricature
    were metaphors of contempt, not a racial classification. Also, the Irish voted, naturalized and held office as
    whites.
  - **Kevin Kenny**, *The American Irish: A History* (Harlow: Longman, 2000) and later essays: Irish-American
    anti-Black hostility has to be explained through labour competition, Democratic politics and Catholic anti-
    abolitionism (abolition was tied to evangelical Protestant nativism). "Becoming white" is a weaker frame. The
    Irish were "white" in law from the start; what they lacked was respectability, not whiteness. (Exact Kenny essay
    titles and pages not confirmed.)
  - **David Roediger**, *The Wages of Whiteness: Race and the Making of the American Working Class* (London: Verso,
    1991; rev. 1999, 2007). Takes W. E. B. Du Bois's "public and psychological wage" of whiteness (*Black
    Reconstruction*, 1935) and argues that white workers, the Irish prominent among them, took on whiteness as
    compensation for wage labour. Roediger stresses that Irish whiteness was *contested*, not absent. *Working Toward
    Whiteness: How America's Immigrants Became White* (New York: Basic Books, 2005) deals mainly with the 1890–1945
    "new immigrants." "Inbetween peoples" is from **James R. Barrett & David Roediger, "Inbetween Peoples: Race,
    Nationality and the 'New Immigrant' Working Class," *Journal of American Ethnic History* 16, no. 3 (Spring 1997):
    3–44**, and refers to southern and eastern Europeans, not the famine Irish.
  - Legal-status critique (see Q9-6): the Irish were never legally non-white. Thomas A. Guglielmo, *White on Arrival*
    (Oxford UP, 2003), makes the parallel argument for Italians: legal whiteness, alongside a racialized
    "Italian-ness." It's a useful model for the brief's legal-versus-social distinction.
- SOURCES: all of the above; none opened this session (budget). Journal volume, issue and page ranges are from the
  worker's memory and should be checked on JSTOR or the publisher's site.
- STATUS: CONTESTED (thesis); bibliography PROBABLE.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none verified. (Don't quote Ignatiev's or Kolchin's wording until it's read with a page
  number.)
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Use Arnesen's "metaphor, not classification" point as the main counterweight. Credit "wages of
  whiteness" to Du Bois via Roediger. Don't use "inbetween" for the 1840s Irish.

### Q9-3 — Matthew Frye Jacobson, *Whiteness of a Different Color*
- CLAIM (brief): Jacobson is an anchor on "graded European hierarchies."
- FINDING (NOT RE-READ THIS SESSION): *Whiteness of a Different Color: European Immigrants and the Alchemy of Race*
  (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1998). It divides American whiteness into three periods: (1) 1790 to
  the 1840s, a unified "free white persons" category (the 1790 Act); (2) the 1840s, starting with mass Irish famine
  migration, to the Johnson-Reed Act of 1924, when whiteness split into a hierarchy of "white races" (Celts,
  Anglo-Saxons, Teutons, Slavs, Hebrews, Iberics, Mediterraneans); (3) 1924 to the 1960s, when these merged into a
  single "Caucasian" race. The Celt is his first case study of a group that was legally white but racially
  "different." The phrase "variegated whiteness" is often linked to Jacobson. The worker can't confirm it's his exact
  wording; search the text before quoting it.
- SOURCES: Jacobson 1998 (Harvard UP). Not opened this session.
- STATUS: PROBABLE (periodization); UNVERIFIED (the phrase "variegated whiteness" as his).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Jacobson's model (legally white, racially graded) is the best scholarly support for the brief's
  "conditionally white" framing. It holds up better than Ignatiev's "became white."

### Q9-4 — John Beddoe, *The Races of Britain* (1885): the "index of nigrescence"
- CLAIM (brief): Beddoe's "Index of Nigrescence" scored "peoples of the British Isles by darkness"; the Irish were
  "measured for 'nigrescence'."
- FINDING (VERIFIED against the 1885 text):
  - **Edition:** *The Races of Britain: A Contribution to the Anthropology of Western Europe*, by John Beddoe, M.D.,
    F.R.S. Bristol: J. W. Arrowsmith; London: Trübner & Co., 1885. Internet Archive copies: `racesofbritainco00bedd`
    (University of Toronto, Robarts; 352 leaves) and `racesofbritainco1885bedd`.
  - **Definition (Ch. I, "On Methods," printed p. 5):** formula printed as **"D + 2N-R-F = Index."** Hair classes:
    R (red), F (fair), B (brown/chestnut, treated as neutral and left out), D (dark), N (black). "The gross index is
    gotten by subtracting the number of red and fair-haired persons" from the dark-haired, and the black-haired count
    is doubled: "I double the black, in order to give its proper value to the greater tendency to melanosity". A
    "net" or percentage index is derived from the gross. **Eye colour is not in the index.** Eyes are classed
    separately (p. 3): "I acknowledge three classes of eyes, distinguished as much by shade as by colour", namely
    "light, intermediate or neutral, and dark." On leaf 21 (the Toronto scan): "A ready means of comparing the colours
    of two peoples or localities is found in the Index of Nigrescence."
  - **Irish findings (Toronto scan; leaf numbers given, see page note below):**
    - Leaf 180: "The figures seem sufficient to prove that the Irish are, as a whole, considerably lighter in eye, but
      yet more considerably darker in hair, than the English as a whole."
    - Leaf 177: "Connaught has less of dark eyes and dark hair than any other province of Ireland"; "Connaught ranks
      second to Ulster as to lightness of eyes"; another Irish province (Munster, by context; confirm) "has more dark
      hair than any province of Ireland or of Great Britain, except Argyle."
    - Leaf 332: "Throughout the greater part of Ireland one distinct type of man decidedly predominates." The upper
      class of the Irish "are darker in eye, though considerably lighter in hair" than the lower. "Thus the indices of
      nigrescence of the upper, in Dublin and in Cork, were 14 and 2 1 -4 (see Irish table), and those of the lower
      class in the same cities respectively 33 and 34-2." (OCR; read 21.4 and 34.2.) A fetch summary of the
      surrounding text says Beddoe puts the upper class's lighter hair down to English ancestry among the landed and
      professional classes. That point is SECONDARY (a summary, not seen word for word).
    - Leaf 333: geography: "blonds are most numerous on and near the eastern coast, and brunets towards the west,"
      with dark-haired districts named in the far west (west Kerry, Clifden in Connemara, "Jar-Connaught," Dingle).
    - Comparison: leaf 326, in the Devon (England) section: "the index of nigrescence is at its maximum, exceeding 50."
      (By the fetch summary, this is the Dartmouth district.)
  - **"Africanoid" (Ch. II, "Prehistoric Races," printed pp. 10–12; leaves 26–29):** a skull and face type, not a
    hair index. Beddoe writes "it may be well, provisionally, to call it Africanoid"; "most of its lineaments are such
    as lead us to think of Africa as its possible birthplace"; and, by the fetch summary, "Ireland is apparently its
    present centre." He links it to prognathism in the Phoenix Park and Borris skulls, which "show the inclination to
    prognathism to be of remote date in Ireland," and says "In the West of Ireland I have frequently seen it". Per a
    fetch summary (not word for word), prominent mouths ran to about 20% of his Irish list against about 6% of the
    English (one summary also gave about 8% for the Welsh).
  - **Page note:** printed pp. 3, 5 and 10–12 come from the running headers of the `racesofbritainco1885bedd` OCR. The
    Irish findings were located by full-text search of the Toronto scan, which returns **leaf** numbers. On two checks
    (leaf 21 = p. 5; leaf 27 = p. 11) printed page = leaf − 16. Fold-out maps and plates may break that offset later
    in the book, so **confirm printed pages for leaves 177, 180, 326, 332 and 333 in the scan before citing.** (The
    attempt to fetch the item's `page_numbers.json` hit the budget limit.)
- SOURCES:
  - Beddoe 1885, Internet Archive `racesofbritainco1885bedd`, OCR text:
    https://archive.org/stream/racesofbritainco1885bedd/racesofbritainco1885bedd_djvu.txt (pp. 3, 5, 10–12)
  - Beddoe 1885, Internet Archive `racesofbritainco00bedd` (Toronto), full-text search:
    https://ia800705.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=racesofbritainco00bedd&doc=racesofbritainco00bedd&path=/2/items/racesofbritainco00bedd&q=nigrescence
    (and queries Africanoid, Connaught, Kerry, Ireland, Dublin, maximum, "considerably lighter", "exceeding 50",
    "one distinct type of man")
  - Metadata: https://archive.org/metadata/racesofbritainco00bedd
  - Second independent source: no secondary scholar was read this session. Curtis's *Apes and Angels* (rev. 1997) is
    the standard secondary discussion of Beddoe's "Africanoid" Irish; check its pages there.
- STATUS: VERIFIED (formula, hair-only scope, eye classes, the Irish lighter-eye/darker-hair conclusion, Dublin/Cork

### Q9-5 — Victorian racial science and the "Celt": Curtis, Gilley, Horsman, Knox
- CLAIM (brief): the craniometric and phrenological "Celtic skull"; the Punch bestiary.
- FINDING (NOT RE-READ THIS SESSION):
  - L. Perry Curtis Jr., *Anglo-Saxons and Celts: A Study of Anti-Irish Prejudice in Victorian England* (Bridgeport,
    CT: Conference on British Studies / University of Bridgeport, 1968); *Apes and Angels: The Irishman in Victorian
    Caricature* (Newton Abbot: David & Charles; Washington: Smithsonian, 1971; rev. ed. Smithsonian Institution
    Press, 1997). Thesis: from the 1860s (Fenianism) Punch, Judy, Fun and others drew "Paddy" as simian, drawing on
    Darwinian-era race science. The English saw the Irish as a racially separate, inferior "Celtic" type. The revised
    edition answers his critics and adds American and Irish counter-caricature.
  - Sheridan Gilley, "English Attitudes to the Irish in England, 1780–1900," in Colin Holmes (ed.), *Immigrants and
    Minorities in British Society* (London: George Allen & Unwin, 1978). Main critique: English hostility was mostly
    religious, political and class-based, not racial. The simian Irishman was mostly the *Fenian*, a political
    figure, and Punch's caricature also turned on English workers and others. Many stereotypes of the "Celt" were
    double-edged or even flattering (Matthew Arnold's poetic Celt). R. F. Foster, "Paddy and Mr Punch" (1991; essay
    collection 1993), extends the critique.
  - Reginald Horsman, *Race and Manifest Destiny: The Origins of American Racial Anglo-Saxonism* (Cambridge, MA:
    Harvard UP, 1981). Traces how an English-derived racial Anglo-Saxonism hardened in the US by the 1840s and cast
    the Celtic Irish as a lesser branch, alongside its main targets (Mexicans, Native Americans, Black Americans).
  - Robert Knox, *The Races of Men: A Fragment* (London: Henry Renshaw, 1850; enlarged 2nd ed. 1862). Knox's
    best-known line, "Race is everything", is reliably his. His anti-Celtic passages (the Celtic race as the source of
    Ireland's ills, calls for the Celt to be "forced from the soil") are widely quoted, but **no Knox wording was read
    this session.** Quote nothing until it's checked against the 1850 text on Internet Archive with a page number.

### Q9-6 — The Naturalization Act of 1790 and whether the Irish were ever legally non-white
- CLAIM (brief): the Irish "always qualified on paper"; the legal-versus-social distinction is load-bearing.
- FINDING (NOT RE-READ THIS SESSION): "An Act to establish an uniform Rule of Naturalization," 26 March 1790, 1 Stat.
  103 (1st Cong., 2nd sess., ch. 3). It admits "any alien, being a free white person" after two years' residence.
  The worker recalls this wording but didn't re-read it; check it against the *Statutes at Large* scan (Library of
  Congress, "A Century of Lawmaking"). **Answer: no.** No US statute or court decision ever classed the Irish as
  non-white. The racial prerequisite cases Ian Haney López covers in *White by Law: The Legal Construction of Race*
  (New York: NYU Press, 1996; rev. 10th-anniversary ed. 2006) run from *In re Ah Yup* (1878) to 1952. They involve
  Asian, South Asian, Middle Eastern, Mexican and mixed-race applicants, and no European group is among the
  petitioners refused. The Irish naturalized and voted in large numbers (the base of the Democratic machines).
  Barriers against them were *non-racial*: the Naturalization Act of 1798 (14-year residence, repealed 1802),
  aimed partly at radical Irish and French immigrants; and the Know-Nothing campaign of the 1850s for a 21-year
  residence term, which never became federal law.
- SOURCES: 1 Stat. 103 (1790); Haney López 1996/2006; Jacobson 1998 ch. 1 (on the 1790 Act). Not opened this session.
- STATUS: PROBABLE (the answer itself is the scholarly consensus. Even Ignatiev and Roediger don't claim legal
  non-whiteness; confirm the citations).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: the statutory phrase "free white person" (verify spelling against 1 Stat. 103).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: State it flatly: legally white from 1790; the barriers raised against the Irish were about
  residence and politics, not race. Then the social racialization.
