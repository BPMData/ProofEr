## Headline findings

- **B5 Peel — the quote is real but misquoted.** It is in a letter to Sir James Graham dated Oct. 13, 1845, printed in C.S. Parker, *Sir Robert Peel from his private papers*, vol. III, p. 223. As printed, it reads "Irish reports, that delay in acting **upon** them is always desirable." The brief has "acting **on** them" and no comma. Peel's own *Memoirs* (vol. II, J. Murray) prints the sentence too, with "upon" and no comma, followed by "; but I foresee…". The very next words in both editions are "But I foresee the necessity that may be imposed upon us…" (Parker) / "; but I foresee the necessity that may be imposed upon us at an early period of…" (Memoirs). Quoting the sentence alone, without that "but", makes Peel sound more dismissive than the letter does.
- **B4 Nicholls — wrong year and three wording errors.** The source is Nicholls's **First Report**, dated **London, November 15th, 1836**, addressed to Lord John Russell, ¶15, pp. 10–11 (in the HMSO 1838 collected *Poor Laws—Ireland: Three Reports*). It is not "1837". As printed it reads "a market to attend, a fair, **or** a funeral, a horse-race, a fight, or a wedding, all else is neglected **or** forgotten". The brief has "a fair or a funeral", "horse race" and "neglected **and** forgotten". The sentence also continues: "they hurry off in search of the excitements and the whiskey…"
- **B1 Malthus — still unverified (budget).** The brief's pack says it is "often cited from the *Essay*". The usual attribution is a private letter from Malthus to Ricardo, 17 Aug 1817, in Sraffa (ed.), *Works and Correspondence of David Ricardo*, vol. VII. Unchecked lead: the commonly cited fuller text goes on "…swept from the soil **into large manufacturing and commercial Towns**." If that is confirmed, cutting the sentence at "soil" changes its meaning, from moving people into towns to clearing them away. **Do not use the quote until the clause is checked.**
- **B9 Percival, B11 and B18 Nangle — unverified (no fetches possible).** Leads are listed below. For now, treat all three as unusable on the quotes wall.

### B5 — Peel, Oct 1845: "tendency to exaggeration and inaccuracy in Irish reports"
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): Sir Robert Peel, October 1845: "There is such a tendency to exaggeration and inaccuracy in Irish reports that delay in acting on them is always desirable."
- FINDING: Verified in Peel's letter **To Sir James Graham, Oct. 13, 1845**, printed in Parker vol. III, p. 223. The running head reads "1845 FAILURE OF POTATO CROP 223", which is scan leaf 239. The letter heading "To Sir James Graham. Oct. 13, 1845." sits on that page just above the passage (OCR box positions: heading top y=698, quote y=905–972). The same page also prints Graham's reply of Oct. 13 and Peel's next letter of Oct. 15. I read the passage through the Internet Archive full-text search of that volume, in pieces:
  - before the quote: "The accounts of the state of the potato crop in Ireland are becoming very alarming."
  - the quote: "There is such a tendency to exaggeration and inaccuracy in Irish reports, that delay in acting upon them is always desirable."
  - after it: "But I foresee the necessity that may be imposed upon us at a[n early period…]"

  Peel's *Memoirs* vol. II prints the same sentence on scan leaf 129; the printed page number was not read. Its wording: "There is such a tendency to exaggeration and inaccuracy in Irish reports that delay in acting upon them is always desirable ; but I foresee the necessity that may be imposed upon us at an early period of…". The words before it differ from Parker's: "…the Irish Government are very unsatisfactory. I presume that if the worst should happen which is predicted, the pressure would not be immediate." The Memoirs may print a fuller or differently cut text of the letter. I did not read the Memoirs letter heading before the budget ran out, so the date and recipient there are not confirmed from the Memoirs.
- SOURCES:
  - C.S. Parker (ed.), *Sir Robert Peel, from his private papers*, vol. III (London: John Murray, 1899), p. 223. Peel to Sir James Graham, Oct. 13, 1845. Internet Archive item `sirrobertpeelfro03peeluoft`, read through the full-text search: https://ia600101.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=sirrobertpeelfro03peeluoft&doc=sirrobertpeelfro03peeluoft&path=/21/items/sirrobertpeelfro03peeluoft&q=%22delay%20in%20acting%22 (plus the "very alarming", "Oct. 13, 1845" and heading queries).
  - *Memoirs by the Right Honourable Sir Robert Peel*, vol. II (London: J. Murray; IA metadata gives the date as 1856), scan leaf 129. Internet Archive item `memoirsbyrightho02peel`, read through the full-text search: https://ia600604.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=memoirsbyrightho02peel&doc=memoirsbyrightho02peel&path=/8/items/memoirsbyrightho02peel&q=%22delay%20in%20acting%22
- STATUS: VERIFIED (wording corrected)
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "There is such a tendency to exaggeration and inaccuracy in Irish reports, that delay in acting upon them is always desirable. But I foresee the necessity that may be imposed upon us…" (Peel to Sir James Graham, 13 Oct 1845, in C.S. Parker, *Sir Robert Peel from his private papers*, vol. III, 1899, p. 223.) Before publishing, check the punctuation once against the page image; the OCR spacing was irregular.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Use "upon", not "on". Honesty requires the "But I foresee…" clause, or a note that Peel goes straight on to plan for possible scarcity. He did act within weeks: he ordered the Indian-corn purchase in Nov 1845. The line works as evidence of official distrust of Irish reports, not as proof that Peel refused to act.

### B4 — George Nicholls: "a market to attend, a fair, or a funeral…"
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): George Nicholls, English Poor Law Commissioner, 1837: "If there be a market to attend, a fair or a funeral, a horse race, a fight or a wedding, all else is neglected and forgotten."
- FINDING: Verified in Nicholls's **First Report** on Poor Laws, Ireland, dated "London, November 15th, 1836". It is addressed to "The Right Honourable Lord John Russell, His Majesty's Principal Secretary of State for the Home Department". The passage is ¶15, starting on p. 10 (running head "Depression of Feeling and Condition") and running onto p. 11. In the table of contents it falls under "Depression of feeling and condition among the peasantry, 9–11". The full sentence, read in pieces from the OCR text:
  > "However urgent the demands upon them for exertion,—if, as in the present season, their crops are rotting in the fields, from excessive wet, and every moment of sunshine should be taken eager advantage of,—still, if there be a market to attend, a fair, or a funeral, a horse-race, a fight, or a wedding, all else is neglected or forgotten ; they hurry off in search of the excitements and the whiskey which abound on such occasions, and[,] with a recklessness hardly to be credited, at the moment that they are complaining most loudly of distress, they take[ ] the most certain steps for increasing it."

  The OCR showed "and;" and "take," at the two bracketed spots. These look like OCR errors; check them against the page image. The brief's "1837" probably reflects when the report was laid before Parliament. The report itself is dated 1836. I did not confirm the parliamentary paper number. Nicholls's *History of the Irish Poor Law* (1856, Project Gutenberg #56957) reprints much of this report. Gutenberg refused the fetch (robots unreachable), and the plain-text copy came back cut off, so that second printing was not checked.
- SOURCES:
  - George Nicholls, *Poor Laws—Ireland. Three Reports by George Nicholls, Esq.* (London: HMSO, 1838), First Report (15 Nov 1836), ¶15, pp. 10–11. Full text of the HathiTrust (University of Illinois) copy, served by Distant Reader: https://distantreader.org/stacks/trust/uiuc/uiuc_5386795.txt
  - (Not checked) George Nicholls, *A History of the Irish Poor Law, in connexion with the condition of the people* (London: John Murray, 1856), Project Gutenberg #56957: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/56957 (fetch truncated or refused).
- STATUS: VERIFIED (one primary printing read; date and wording corrected)
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "if there be a market to attend, a fair, or a funeral, a horse-race, a fight, or a wedding, all else is neglected or forgotten" (George Nicholls, First Report on Poor Laws, Ireland, to Lord John Russell, 15 Nov 1836, ¶15, p. 10; in *Poor Laws—Ireland: Three Reports*, HMSO, 1838).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Date it 1836, or "report of Nov 1836, presented 1837". Nicholls himself puts the "desultory habits" down partly to the insecurity of tiny holdings and uncertain wage work. Mention that if the essay uses this as an example of the stereotyping gaze, so the reader sees he offered a structural cause too.

- FINDING: Not checked this session (budget). The usual attribution is not the *Essay*. It is a private letter from Malthus to David Ricardo, dated 17 August 1817, printed in P. Unchecked lead, from memory, not read: the sentence is usually quoted in full as ending "…swept from the soil into large manufacturing and commercial Towns." If so, the brief's version cuts off a purpose clause that changes the meaning.
- SOURCES: none read.
- STATUS: UNVERIFIED
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none
