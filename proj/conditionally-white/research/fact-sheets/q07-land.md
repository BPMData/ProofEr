# Q07 — Land: the 60/14/5 series and the confiscation century

Fact sheet, research worker, 2026-11-24. Research stopped early: the shared web-search budget (200 calls) and web-fetch budget (400/hour) for this session both ran out partway through, and archive.org was refused by the proxy (HTTP 403). Items marked "not opened this session" carry candidate citations from the standard literature that a follow-up pass must open before anything ships.

## Headline findings (what changes or contradicts the brief)

1. **"60% → 14% → 5%" is a composite series, not one source's table.** The 1641 and 1703 points (59% and 14%) go back to J.G. Simms's work on the Williamite land settlement. Toby Barnard repeats them in the *Encyclopedia of Irish History and Culture*, and Britannica says "less than 15 percent" by 1703. The third point (about 5%) is a separate 18th-century estimate. One reference work says only "it has been calculated", and a review attributes it to S.J. Connolly's period. Other reference works give **3%** (Thomas P. Power gives 1776; the Down Survey site says the mid-18th century). **No source opened in this session ties "5%" to the year 1778.** Safer label: "about 5% (estimates 3–5%) by the 1770s".
2. **The 1641 baseline isn't settled.** Simms/Barnard give 59%; Lenihan (2016) says "over 60%". Trinity College's own Down Survey site gives **two different figures**. Its history page says "almost 70%" was held by Catholics before 1649. Its map page, which uses modern measurement, gives **42.2% Catholic, 42.1% Protestant, 3.8% common/unprofitable, 12.0% not in survey** for 1641. Present 1641 as "roughly three-fifths" and footnote the range.
3. **The brief's "1652 — ~80% of Ireland confiscated in one statute" is wrong as stated.** The "about 80 percent" figure (Power) describes land that "changed hands in the seventeenth century", across the whole century and not under one act. The 1652 Act for the Settlement of Ireland set out who would lose land. The transfers came through the 1653 Act of Satisfaction, the Down Survey (1656–58) and later acts. Careful statement: the Catholic share fell from about 59–60% (1641) to about 22% (1685/1688) after the Cromwellian and Restoration settlements (Simms 1951; History Ireland review).
4. **"To hell or to Connacht" has no contemporary attribution in any source opened here.** Neither the Down Survey history page nor Barnard's Cromwellian Conquest entry uses it. Don't attribute it to Cromwell. Present it as a later, popular phrase (UNVERIFIED origin; see Q07-1b).
5. **There is a 1688 data point between 1641 and 1703: about 22%.** It comes from Simms, "Land owned by Catholics in Ireland in 1688", *IHS* 7 (1951); one review gives the date as 1685. Contemporary estimates disagreed widely: Petty (1672) "slightly under a third" of good land, Lawrence (1682) "a bit over a fifth", Butler "one-seventh or one-eighth". The ledger could show 4 bars (1641 / 1688 / 1703 / 1770s) to keep its honesty.
6. **Converts undercount Catholic interest in land.** Power calls the 14%→3% decline "misleading" because it leaves out converts who stayed Catholic in allegiance, and because Catholic leaseholders grew richer. The Ulster Historical Foundation notes 1,878 registered converts between 1702 and 1752. Cullen (1986) and Whelan (1995) argue the penal-law impact has been overstated (Whelan's "underground gentry" of Catholic middlemen). This caveat has to sit next to the 5% bar.
7. **1829 electorate: "216,000 → 37,000" has a secondary chain only, and figures differ.** The chain is Foster, *Modern Ireland* pp. 301–302, via a Wikipedia footnote, and an unpublished Colin Barr manuscript. The Wikipedia article on the 1829 Act itself says about 215,000 → about 40,000. Show it as "about 216,000 to roughly 37,000–40,000 county voters".
8. **The 1704 Popery Act statute citation is 2 Anne c.6 (Irish). Its session is 1703–04, so sources date it "1703" or "1704".** The 1709 act is 8 Anne c.3. In 1695, arms = 7 Will. III c.5 and education = 7 Will. III c.4. All of these were checked against the University of Minnesota Law Library's Irish penal-laws collection. The brief's provisions (no purchase, gavelkind unless the eldest son conforms, 31-year leases) are correct.

### Q07-0a — Catholic land share, 1641 ≈ 60%
- CLAIM: 1641 ≈ 60% of Irish land held by Catholics.
- FINDING: The most-repeated scholarly figure is **59%** (Simms → Barnard). Lenihan (2016) says "over 60%". The Down Survey project gives conflicting numbers on its own pages: "almost 70%" in the history text, and 42.2% Catholic, 42.1% Protestant and 12% "not in survey" on the modern-measurement map. The 42% figure is a share of *all* land, including the unsurveyed 12% and common land. As a share of land with a known owner it is about 50%. That is still well below 59%. The traditional figures are generally understood to be shares of *profitable* acres from the Books of Survey and Distribution (PROBABLE; Simms's methodology not opened this session).

  - T.C. Barnard, "Planters and Policies in Cromwellian Ireland", *Past and Present* 61 (1973), p. 32: 59%. SECONDARY: the citation was seen on Lancaster University's "Troubled Geographies" ch. 2 page, which quotes "In 1641 Catholics still owned 59 percent of Ireland's land." https://lancaster.ac.uk/troubledgeogs/chap2.htm

### Q07-0e — 1778 ≈ 5%
- CLAIM: 1778 ≈ 5%.
- FINDING: "About 5 per cent… in the 1770s" appears in the Ulster Historical Foundation's statute background as an unattributed "it has been calculated". The History Ireland review summarises the 18th-century decline as "14 to 5 per cent", in a paragraph about Connolly's argument. That review also says the transfer happened "largely through conformities to the established church, many of which were purely formal". Power gives **3% in 1776** and calls the statistic misleading. ### Q07-1a — 1652 Act for the Settlement of Ireland / "~80% confiscated"
- CLAIM: "1652 — Cromwell's Act of Settlement: ~80% of Ireland confiscated in one statute."
- FINDING: The TCD Down Survey history page dates the (English) Act of Settlement to 1652 and the Act of Satisfaction to September 1653. The exact date (12 Aug 1652) and the statute's text were not opened this session (candidate: Firth & Rait, *Acts and Ordinances of the Interregnum*, vol. 2, via British History Online).
- SOURCES: https://downsurvey.tchpc.tcd.ie/history.html ; Barnard, encyclopedia.com (above) ; Power, encyclopedia.com: "About 80 percent of the land of Ireland changed hands in the seventeenth century." ; History Ireland review (above).

- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "About 80 percent of the land of Ireland changed hands in the seventeenth century." (Thomas P. Power, *Encyclopedia of Irish History and Culture*, via encyclopedia.com)

### Q07-2 — Penal Laws: land, arms, education statutes
- CLAIM: no purchase; gavelkind unless a son converts; leases capped at 31 years; 1695 arms and education acts.
- FINDING (all from the statute abstracts on the University of Minnesota Law Library's Irish penal-laws site, compiled by M. Patricia Schaffer, © 2000, and the Ulster Historical Foundation):
  - **7 Will. III c.4 (1695), "An Act to Restrain foreign Education"**: penalties for sending children to Catholic schools abroad. Sec. 9: "no person of the popish religion shall publicly teach school or instruct youth", with a £20 fine and three months' prison.
  - **7 Will. III c.5 (1695), "An Act for the better securing the government, by disarming papists"**: arms to be surrendered by 1 March 1696. Sec. 10: "No papist shall be capable of having or keeping for his use, any horse, gelding or mare of five pounds value." Gentlemen covered by the Articles of Limerick were exempt.
  - **1 Anne c.26 (1702)**: Catholics disabled from purchasing or leasing forfeited estates.
  - **2 Anne c.6 (1703/4), "An Act to prevent the further Growth of Popery"**: Sec. 6 "Every papist shall be disabled to purchase any lands"; leases no longer than 31 years at a rent not less than two-thirds of the improved yearly value. Sec. 10: gavelkind, meaning the land of a papist descends equally to all sons. Sec. 12: if the eldest son is Protestant at the father's death, the land descends to him under common law. Sec. 7–8: a Catholic heir must conform within six months or the nearest Protestant relation takes the land. Also covers residence bars (Limerick, Galway) and an oath required for voting (UHF).
  - **8 Anne c.3 (1709)**: explains and tightens 2 Anne c.6. Annuities charged on land are barred; evasive settlements void; Protestant "discoverers" can sue; registered priests must take the oath of abjuration; £10 reward for discovering popish schoolmasters.
  - Relaxations: **11 & 12 Geo. III c.31 (1772)** allowed 61-year leases for bog reclamation. **17 & 18 Geo. III c.49 (1778, Gardiner's Act)** allowed leases up to 999 years and made Catholic land "descendable, deviseable, and transferable", from 1 August 1778. The Irish Statute Book titles it "Leases for Lives Act 1777". **21 & 22 Geo. III c.24 (1782)** allowed Catholics to acquire land except in parliamentary boroughs. **1 Geo. II c.9 (1727)** removed the Catholic vote; it was restored in 1793.
  - Scholarship: J.G. Simms, "The making of a penal law (2 Anne, c.6), 1703–4", *IHS* 12:46 (Oct 1960), pp. 105–118, doi:10.1017/S0021121400019660, calls it "the most notorious of the penal laws".
- SOURCES: https://librarycollections.law.umn.edu/irishlaws/land.html ; https://librarycollections.law.umn.edu/irishlaws/education.html ; https://librarycollections.law.umn.edu/irishlaws/weapons.html ; https://ulsterhistoricalfoundation.com/irish-parliament/background-to-statutes/religion ; https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/1777/act/49/enacted/en/html ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/irish-historical-studies/article/abs/making-of-a-penal-law-2-anne-c6-17034/BFF887B070C7357A2D7A26C2AA2E9E45
- STATUS: VERIFIED (statute citations and provisions, from statute abstracts; the printed *Statutes at Large (Ireland)* page was not opened).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "Every papist shall be disabled to purchase any lands" (2 Anne c.6, s.6, as abstracted by the University of Minnesota Law Library's Irish penal laws collection). "No papist shall be capable of having or keeping for his use, any horse, gelding or mare of five pounds value." (7 Will. III c.5, s.10, same collection; this is the five-pound horse, a possible link to the "one stolen horse" motif.)
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The brief's "no inheritance of land" should read "no undivided inheritance": estates were gavelled among sons unless the eldest conformed. "No education" = no Catholic schoolteaching and no schooling abroad. Note that the 1695 horse clause exists if the horse motif wants a legal footnote.

### Q07-4 — Statutes of Iona (1609)
- CLAIM: The Scottish crown legislated the Highland Gaels into Protestantism in 1609.
- FINDING: Not verified this session (budget exhausted before the fetch). Standard account, not opened: agreed on Iona in August 1609 by Andrew Knox, Bishop of the Isles, with Hebridean chiefs. The nine statutes covered support for Protestant ministers and churches, inns, limits on chiefs' households and on "sorning" (free quartering), restrictions on wine and whisky imports, education of chiefs' heirs in the Lowlands in English, bans on firearms, and bards. Scholars note it was as much about chiefly control and order as religion. Candidate citations to open: Julian Goodare, "The Statutes of Iona in context", *Scottish Historical Review* 77 (1998); Martin MacGregor, "The Statutes of Iona: text and context", *Innes Review* 57 (2006). Volume and page details are unverified.
- SOURCES: none opened.
- STATUS: UNVERIFIED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: "Legislating the Gaels into Protestantism" overstates it. Goodare's reading (as generally summarised) stresses Crown control of chiefs. Phrase it as "binding Hebridean chiefs to the Kirk, Lowland schooling for their heirs, and Crown order". Verify before use.
