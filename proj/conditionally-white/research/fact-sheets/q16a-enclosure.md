# Q16a — The enclosure file (enclosure as a "system" across Britain and Ireland)

Research worker fact sheet. Compiled 2026-11-24. **Tool limits hit:** the session-wide web-search budget and the shared web-fetch budget both ran out partway through this batch, and the proxy refused direct downloads from archive.org, gutenberg.org and thelandmagazine.org.uk by shell. The fetch tool also cuts long book pages at about 125,000 characters, so the later chapters of Hammond, *The Village Labourer*, and of Young's *Autobiography* (both on Project Gutenberg) could not be reached. Several items are therefore SECONDARY or UNVERIFIED. Each one names what is still needed. Anything marked "background knowledge" was **not** read this session and must be checked before use.

## Headline findings
- **"About 7 million acres / about 4,000 acts / 1760–1870 / one sixth of England"** is Simon Fairlie's (*The Land* 7, 2009, p.25ff), and he footnotes it to **Gilbert Slater (1913)**, not to Turner. "One sixth" does not fit "7 million acres": England is about 32 million acres, so 7M is about 21–22%. The figures that are better sourced read: **6.8 million acres, "just over a fifth" of England, over 5,200 enclosure Bills 1604–1914** (UK Parliament, Living Heritage), and **"over 7m acres, around 21 per cent of the area of England"** (Overton 1996, p.62, via Whyte 2003). The Tate–Turner *Domesday* database counts **5,383 acts**. Do **not** print "one sixth".
- **The "0.6% owned 98.5%" line** traces to **Kevin Cahill, *Who Owns Britain* (2001), p.30**, as cited by Fairlie (p.27). I could not check Cahill's own basis. For **Ireland**, the *Spectator* review of the 1876 Irish Return (1 July 1876) gives **32,614 owners of more than one acre out of a population of 5,409,435**, which is **≈0.6%**. All owners, including those under one acre, numbered **68,758 (≈1.3%)**. The brief's "~20,000 owners" and "~3% of population" do **not** match the Return as reported. "~750 owners held half the land" is UNVERIFIED.
- **Young's "nineteen out of twenty" quote:** the brief's exact wording ("by nineteen out of twenty Inclosure Bills the poor are injured, and most grossly") was **not found in any source**. Watch for a mix-up: **"in nineteen cases out of twenty, Committees of this House sitting on private Bills neglected the rights of the poor"** is **Lord Lincoln in the Commons, 1 May 1845** (Hansard vol 80 c25), which the Hammonds quote. Do not attribute that line to Young.
- **"Suppression" is too strong.** What Young himself documents (*Autobiography*, 1898) is that the Board's committee sat on his 1801 paper for a month and then told him he "might do what I pleased with it for myself, but not print it as a work for the Board" (as quoted by Fairlie). He then published it under his own name in 1801. "Refused to publish it as a Board paper" is accurate. "Suppressed his findings" is not.
- **"The rich crowded their beasts on, and literally eat out the poor"** is attributed by Fairlie (p.30) to **E.C.K. Gonner, *Common Land and Inclosure* (1912)**, pp.306/337. These are not Neeson's words, not E.P. Thompson's, and not known to be from an 18th-century pamphlet. I could not check whether Gonner was quoting an earlier writer.
- **Tate's "almost monotonous in their reiteration"** is real as a quotation in Fairlie (p.25: "Tate observes: …"). It refers to the **"ninety odd volumes"** of the Board of Agriculture's county reports. Fairlie's note points to Tate, *The English Village Community and the Enclosure Movements* (1967). Tate's own page is UNVERIFIED.
- **Hardin's retraction:** "The title of my 1968 paper should have been 'The Tragedy of the Unmanaged Commons'" is cited by Fairlie to **Hardin, "The Tragedy of the 'Unmanaged' Commons", in R.V. Andelson (ed.), *Commons Without Tragedy* (Shepheard-Walwyn, 1991)**. A bibliography also lists **Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Unmanaged Commons", *TREE* 9(5): 199 (1994)**. Wikipedia paraphrases a later remark (Hardin 1998) as "unregulated". "The most cited paper in *Science*" is **UNVERIFIED**. The documented claim is narrower: a survey found it the most frequently cited of the works with the greatest career impact on biologists (Barrett & Mabry 2002, via van Laerhoven & Ostrom 2007).

### Q16a-1 — Parliamentary enclosure totals
- CLAIM: ~7M acres, ~4,000 acts, 1760–1870, ~1/6 or ~21% of England.
- FINDING: There are several numbers in circulation, and they depend on the period and on what is counted (acts or bills, England or England and Wales, open field plus waste).
  - **Fairlie 2009** (*The Land* 7, p.25ff): between 1760 and 1870 "about 7 million acres (about one sixth the area of England) were changed … from common land to enclosed land" by about 4,000 acts. Footnote 37: G. Slater, "Historical Outline of Land Ownership in England", in *The Land: The Report of the Land Enquiry Committee* (Hodder & Stoughton, 1913). The "one sixth" is internally inconsistent: 7M of England's ~32M acres is ~21–22%.
  - **UK Parliament, Living Heritage ("Enclosing the land")**: "over 5,200 enclosure Bills were enacted by Parliament" between 1604 and 1914, enclosing "some 6.8 million acres", "just over a fifth of the total area of England". 6.8M acres is the figure usually credited to Turner (1980). I could not open Turner 1980 to confirm the exact figure and page.
  - **Overton, *Agricultural Revolution in England* (CUP 1996), p.62**, as cited by I. Whyte, *Rural History* 14(1) 2003, p.21 and note 1: "Over 7m acres, around 21 per cent of the area of England, was affected".
  - **Tate & Turner, *A Domesday of English Enclosure Acts and Awards* (Reading, 1978)**: the database "records 5,383 acts and covers the universe of Parliamentary enclosure" (Heldring, Robinson & Vollmer, Hoover LRP WP 22003, 2022, p.17).
  - **Turner, "Parliamentary Enclosures: Gains and Costs", *ReFRESH* 3 (Economic History Society, Autumn 1986)**: "18 per cent was enclosed by parliament in two short bursts of activity each of about 20 years duration" (the 1760s–70s and the French Revolutionary/Napoleonic wars). The main span is 1750–1830.
  - **Wordie, "The Chronology of English Enclosure, 1500–1914", *EcHR* 2nd ser. 36(4) 1983, pp.483–505**, as summarised in Turner's *ReFRESH* piece. Shares of England's area: enclosed by 1500 c.45%; enclosed 1500–99 c.2%; 1600–99 c.24%; 1700–99 c.13%; 1800–1914 c.11.4%; still common in 1914 c.4.6%. Wordie's point is that the **17th century**, not the parliamentary era, saw the most enclosure.
- SOURCES:
  - UK Parliament, "Enclosing the land", Living Heritage, https://webtest.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/towncountry/landscape/overview/enclosingland
  - M. Turner, "Parliamentary Enclosures: Gains and Costs", *ReFRESH* 3 (1986), https://files.ehs.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/29061004/Turner3b.pdf (no page numbers in the file)
  - I. Whyte, "'Wild, Barren and Frightful' – Parliamentary Enclosure in an Upland County: Westmorland 1767–1890", *Rural History* 14(1) 2003, pp.21–38, https://core.ac.uk/download/71296.pdf (p.21, note 1 → Overton 1996 p.62)
  - Heldring, Robinson & Vollmer, "The Economic Effects of the English Parliamentary Enclosures", Hoover Institution LRP WP 22003 (4 Mar 2022), p.17, https://hoover-s3-website.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/research/docs/lrp_wp_22003_sm.pdf
  - S. Fairlie, "A Short History of Enclosure in Britain", *The Land* 7 (Summer 2009), https://www.thelandmagazine.org.uk/articles/short-history-enclosure-britain (and the PDF of the issue)
  - Not opened: Turner 1980 and 1984; Tate 1967 and 1978 (database only, via Heldring et al.); Wordie 1983 (via Turner only).
- STATUS: PROBABLE for "about 6.8–7 million acres, about a fifth (≈21%) of England, over 5,000 acts, mostly 1750–1830". CONTESTED/UNUSABLE for "one sixth". "~4,000 acts 1760–1870" is SECONDARY (Fairlie via Slater 1913).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "Over 7m acres, around 21 per cent of the area of England, was affected" (Whyte 2003, p.21, citing Overton 1996, p.62). "18 per cent was enclosed by parliament in two short bursts of activity each of about 20 years duration" (Turner, *ReFRESH* 3, 1986).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Use "roughly 7 million acres, about a fifth of England, by more than 5,000 private Acts, most of them 1750–1830". Note in "About these numbers" that the counts differ: some count acts, some bills, and the period varies. If a chart needs it, Wordie's chronology gives the longer arc.

### Q16a-2 — Return of Owners of Land ("New Domesday") concentration figures
- CLAIM: "0.6% of the population owned 98.5% of the agricultural land"; "4,000 people owned 44%"; Ireland ~750 owners held half the land, ~20,000 owners, ~3% of the population.
- FINDING:
  - **"0.6% / 98.5%"**: Fairlie, *The Land* 7, p.27, in his own words: "0.6 per cent of the population owned 98.5 per cent of the agricultural land". He attributes it to the 1872 Return and cites Kevin Cahill, *Who Owns Britain* (Canongate 2001), p.30 (fn 44). I did not read Cahill, so I cannot say which population, which return (England and Wales only, or the UK) or which land base he used. SECONDARY. Use with that caveat, or not at all.
  - **"4,000 people owned 44%"**: no source found in this session. UNVERIFIED.
  - **Bateman**: his 1883 4th edition carries the title *The Great Landowners of Great Britain and Ireland: a list of all owners of three thousand acres and upwards … also one thousand three hundred owners of two thousand acres and upwards* (London: Harrison), pp. v–vi, per the Wikipedia article's note. I did not open the book.
  - **IRELAND, 1876 Return**: *Land owners in Ireland: return of owners of land of one acre and upwards in the several counties …*, Local Government Board for Ireland, HMSO, C.1492, Sessional Papers 1876, vol. 80, viii + 325 pp. (EPPI record). The summary tables are not online in that record. A contemporary review in the *Spectator* (1 July 1876, p.2) reports: "68,758 persons out of 5,409,435 own any land at all"; "only 32,614 have more than an acre"; the rest held "only 9,065 acres, chiefly house-property". In Connaught, "2,941 own 4,188,207 acres". So owners of 1 acre or more were **≈0.60%** of the population, and all owners **≈1.27%**. The reviewer also says the data were old (valuation figures about 25 years out of date). An earlier Irish return exists: "Return dated the 7th March 1872, No. 167", moved for by the member for Westmeath (HC Deb 6 July 1876 vol 230 cc1047–8).
  - "~750 owners held half the land": UNVERIFIED. It is widely repeated, but I found no source this session. The figure to try for next is in W.E. Vaughan, *Landlords and Tenants in Mid-Victorian Ireland* (1994), or in the Return's own summary tables.
  - "~20,000 owners" and "~3% of population" are **not supported**. The Return as reported gives 32,614 owners of 1 acre or more (≈0.6%) and 68,758 owners in all (≈1.3%).
- SOURCES:
  - *Spectator*, 1 July 1876, p.2, "The Irish Domesday Book has appeared…", https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/1st-july-1876/2/the-irish-domesday-book-has-appeared-but-it-is-so (the OCR is partly garbled; only clean passages are quoted)
  - EPPI/DIPPAM record of C.1492 (1876), https://www.dippam.ac.uk/eppi/documents/16252
  - HC Deb 6 July 1876 vol 230 cc1047–8, https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1876/jul/06/question-3
  - Fairlie, *The Land* 7 (2009), p.27, citing Cahill 2001, p.30
  - Wikipedia, "Return of Owners of Land, 1873" (a lead only; it gives Bateman's 1883 title, pp. v–vi, and Lindert, "Who Owned Victorian England?", *Agricultural History* 61(4) 1987, pp.25–51), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_Owners_of_Land,_1873
- STATUS: Ireland 32,614 / 68,758 / 5,409,435 is SECONDARY: a contemporary review of the primary Return, which still needs checking against the C.1492 summary table. "0.6/98.5" is SECONDARY (Cahill via Fairlie). "750 own half", "4,000 own 44%", "~20,000" and "~3%" are UNVERIFIED or contradicted.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "68,758 persons out of 5,409,435 own any land at all" … "only 32,614 have more than an acre" (*Spectator*, 1 July 1876, p.2).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: For Ireland, the clean line is "about 32,600 people, roughly 0.6% of the population, owned all of Ireland's land holdings of one acre or more" (check against the Return's own table before final). Also see Lindert 1987 for the scholarly debate over how concentrated landed wealth really was.

### Q16a-3 — Board of Agriculture county reports and Tate's "almost monotonous" remark
- CLAIM: the Board's county reports (1790s–1810s) and W.E. Tate's remark that they are "almost monotonous in their reiteration".
- FINDING: Fairlie (*The Land* 7, p.25) introduces it with "Tate observes:" and quotes: "The ninety odd volumes are almost monotonous in their reiteration of the point that agricultural improvement … has come through enclosure and that more enclosure must take place." His fn 35 cites "Curtler, op cit 10; Tate op cit 17", and adds that "a pro-enclosure summary of the General Views can be found on pp224-252 of Lord Ernle, *English Farming Past and Present*, 1912". Fairlie's Tate entry is W.E. Tate, *The English Village Community and the Enclosure Movements* (Gollancz, 1967); his fn 16 gives pp.122–125 for a different point. The page in Tate for "monotonous" is not given, and I did not see the original. Fairlie also quotes a description of the Board as a body of gentlemen, mostly landowners, funded by government rather than a government department, and he calls the reports state propaganda (paraphrased; not checked word for word).
- SOURCES: Fairlie 2009, p.25 (the Land Magazine article page and the issue PDF). Original: Tate 1967, page unknown.
- STATUS: SECONDARY (the quote is real in Fairlie and attributed to Tate; Tate's page is UNVERIFIED).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "The ninety odd volumes are almost monotonous in their reiteration of the point that agricultural improvement … has come through enclosure and that more enclosure must take place." (W.E. Tate, as quoted in S. Fairlie, "A Short History of Enclosure in Britain", *The Land* 7, 2009, p.25). The ellipsis is in the fetched text, so the omitted words are unknown. Get the full sentence from Tate before printing.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Usable as "the historian W.E. Tate called the reports 'almost monotonous'…", but only with Tate's page checked. Until then, cite it as quoted in Fairlie.

### Q16a-4 — Arthur Young 1801 and the alleged suppression
- CLAIM: Young's 1801 *Inquiry into the Propriety of Applying Wastes to the Better Maintenance and Support of the Poor* said "by nineteen out of twenty Inclosure Bills the poor are injured, and most grossly", and the Board of Agriculture suppressed his findings.
- FINDING:
  - **The quote**: the brief's wording turned up in none of the sources reached. Searches on it returned only the 1845 debate. Lord Lincoln, introducing the General Inclosure Bill, said "in nineteen cases out of twenty, Committees of this House sitting on private Bills neglected the rights of the poor" (HC Deb 1 May 1845 vol 80 c25; the Hammonds quote it in *The Village Labourer*, ch. 2/3). Background knowledge, not read this session: the Hammonds quote Young's 1801 *Inquiry* (also printed in *Annals of Agriculture* vol. 36) roughly as "by nineteen Inclosure Bills in twenty they are injured, in some grossly injured". This must be checked against the *Inquiry* or *Annals* text before any wording is used.
  - **Suppression**: Fairlie (p.25), citing Young's *Autobiography* (ed. M. Betham-Edwards, 1898; repr. A.M. Kelley 1967): Young presented the 1801 report to the Board's committee. The committee sat on it for about a month and then "told me I might do what I pleased with it for myself, but not print it as a work for the Board." The Hammonds' preface quotes Young's diary for 14 July 1806: "I have been reading over my Inquiry into the Propriety of applying Wastes to the better Maintenance of the Poor. … Thank God I wrote it, for though it never had the smallest effect except in exciting opposition and ridicule…". So the documented fact is that the Board **refused to issue it as a Board publication**. Young published it himself (1801). Calling that "suppression of findings" overstates it. Background, unverified: the Board did later issue Young's *General Report on Enclosures* (1808).
- SOURCES:
  - HC Deb 1 May 1845 vol 80 cc23–31 (Lincoln at c25), https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1845/may/01/enclosures-of-commons-and-waste-lands
  - J.L. & B. Hammond, *The Village Labourer 1760–1832* (new ed. 1920), Preface and ch. 2, https://historyofeconomicthought.mcmaster.ca/hammond/village.html (partial text)
  - Fairlie 2009, p.25, citing Young, *Autobiography* (1898)
  - Not reachable this session: *Autobiography* ch. XIV ("The appropriation of waste lands", p.347 per the Gutenberg table of contents, 1801–03), https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/73426 (the fetch was cut before the chapter); Young's 1801 *Inquiry* itself
- STATUS: The refusal to print it as a Board work is SECONDARY: Young's own words, quoted by Fairlie, with the *Autobiography* page still to confirm (ch. XIV, c. p.347). The "suppressed findings" framing is CONTESTED/overstated. The brief's "nineteen out of twenty … most grossly" wording is UNVERIFIED and probably a misquotation.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "…told me I might do what I pleased with it for myself, but not print it as a work for the Board." (Arthur Young, *Autobiography*, 1898, as quoted in Fairlie 2009, p.25). "Thank God I wrote it, for though it never had the smallest effect except in exciting opposition and ridicule…" (Young, diary, 14 July 1806, in *Autobiography*, quoted in Hammond, *Village Labourer*, Preface).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Say "the Board would not publish it under its name", not "suppressed". Do not print a "nineteen out of twenty" line from Young until it has been checked in the *Inquiry* or *Annals* vol. 36. Never attribute Lincoln's 1845 line to Young.

### Q16a-5 — Neeson: cow value, Otmoor geese, Otmoor riots, "rich crowded their beasts"
- CLAIM: Neeson estimates a cow on the common was worth about half a labourer's wage; geese on Otmoor were worth about a year's income; the Otmoor riots of 1830; the source of "The rich crowded their beasts on, and literally eat out the poor".
- FINDING:
  - **Cow**: Fairlie, p.25ff: "According to J M Neeson a poor cow providing a gallon of milk per day in season brought in half the equivalent of a labourer's annual wage." Fn 62: "Neeson, op cit 28 pp 165, 311 and passim". Fairlie's reference 10 cites Neeson, *Commoners* (CUP 1993) p.156. I did not read Neeson directly.
  - **Otmoor geese**: Fairlie's main text says "Geese at Otmoor could bring in the equivalent of a full time wage". His box (p.26), drawing on the Hammonds: "a cottager could sometimes clear £20 a year from running geese there". This comes from the Hammonds and Fairlie, **not** a Neeson page cite. I did not find a Neeson page for geese.
  - **Otmoor riots**: Fairlie's box (p.26, from the Hammonds): armed resistance to the enclosure proposals of 1801 and 1814; in 1830 a crowd pulled down the fences; 44 rioters were arrested and escaped during St Giles' fair. (Paraphrased from the fetch; check the Hammonds' text for wording and numbers.)
  - **"The rich crowded their beasts on, and literally eat out the poor"**: Fairlie, p.30, presents it as the end of a passage attributed to **E.C.K. Gonner, *Common Land and Inclosure* (Macmillan, 1912)**. Fn 59: "Gonner, op ci 56 p337 and p306; Neeson, op cit 10, pp86 and 156; Thirsk …". The words are not Neeson's and not Thompson's. I could not check whether Gonner was himself quoting a contemporary, and a web search on the exact phrase returned nothing relevant.
- SOURCES: Fairlie 2009, pp.25–26 and 30. Hammond, *Village Labourer* (Otmoor account, ch. on enclosure; not reached past ch. 2). Not opened: Neeson 1993; Gonner 1912.
- STATUS: Cow ≈ half a wage is SECONDARY (Neeson via Fairlie; pages 156/165/311 to confirm). Geese ≈ a full wage / £20 a year is SECONDARY (Hammond via Fairlie). Otmoor 1830 is SECONDARY. "Rich crowded…" is SECONDARY (Gonner via Fairlie; pp.306/337 to confirm).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "The rich crowded their beasts on, and literally eat out the poor." (E.C.K. Gonner, *Common Land and Inclosure*, 1912, as quoted in Fairlie, *The Land* 7, 2009, p.30).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The Gonner line is evidence that the rich overstocked commons. It is an early-20th-century historian's summary, not a contemporary voice. Label it that way. Hedge the cow and geese values as estimates that vary by place and decade.

### Q16a-6 — Hardin, Lloyd, the "unmanaged commons", citation claims, Ostrom, critiques, Fairlie
- CLAIM: Hardin, *Science* 162 (13 Dec 1968); Lloyd 1833; Hardin's later "unmanaged commons" qualification; "most cited paper in *Science*"; Ostrom 1990; Angus and Fairlie critiques; Fairlie, *The Land* 7 (Summer 2009).
- FINDING:
  - Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons", *Science* 162: 1243–1248 (1968): pages confirmed via the CCRI reference list. The 13 December 1968 date (issue 3859) is from background knowledge and was not read this session.
  - Lloyd: *Two Lectures on the Checks to Population* (Oxford, 1833), per Wikipedia. Note that the CCRI page miscites it as "Two letters".
  - The "unmanaged" retraction: Fairlie quotes "The title of my 1968 paper should have been 'The Tragedy of the Unmanaged Commons'", with the context "Clearly the background of the resources discussed by Lloyd (and later by myself) was one of non-management … under conditions of scarcity" (the ellipsis is the fetch's). His fn 9: Garrett Hardin, "The Tragedy of the 'Unmanaged' Commons", in R.V. Andelson (ed.), *Commons Without Tragedy* (Shepheard-Walwyn, 1991). Separately, a bibliography lists Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Unmanaged Commons", *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 9(5): 199 (1994). The same page attributes "Under conditions of scarcity, where the number of players is not small, an unmanaged commons inevitably ends in ruin" to Hardin 1988 (perhaps "Commons Failing", *New Scientist* 120(1635), 22 Oct 1988, p.76). That is SECONDARY and unconfirmed. Wikipedia paraphrases Hardin 1998 as saying he should have titled it "The Tragedy of the Unregulated Commons". So the 1991/1994 wording is "Unmanaged", and the 1998 paraphrase is "Unregulated".
  - Citation claim: Wikipedia cites van Laerhoven & Ostrom 2007 (*IJC* 1(1): 3–28) reporting that the 1968 article "was the one having the greatest career impact on biologists and is the most frequently cited", based on Barrett & Mabry 2002 (*BioScience* 52(3): 282). That is a survey of biologists, **not** a ranking of *Science* papers. "The most cited paper in *Science*" is UNVERIFIED and should not be printed.
  - Ostrom, *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* (CUP 1990): confirmed in the CCRI references. CCRI says the claim that all commons end in ruin is "patently false". Ostrom won the 2009 Nobel economics prize (Wikipedia). Another critique named on Wikipedia: Susan Jane Buck Cox ("triumph of the commons"; full citation not on the page; background: "No Tragedy on the Commons", *Environmental Ethics* 7, 1985). Ian Angus: not verified this session.
  - Fairlie, "A Short History of Enclosure in Britain", *The Land* issue 7 (Summer 2009): VERIFIED on the magazine's site and in the issue PDF. His key claims: ~7M acres and ~4,000 acts 1760–1870 (after Slater); Hardin's commons was really an unmanaged, open-access one; overstocking was done by rich commoners (Gonner); a commoner's cow was worth about half a labourer's wage (Neeson); the Board's reports were state propaganda (Tate); Young's 1801 report was refused as a Board publication; the Return shows 0.6% owning 98.5% (Cahill); the Otmoor resistance. The same issue carries his "The Tragedy of Private Property" piece, which uses "nearly half the country is owned by 40,000 land millionaires", the Black Act and the Highland Clearances.
- SOURCES: Fairlie (magazine site and issue PDF); CCRI, "The real 'tragedy of the commons'" (undated, c.2018), https://ccri.ac.uk/?p=16032; Wikipedia, "Tragedy of the commons" (lead only), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commons_dilemma; notes page listing the Hardin bibliography (lead only), https://sites.google.com/site/notesbyhk/short/ghardin-on-commons; Fairlie, "The Tragedy of Private Property" (repr.), https://cooperative-individualism.org/fairlie-simon_tragedy-of-private-property-2009-summer.pdf
- STATUS: Hardin 1968, Lloyd 1833, Ostrom 1990 and Fairlie 2009 are VERIFIED for bibliographic data. The "Unmanaged" retraction is SECONDARY (Fairlie quoting Hardin 1991; the TREE 1994 item exists by bibliography). The "most cited in *Science*" claim is UNVERIFIED; use the narrower survey claim. Angus is UNVERIFIED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "The title of my 1968 paper should have been 'The Tragedy of the Unmanaged Commons'" (Hardin, in Andelson ed., *Commons Without Tragedy*, 1991, as quoted in Fairlie 2009). Hardin 1968's own conclusion as Fairlie quotes it: "The alternative of the commons is too horrifying to contemplate."
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Frame Hardin's pasture as open access, not a managed commons, and give his own later retraction. Ostrom supplies the field evidence. Avoid the claims about citation rank.
