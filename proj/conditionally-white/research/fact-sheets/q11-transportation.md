# Q11 — The ancestor's menu: transportation in the famine years

Research worker fact sheet. Compiled 2026-11-24. **Budget note:** the shared web-search budget (200/turn) and web-fetch budget (400/hour) both ran out partway through this batch. Items marked UNVERIFIED below were not reached, not disproved; each names the closest source to check next.

## Headline findings

1. **The "menu" had a third door the brief leaves out: Bermuda (and Gibraltar).** In 1846 the transportation of Irish male convicts to Australia was suspended for about two years (NAI); men sentenced in 1846–48 were often sent to the Bermuda hulks instead. The NAI index itself shows Co. Cavan cattle stealers tried 5 Jan 1847 sentenced to 10 years' transportation and listed against the ship *Medway*, which NAI elsewhere documents as going to Bermuda (Nov 1847), where it became a prison hulk. So "sentenced to transportation" in 1846–48 ≠ "went to Australia."
2. **Not every famine "transportation" sentence was carried out.** The NAI finding aid warns that from about 1848 register entries "can confirm a sentence but cannot be cited as proof of transportation" (paraphrase of NAI text; see I-3).
3. **Van Diemen's Land is confirmed as the main Australian destination** for Irish convicts in the famine years: NAI says transportation to VDL was "the most common sentence for serious offences, usually for seven years"; Richard Davis (Companion to Tasmanian History) gives 7,248 Irish male and 4,068 Irish female convicts arriving in VDL 1840–1853 (20% of all convicts there). Transportation from Ireland ended in 1853 (NAI).
4. **The surge in sentences is documented in Hansard.** Earl Grey, HL Deb 6 Mar 1849 col. 247: the average number of men sentenced to transportation in Ireland was "less than 545" a year up to 1845, then "1,870" in 1847 and "nearly 1,900" in 1848. Lords Monteagle and Grey both blamed "distress"; Monteagle said the serious offences were mostly "sheep stealing and cattle stealing."
5. **"Committing a crime in order to be transported" has primary archival cases, but they are female and mostly arson, not horse theft:** Mary and Johanna Kelleher of Bantry, 1848 (CRF 1848/K39) and Margaret Byrne of Carlow, 1849 (CRF 1849/B 25), per Rena Lohan's NAI guide. No horse-theft case of this kind was found. The family legend is *plausible in kind* but this batch found no documented male horse-thief-for-transportation case.
6. **Counter-evidence exists and must be shown:** the Irish government wrote that transportation was "viewed with the greatest terror by the Irish" (NAI famine article), and Peel is quoted as saying it was dreaded in Ireland. The motive in the legend is contested, not settled.
7. **The NAI "database" is no longer a live search.** NAI now serves the Ireland–Australia index as a downloadable PDF, searchable by name; you cannot filter it by crime online. A crime search ("horse") means text-searching the PDF. Our fetch tool could read only the first ~87,000 characters (surnames A–ARM), so no counts for horse theft 1846–52 could be produced.
8. **NAI's own headline figure differs from the commonly cited one:** the NAI finding aid says "some 160,000 persons were transported to Australia, ... approximately 26,500 of whom left from Ireland"; a Maynooth MA thesis (Butler 2005) says "around 30,000 sent directly from Ireland." Present as a range and name both.
9. **Earl Grey orphan scheme: 4,114 girls / 20 ships confirmed by one expert source (Perry McIntyre, *Breac* 2018), but his own per-colony figures sum to 4,093, and his article gives two different dates for the Hyde Park Barracks memorial (1998 McAleese / 1999 Deane).** PROBABLE, needs McClaughlin as second source.
10. **Horse theft's legal status in 1840s Ireland (capital or not), WA/Irish ships, the 1849–50 NSW "exiles", Moreton Bay and Norfolk Island dates, last VDL ship: all UNVERIFIED in this batch** (budget ran out). Do not publish them from this sheet.

### Q11-1a — Which colonies received convicts when; where a famine-era Irish horse thief would go
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): NSW to 1840 (brief "exiles" 1849–50); VDL to 1853 with ships direct from Dublin/Cork; WA 1850–1868; Bermuda and Gibraltar hulks. An Irish horse thief sentenced 1846–1852 most likely went to VDL.
- FINDING:
  - **End of transportation from Ireland: 1853.** NAI: transportation from Ireland ran from 1791 (first ship April 1791, to NSW) and "ceased in 1853," commuted thereafter to prison sentences in Ireland, with the exception of Fenians transported after the 1867 rising. NAI lists destinations as NSW and Tasmania, "later" Bermuda and Gibraltar, and the West Indies. NAI's transportation pages do **not** mention Western Australia.
  - **VDL as the default destination in the 1840s:** NAI (famine CSO article): "Transportation to Van Diemen's Land" was the most common sentence for serious offences, usually seven years (also 10, 14, life).
  - **VDL totals:** Maxwell-Stewart (Companion to Tasmanian History, "Convicts"): about 75,000 convicts served time in VDL 1803–1853, 67,000 shipped from British and Irish ports, about 45% of all convicts landed in Australia; transportation's "eventual demise in 1853 was widely celebrated." He also writes that "as before 1840 relatively few convicts were transported direct from Ireland" — i.e., the Irish direct flow to VDL is a post-1840 phenomenon.
  - **Irish arrivals in VDL 1840–1853:** Davis: "Between 1840 and 1853, 7248 Irish male and 4068 Irish female convicts arrived in the island, 20 percent of all convicts." (Note: this may include Irish-born people tried in Britain; the page does not say "direct from Ireland.")
  - **1846 suspension and Bermuda:** NAI (Kilmainham register article): in 1846 "the transportation of Irish male convicts to Australia had been suspended," so other destinations were sought. NAI famine article: Australian pressure led to a two-year suspension for Irish men; women less affected. Butler (2005 MA, p. 28): no convicts sent from Ireland to Australia from November 1846 for nearly two years; p. 29: from September 1848 to November 1852 about 4,000 were sent.
  - **Lord Grey's 1848 "exile" system:** NAI famine article: three stages — separate confinement at home, public works at Gibraltar or Bermuda (men only), then transportation on ticket-of-leave; Ireland lacked accommodation to run it as designed; in July 1850 Governor Denison refused to take more Irish convicts on tickets-of-leave.
  - **Worked example from NAI (Kilmainham register):** Charles Perry, George Pasley and James Meath, each "10 years' transportation" for burglary and robbery, June 1847; put aboard the *Medway* for Bermuda in Nov 1847 (arrived c. Jan 1848, became a hulk). Pasley was moved in April 1849 to the *Neptune*, which was refused landing at the Cape of Good Hope, and reached VDL on 5 April 1850. Meath stayed on the *Medway* until January 1854. Perry was pardoned in 1851 and sent to England.
  - **Conclusion (for the essay):** A man sentenced to transportation in Ireland in **1846–early 1848** would most likely have gone to **Bermuda (or Gibraltar)**, possibly later passing on to VDL; one sentenced **late 1848–1852** would most likely have gone **direct to VDL**. NSW and Moreton Bay were effectively closed; WA is unlikely for an Irish-tried man (UNVERIFIED — see below). After 1853 a sentence would be served in Ireland.
  - **UNVERIFIED in this batch (budget ran out):** the 1849–50 NSW "exile" ships (e.g., *Hashemy*, 1849); Moreton Bay closure date (usually given as 1842); Norfolk Island's second settlement dates and its 1844 transfer to VDL; whether any convict ship sailed from Ireland to WA (the usual account is that WA received few Irish-tried convicts until the *Hougoumont*, 1868, with Fenians aboard); the name and date of the last convict ship to VDL (convictrecords.com.au lists a *Midlothian* voyage, search result only, not opened). Next sources: Fremantle Prison and State Library of WA pages; Libraries Tasmania; Bateson, *The Convict Ships* (1969).
- SOURCES:
  - National Archives of Ireland, "Guide to penal transportation records: Ireland to Australia, 1788–1868," https://nationalarchives.ie/?p=17241 (read via fetch tool).
  - National Archives of Ireland, "Ireland-Australia transportation records," https://nationalarchives.ie/?p=772.
  - National Archives of Ireland, famine topic article on CSO records and convict management, https://nationalarchives.ie/topics/famine/convmgmt.html (no author or CSO reference numbers shown).
  - National Archives of Ireland, "Prisoners of the past: General Register of Prisoners, Kilmainham, 1846–48" (PRIS/1/10/7), https://nationalarchives.ie/?p=5127.
  - Hamish Maxwell-Stewart, "Convicts," *The Companion to Tasmanian History*, University of Tasmania, https://www.utas.edu.au/library/companion_to_tasmanian_history/C/Convicts.htm.
  - Richard Davis, "Irish Community," *The Companion to Tasmanian History*, https://www.utas.edu.au/tasmanian-companion/biogs/E000516b.htm.
  - Patrick Butler, *The Transportation of Convicts from County Tipperary to Australia 1836–1853*, MA thesis, NUI Maynooth, 2005, pp. 28–29, https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/5096/1/Patrick_Butler_20140623100109.pdf (SECONDARY; MA thesis).
- STATUS: VERIFIED (1853 end; VDL as main destination; 1846 suspension; Bermuda/Gibraltar role; Davis and Maxwell-Stewart figures). UNVERIFIED (NSW exiles, Moreton Bay, Norfolk Island, WA-from-Ireland, last ship).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "Between 1840 and 1853, 7248 Irish male and 4068 Irish female convicts arrived in the island, 20 percent of all convicts." — Richard Davis, "Irish Community," *Companion to Tasmanian History* (UTas). / "as before 1840 relatively few convicts were transported direct from Ireland" — Hamish Maxwell-Stewart, "Convicts," *Companion to Tasmanian History* (UTas). / In 1846 "the transportation of Irish male convicts to Australia had been suspended" — NAI, Kilmainham register article.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The honest "menu" depends on the year of sentence: 1846–48 → likely Bermuda hulks first; 1848–52 → likely VDL direct. Don't say "Australia" as if it were automatic. Davis's 7,248/4,068 is "Irish," not "tried in Ireland" — say "Irish convicts."

### Q11-2b — Typical sentences for livestock theft in famine-era Ireland

### Q11-2c — The famine surge in transportation sentences
- CLAIM: Rising convictions for theft 1846–49.
- FINDING: Earl Grey (Colonial Secretary) in the Lords, 6 March 1849: average men sentenced to transportation in Ireland "less than 545" a year up to 1845; 1,870 in 1847; "nearly 1,900" in 1848. He said the sentence had "been latterly passed in Ireland in rather an indiscriminate manner," and attributed the rise to "the great distress which prevailed in the country." Monteagle: "The pressure of distress was the great cause of the increase of crime." Butler (2005, p. 19) gives a different series: average 673 transported a year before 1846, 2,687 by 1848 (different measure; source not checked). Nicholas Woodward, "Transportation Convictions during the Great Irish Famine," *Journal of Interdisciplinary History* 37:1 (2006), pp. 59–87: abstract confirms an increase in criminal activity, a shift of crime toward the worst-hit areas, young men still the main offenders, and that famine-era convicts "often received lighter sentences." Woodward's tables were not accessible.
- SOURCES:
  - HL Deb 06 March 1849 vol 103 cc244–50, cols. 245, 247 (primary).
  - N. Woodward, *J. Interdisciplinary History* 37.1 (2006): 59–87, abstract only, https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/journal_of_interdisciplinary_history/v037/37.1woodward.pdf.
  - Butler 2005, p. 19 (SECONDARY).
  - Helen Mary Yates, *Staying Alive: Crime, Agency, and Trauma during the Great Irish Famine of 1845–1852*, PhD thesis, University of Staffordshire, 2025, p. 29 (cites Woodward: crime figures "doubled during the Famine"), https://eprints.staffs.ac.uk/9734/2/Helen%20Yates%20-%20Thesis_.pdf (SECONDARY).
- STATUS: VERIFIED (Grey's figures, as spoken in Parliament; the figure for 1848 is his estimate: "he believed").
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "He found that up to 1845 the average number of male persons sentenced to transportation in Ireland was less than 545, while in 1847 the number was 1,870, and in 1848 it amounted, he believed, to nearly 1,900." — Earl Grey, HL Deb 6 March 1849, vol 103, col. 247 (Hansard reported speech; two fetched fragments joined at the comma).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: A strong chart candidate (three points: <545 / 1,870 / ~1,900), but it is men only, sentences not departures, and 1848 is "nearly." Label it so. A second source for the series is still needed (Woodward's tables or the parliamentary returns of convictions).

### Q11-2d — Committing crime in order to be transported (the load-bearing context)
- CLAIM: Famine-era Irish people committed crimes in order to be transported or jailed.
- FINDING: Primary-based cases exist, all women in this batch:
  - Mary and Johanna Kelleher, Bantry, 1848 (CRF 1848/K39): committed an offence so as to be transported with their mother; discharged and sent as free settlers.
  - Margaret Byrne, Carlow, 1849 (CRF 1849/B 25): death sentence for arson on an occupied house commuted to transportation for life; said she committed the crime "in anticipation of a transportation sentence."
  - Butler (2005, Tipperary, SECONDARY): Margaret Leggitt, 20, 15 years for arson in 1849, statement that five people burned the house so as to be transported (p. 22); Bridget Murphy, sentenced 1841 (pre-famine), confessed in Australia to arson to be transported (p. 20); "Some coveted the chance of escaping their misery by perpetrating crime in order to be transported" (p. 26); "Some people committed the most trivial of crimes just to be arrested" for prison food (p. 8).
  - Governor Denison (VDL) on Irish women landed January 1849 (272 of 298 convicted in 1847): they "seem not to look on their removal as a punishment" (NAI famine article; attitude after sentencing, not proof of motive).
  - Yates (2025 PhD) frames crime as survival: "theft, imprisonment to secure food, or transportation as a route to a new life" (abstract, p. ii).
  - **Allegation at the time:** Lord Campbell had alleged a "settled design of the Irish magistrates" to give paupers emigration at public expense via transportation; Monteagle rejected this as unjust (HL Deb 6 Mar 1849, col. 245). This shows the idea was current in 1849, but as a charge against magistrates, not convicts.
  - **Counter-evidence:** Irish government (via NAI article): transportation "viewed with the greatest terror by the Irish," and separation from home feared more than prison "except where starvation awaits the unfortunate criminal in his own country." Peel quoted as saying transportation was "not much dreaded" in England but "in Ireland it was" (RTÉ Brainstorm, Anna McKay, 2021; SECONDARY, Peel's original not traced).
  - **No horse-theft or male case of deliberate crime-for-transportation was found** in this batch.
- SOURCES:
  - Rena Lohan, "Sources in the National Archives for research into the transportation of Irish convicts to Australia (1791–1853)," NAI web version of *Irish Archives* (Journal of the Irish Society for Archives), Spring 1996, https://www.nationalarchives.ie/topics/transportation/transp8.html (cites CRF 1848/K39; CRF 1849/B 25).
  - NAI famine CSO article (above).
  - HL Deb 6 March 1849, col. 245 (above).
  - Butler 2005, pp. 8, 20, 22, 26 (SECONDARY).
  - Yates 2025, p. ii (SECONDARY).
  - Anna McKay, "How the Great Famine led to Irish convicts being sent to Bermuda," RTÉ Brainstorm, 8 June 2021, https://www.rte.ie/brainstorm/2021/0608/1226956-bermuda-irish-convicts-great-famine (SECONDARY).
- STATUS: VERIFIED that such cases are documented (archival references via NAI); CONTESTED as a general pattern (contemporaries also said transportation was dreaded); UNVERIFIED for horse theft or for men.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "It was found they had committed the offence in order to be transported, as they wished to go with their mother who had been given a seven year sentence." — Rena Lohan, NAI, citing CRF 1848/K39. / "She stated that she had committed the crime in anticipation of a transportation sentence (CRF 1849/B 25)." — Lohan, NAI. / transportation had "been viewed with the greatest terror by the Irish" — Irish government, quoted in NAI famine CSO article.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The legend sits on a real pattern: documented, archived cases of people choosing a crime to be transported. Frame it as "the archive shows people did this; the family says he did," and give the counter-view (terror of transportation) in one clause. Don't claim it was common among men or among horse thieves.

### Q11-3 — The records databases and the search a reader could run
- CLAIM: NAI's online database (transportation.nationalarchives.ie) can be searched by crime and year; convictrecords.com.au; Libraries Tasmania; State Records NSW.
- FINDING:

### Q11-4 — Conditions: ships, hulks and the VDL probation system
- CLAIM: Convict ship conditions; probation replaced assignment in VDL in the 1840s.
- FINDING:
  - **Probation (VDL):** Maxwell-Stewart: after the Molesworth Report "condemned the assignment system as a mere lottery, a series of probation stations was constructed from the 1840s"; prisoners served probationary road labour set by sentence length, "Only when they had negotiated this period of hard labour could they be hired out to the private sector"; expensive and hard to run. (So for an 1848–52 arrival: probation gang → pass-holder hired out → ticket-of-leave.)
  - **Ticket-of-leave stage:** NAI: Grey's 1848 scheme ended in transportation on ticket-of-leave; Denison stopped Irish ticket-of-leave arrivals in July 1850.
  - **Bermuda hulks (alternative destination):** McKay (RTÉ, 2021): seven hulks at Bermuda 1824–1863, about 250 men each; chain-gang quarrying; in 1848, 44 of a shipment of 704 died after arrival (~6%); many Irish arrived half-starved with scurvy; yellow fever. (SECONDARY; no scholarly source named.)
  - **Ship mortality:** not reached. A UK National Archives page on "morbidity and mortality on convict voyages" was found in search but not opened.
- SOURCES:
  - Maxwell-Stewart, "Convicts," Companion to Tasmanian History (above).
  - NAI famine CSO article (above).
  - McKay, RTÉ Brainstorm 2021 (SECONDARY).
- STATUS: VERIFIED (probation system outline); SECONDARY (Bermuda conditions); UNVERIFIED (shipboard conditions and death rates).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "Following the publication of the highly critical Molesworth Report which condemned the assignment system as a mere lottery, a series of probation stations was constructed from the 1840s." — Hamish Maxwell-Stewart, "Convicts," *Companion to Tasmanian History* (two fetched fragments joined).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Use "probation" not "assignment" for a famine-era arrival in VDL. Bermuda mortality figure needs a primary source (Bermuda convict establishment reports in Parliamentary Papers) before it's charted.

### Q11-5 — The Earl Grey orphan emigration scheme (1848–1850)
- CLAIM: ~4,114 workhouse girls to Sydney, Port Phillip and Adelaide; McClaughlin, *Barefoot and Pregnant?*; Hyde Park Barracks memorial.
- FINDING (one source opened, late, after budgets partly refilled): Perry McIntyre (UNSW; a historian of the scheme and of the Great Irish Famine Commemoration Committee's work), writing in *Breac* (Notre Dame), 28 Jan 2018: **4,114** "single teenage women from workhouses in every county during 1848 to 1850" to three colonies. Ship breakdown: **Port Phillip 6 ships / 1,255 girls; Sydney 11 ships / 2,232 girls (commissioners' official figure 2,220); Adelaide 3 ships / 606 girls** → 20 ships. **Arithmetic flag:** 1,255 + 2,232 + 606 = 4,093 (4,081 with the official Sydney figure), not 4,114; the article doesn't reconcile this (Adelaide records are noted as incomplete). First sailings: *Earl Grey* 3 June 1848 for Sydney; *Roman Emperor* 27 July 1848 for Adelaide; last Sydney arrival *Tippoo Saib*, end of July 1850 (youngest, five girls aged 13). Reception: Australian press called them "immoral dregs of the workhouse" though McIntyre says "this was not the whole story"; opposition ended the scheme within three years. **Memorial date conflict inside the article:** Hyde Park Barracks memorial "unveiled" by Governor-General Sir William Deane on 28 August 1999, but the conclusion says President Mary McAleese "inaugurated" it on 2 September 1998 — needs a second source (likely one was a foundation/launch and the other the unveiling; unverified).
- SOURCES:
  - Perry McIntyre, "Remembering and Commemorating the Great Famine and Emigration to Australia," *Breac: A Digital Journal of Irish Studies*, University of Notre Dame, 28 January 2018, https://breac.nd.edu/articles/remembering-and-commemorating-the-great-famine-and-emigration-to-australia/ (cites McClaughlin, *Barefoot and Pregnant?* vol. 2, 2001, pp. 397–402, for Adelaide).
  - Second source still needed: Trevor McClaughlin, *Barefoot and Pregnant? Irish Famine Orphans in Australia* (vols. 1991, 2001); irishfaminememorial.org orphan database; Museums of History NSW (Hyde Park Barracks).
- STATUS: PROBABLE (4,114 and 20 ships from one expert source; second source not reached; per-colony numbers don't sum to the total).
