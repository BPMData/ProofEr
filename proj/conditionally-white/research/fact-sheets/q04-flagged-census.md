## Headline findings

1. **B24 (Census quote) is real, but the pack misquotes it.** The 1851 *General Report* (Part VI, Dublin 1856) says the results "are, on the whole, **so** satisfactory". The pack drops "so", and its ellipsis hides "and has been since decreasing". The sentence opens "In conclusion," and it sits on IA scan page 57 of the General Report. The exact wording is below. Use it, not the pack's version.
2. **Census totals:** 1841 = **8,175,124** (VERIFIED, General Report table, IA p. 14; also the 1841/1851 Abstract on DIPPAM). 1851 = **6,552,385** in the final General Report (IA pp. 46 and 56; erratum p. 107 corrects Part IV's 6,552,386). The preliminary 1851 *Abstract* printed **6,515,794** (a decrease of 1,659,330). Chart 6,552,385 and footnote that the preliminary count was lower.
3. **PS-01 (Mitchel's 187 → 516) checks out against the Census itself.** Part V (*Tables of Deaths*, 1856) gives registered starvation deaths of 187 (1842), 211 (1843), 268 (1844) and 516 (1845), then 2,041 (1846). These are on IA pp. 256 and 282. Mitchel's "increased every year" is accurate. His "one-tenth of the unregistered" is his own guess, not the Census's.
4. **B7 (bailiff) is real, but the pack's wording is modernized.** The earliest text found is O'Rourke, *History of the Great Irish Famine* (1875; 1902 3rd ed., p. 160). He cites the *Freeman's Journal* correspondent, writing from Templemore, in spring 1846. O'Rourke prints "What the d—— do we care…", not "devil". He also prints "the consequence" (singular), and the rent demanded is the November rent. The FJ issue date was not pinned down.
5. **B6 ("Duke of Cambridge") is still SHAKY and unsourced in anything primary I could open.** The only dated lead is a secondary one: Advertiser.ie (Galway) cites *The Nation*, 24 January 1846. The longer "rotten potatoes and seaweed" opening returns **zero** web hits outside quote compilations, so it may be a later expansion. Also, in Jan 1846 the Duke of Cambridge was Prince Adolphus (1774–1850), Victoria's **uncle**. He was not "Victoria's cousin and head of the British army", which is how IrishCentral/O'Dowd describe him; that was his son George, who became Duke only in 1850. Do not ship this quote until *The Nation*, 24 Jan 1846, has been read.
6. **B22 (Jowett/Senior):** the phrase is **not** in Abbott & Campbell, *Life and Letters of Benjamin Jowett* (1897), vols 1–2, by full-text search. It is not in Tollemache's *Benjamin Jowett* (1895) either. Its printed origin is still untraced, so it stays SECONDARY via Woodham-Smith.
7. **B16 (pig/McManus) and B28 (Edwards 2015):** these are UNVERIFIED because the search budget ran out. B16 is not in O'Rourke.

### B24 — 1851 Census "on the whole, satisfactory … general advancement"
- CLAIM (as the pack states it): "...we feel it will be gratifying to your Excellency to find that although the population has been diminished in so remarkable a manner by famine, disease and emigration between 1841 and 1851... the results of the Irish census of 1851 are, on the whole, satisfactory, demonstrating as they do the general advancement of the country." — *The Census of Ireland for the Year 1851*.
- FINDING: The sentence exists, in the Commissioners' concluding paragraph of **Part VI, General Report** (Dublin: HMSO, 1856), addressed to the Lord Lieutenant ("your Excellency"; Part IV of the same census is addressed to the Earl of Carlisle). The pack's text has two errors: it drops **"so"** before "satisfactory", and its ellipsis removes **"and has been since decreasing"**. The ellipsis matters, because it shows the Commissioners knew that the decline was still going on. The paragraph goes on to list the "advancement": more arable land and stock since 1841, the worst class of houses being replaced, a smaller share of families depending on manual labour, and progress in education. Cross-check: *The Spectator*, 13 Sept 1856 ("The Irish Census") reviews this report. It calls the Commissioners' tone "congratulatory" and paraphrases their evidence as "substantial evidences of advancement". I read that paraphrase, not the sentence itself.
- SOURCES:
  - *Census of Ireland for the Year 1851, Part VI: General Report* (Dublin: Alexander Thom for HMSO, 1856), Internet Archive item `op1247883-1001` (title in IA metadata: "Census of Ireland 1851 : part VI, general report", date 1856). Text found with IA full-text search-inside: https://ia600702.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=op1247883-1001&doc=op1247883-1001&path=/26/items/op1247883-1001&q=%22general+advancement%22 — **IA page 57**. The report's opening section uses Roman numbering (the same text refers to "Table VII., page xvi"), so the printed folio is probably **lvii**. Confirm on the scan before you print a page cite. The command-paper number was not captured; get it from EPPI/DIPPAM.
  - *The Spectator*, 13 September 1856, p. 12/13, "The Irish Census" (secondary review, confirms the congratulatory framing): https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/13th-september-1856/13/the-irish-census
- STATUS: VERIFIED (wording). The printed page number still needs a check on the scan.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT (OCR read; I corrected only plain OCR noise, shown in brackets: "satis-actoiy" → satisfactory, "eci easing" → decreasing, a doubled comma after "famine"):
  > "In conclusion, we feel it will be gratifying to your Excellency to find that although the population has been diminished in so remarkable a manner by famine, disease, and emigration between 1841 and 1851, and has been since [de]creasing, the results of the Irish Census of 1851 are, on the whole, so satis[f]actory, demonstrating as they do the general advancement of the country."
  > — *Census of Ireland for the Year 1851, Part VI: General Report* (1856), IA p. 57 [printed p. lvii?].

The bracketed words are illegible in the OCR ("and . f . ue 0 , fa™ stock"). Do not print that clause until it has been read from the scan.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: This works as the centerpiece, quoted in full with "so satisfactory" and "has been since decreasing" restored. That version is stronger and more exact. Have someone read the page image once to confirm the OCR, especially "decreasing" and the stock clause.

### Census totals 1841 / 1851 (chart data)
- CLAIM: 1841 = 8,175,124; 1851 = 6,552,385.
- FINDING: Both are confirmed in the 1856 General Report. 1851 census night was **30 March 1851**: General Report p. 56, "the number of inhabitants on the 30th of March in that year — the date of the Census — was 6,552,385 persons". The earlier printed *Abstracts of the Census of Ireland taken in the Years 1841 and 1851* gave a preliminary **6,515,794** for 1851 (decrease 1,659,330). That abstract says its figures "may be revised" and that no adjustment for births and deaths was made. It also says that the 1841 figure includes 5,841 harvest labourers who had left Ireland before census day (7 June 1841), and that neither count includes the army serving in Ireland. Signatories: Edward Singleton, William Donnelly (Registrar-General), W. R. Wilde (OCR "W. E. Wilde").
- SOURCES:
  - General Report 1856 (IA `op1247883-1001`): 8,175,124 at IA p. 14 (summary table); 6,552,385 at IA pp. 46, 56; erratum at p. 107: "the population of Ireland should have been stated as 6,552,385, instead of 6,552,386" (an error of 1 in Tyrone, females aged 82). Search-inside URLs: …&q=8,175,124 and …&q=6,552,385 on the same base as above.
  - *Abstracts of the Census of Ireland taken in the Years 1841 and 1851*, EPPI/DIPPAM document 12939: https://www.dippam.ac.uk/eppi/documents/12939/pages/327749 (1841 = 8,175,124; 1851 preliminary = 6,515,794).
- STATUS: VERIFIED (two documents of the census office: the preliminary abstract and the final report. They are not fully independent, so a modern series such as the CSO/Vaughan & Fitzpatrick *Irish Historical Statistics* would make a good second source.)
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "The number of inhabitants on the 30th of March in that year — the date of the Census — was 6,552,385 persons." — General Report (1856), IA p. 56.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Chart 8,175,124 → 6,552,385, a fall of 1,622,739 (19.8%; this is my arithmetic). Footnote that the first count in 1851 was 6,515,794. The census dates differ: 6 June 1841 vs 30 March 1851. The Abstract notes call 7 June the census date, so check which date the essay prints.

### PS-01 — Registered "deaths by famine" 187 (1842) → 516 (1845)
- CLAIM: Mitchel, *Last Conquest*: "The Report of the Census Commissioners, for 1851, declares that, while in 1842 the deaths registered as deaths by famine amounted to 187, they increased every year, until the registered deaths in 1845 were 516."
- FINDING: This is confirmed in *Census of Ireland 1851, Part V: Tables of Deaths* (1856). IA p. 256: "from 187 in the year 1842" to "516 in 1845". IA p. 282 gives the series 1842 (OCR-garbled "1«7", meaning 187), 211 in 1843, 268 in 1844, 516 in 1845, and 2,041 in 1846. A summary of the p. 256 passage (the fetch tool would not reproduce it in full) also gives a peak of 6,058 in 1847 and a total of 21,770 starvation deaths in the returns, with only 117 in the decade before 1841. I have that from a tool summary only, so re-read p. 256 before quoting those three numbers. The Census calls the cause "starvation". Mitchel's "deaths by famine" is his own wording. His claim that registered deaths were "perhaps one-tenth" of real hunger deaths is his own estimate. It does not come from the Census.
- SOURCES:
  - *Census of Ireland for the Year 1851, Part V: Tables of Deaths*, vols I–II (1856), IA item `op1247882-1001`. Search-inside: https://ia601908.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=op1247882-1001&doc=op1247882-1001&path=/8/items/op1247882-1001&q=starvation (and q="516 in 1845", q="187 in the year 1842"), IA pp. 256, 282.
  - John Mitchel, *The Last Conquest of Ireland (Perhaps)*: the pack's transcription (PS-01). The edition is not settled (PS-01a).
- STATUS: VERIFIED for 187 / 211 / 268 / 516 / 2,041. PROBABLE (snippet summary only) for 6,058 / 21,770 / 117.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "from 187 in the year 1842" … "516 in 1845". The full sentence was not captured. Transcribe it from the scan at IA p. 256.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: These are deaths *registered as starvation* in a census taken after the famine, which collected them from household returns and institutions. They undercount badly. The Census's own famine-era "starvation" total (~21,770) is tiny next to the ~1 million excess deaths. Use the series to show that registered starvation deaths were rising *before* the blight, but caption it as "registered, not actual". The 1845 figure is the whole year, and blight appeared only from September 1845.

### B7 — The bailiff's reply ("black potatoes")
- CLAIM: "What the devil do we care about you or your black potatoes? It was not us that made them black. You will get two days to pay the rent, and if you don't you know the consequences." — *Freeman's Journal*, April 1846.
- FINDING: The earliest printed text found is in Rev. John O'Rourke, *The History of the Great Irish Famine of 1847* (1st ed. 1875; 3rd ed. Dublin: James Duffy, 1902), p. 160. Chapter IV's contents list it as "Notice for rent — The bailiff's reply". O'Rourke says "the correspondent" (named on p. 159 and footnoted on p. 160 as *The Freeman's Journal*) wrote from Templemore, Co. Tipperary. A "certain noble proprietor" had just visited his estate, and once he left, notices were served on his tenantry to pay the November rent. The tenants asked for time, "saying they had only a few black potatoes left". O'Rourke then gives the reply. The surrounding narrative cites a *Morning Chronicle* letter from Limerick dated 16 April [1846] on p. 158, so April 1846 fits, but the FJ issue date itself was not captured. Wording differences from the pack: O'Rourke has **"the d——"** (dashed in the source), not "devil". He has **"—it was not us that made them black—"** inside one sentence, and **"the consequence"** (singular).
- SOURCES:
  - O'Rourke, *History of the Great Irish Famine of 1847*, 3rd ed. (1902), p. 160, footnote citing *The Freeman's Journal*. IA item `historyofgreatir00orou`. Search-inside: https://ia601503.us.archive.org/fulltext/inside.php?item_id=historyofgreatir00orou&doc=historyofgreatir00orou&path=/13/items/historyofgreatir00orou&q=%22black+potatoes%22 (also q=correspondent, q=Freeman).
  - Project Gutenberg #14412 (same 3rd edition) has the chapter IV contents entry: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14412/pg14412.txt
  - Evergreen State College course page "Eyewitness Reports of the Famine" (a compilation, lead only): https://sites.evergreen.edu/ireland1617/?p=175
- STATUS: PROBABLE. The text is verified in O'Rourke with the FJ named as his source. The FJ issue itself is unread and its date unconfirmed.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "What the d—— do we care about you or your black potatoes?—it was not us that made them black—you will get two days to pay the rent, and if you don't you know the consequence." — reported by the *Freeman's Journal*'s correspondent at Templemore, spring 1846, as quoted in J. O'Rourke, *History of the Great Irish Famine of 1847* (3rd ed., 1902), p. 160. The dash after "d" is as printed. The length of the dash could not be told from the OCR, so check the scan.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Caption it as a newspaper correspondent's report of a bailiff's words, a rent demand on a titled landlord's estate near Templemore. Don't print "devil". To get the exact FJ date, search the *Freeman's Journal* for April 1846 for "Templemore" (Irish Newspaper Archives / British Newspaper Archive; both need a subscription).

### B6 — "Duke of Cambridge": rotten potatoes, seaweed, grass
- CLAIM: The Duke of Cambridge, January 1846: "Rotten potatoes and seaweed, or even grass, properly mixed, afforded a very wholesome and nutritious food. All knew that Irishmen could live upon anything and there was plenty of grass in the field though the potato crop should fail."
- FINDING: No primary text was found. The trail of attribution:
  (a) Advertiser.ie (Galway) cites ***The Nation*, 24 January 1846**, for the second sentence only ("…even though the potato crop should fail"). This is the only dated citation found.
  (b) Niall O'Dowd's 2023 speech (IrishCentral) gives the full two-sentence version with no source. It calls the Duke "Queen Victoria's cousin and head of the British army". **That is wrong for January 1846.** The 1st Duke of Cambridge was then Prince Adolphus (1774–1850), Victoria's *uncle*. His son Prince George succeeded in July 1850 and was Commander-in-Chief from 1856. (Background fact, not fetched this session. Confirm via ODNB or another reference work before printing.)
  (c) The Evergreen compilation gives only the second sentence.
  An exact-phrase web search for "Rotten potatoes and seaweed, or even grass, properly mixed" returned no hits at all. The first sentence may be a later addition or paraphrase. The phrase is also not in O'Rourke (1902) going by the readable part, and it is not in Ian Miller, "The Chemistry of Famine", *Medical History* 56:4 (2012), which covers the nutrition-advice controversies (that article does mention other dukes). Kinealy and Woodham-Smith were not checked: no searchable text was available.
- SOURCES:
  - Advertiser.ie, "A 'selfish, perverse and turbulent' people" (secondary; cites *The Nation*, 24 Jan 1846): https://www.advertiser.ie/galway/article/76676/a-selfish-perverse-and-turbulent-people
  - IrishCentral, O'Dowd full speech (secondary, no source, wrong identification): https://www.irishcentral.com/opinion/niallodowd/full-speech-irish-famine-american-history
  - Evergreen "Eyewitness Reports" (compilation): https://sites.evergreen.edu/ireland1617/?p=175
  - Miller 2012, *Medical History* 56(4): 444–462, doi:10.1017/mdh.2012.27 (negative check): https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/D8893519DDC484BFB970C3B73E4C3118/core-reader
- STATUS: UNVERIFIED. It is **UNUSABLE as printed in the pack**, because the two-sentence version has no source and the speaker is described wrongly in circulation.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none. If *The Nation* of 24 Jan 1846 is read, transcribe from it. It probably reports a speech at an English agricultural or county meeting in mid-January 1846 (Parliament opened on 22 Jan 1846). Check whether *The Nation* reports him directly or in reported speech ("His Royal Highness said…"), and whether it quotes another paper.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: If it can't be checked against *The Nation*, drop it or hedge it hard ("a remark attributed in Irish nationalist press to the Duke of Cambridge, the Queen's uncle…"). ### B22 — Jowett recalling a political economist (Nassau Senior)
- CLAIM: "I have always felt a certain horror of political economists since I heard one of them say he feared the famine of 1848 in Ireland would not kill more than a million people, and that would scarcely be enough to do any good." — Benjamin Jowett, recalling Nassau Senior.
- FINDING: The printed origin is untraced. Negative checks by IA full-text search: Evelyn Abbott & Lewis Campbell, *The Life and Letters of Benjamin Jowett* (London: Murray, 1897), **vol. 1** (`lifelettersofben01abbouoft`): "political economists" occurs only on p. 155 ("All the world are become Political Economists…"), a different passage, and "a million" has no hits. **Vol. 2** (`lifelettersofben02abbouoft`): "political economists" and "famine" have no hits. L. A. Tollemache, *Benjamin Jowett, Master of Balliol* (London: Arnold, 1895) (`benjaminjowettma00tolluoft`): "political economists" has no hits. Possible remaining sources are Abbott & Campbell (eds.), *Letters of Benjamin Jowett* (1899), Jowett's notebooks and papers at Balliol, or a memoir of Jowett by someone else. The ending varies across modern citations ("any good" vs "much good"), so the exact wording is not settled. From the pack's framing: Jowett's original says only "one of them". Identifying the economist as Nassau Senior is usually credited to Cecil Woodham-Smith, *The Great Hunger* (1962). I did **not** confirm that Woodham-Smith names Senior or what she cites, because no searchable copy was reachable.
