## Headline findings

1. **PS-07 is misquoted in the user's copy.** The book does not say "never has been effaced". The 1851 text reads: "and the impression *then* and *there* made never *has* nor never *can* be effaced;" (four words in italics) and the sentence goes on: "it was the *first*, and the beginning of these dreadful days yet in reserve." Fix the quote.
2. **PS-07 place: Kingstown** (now Dún Laoghaire, near Dublin). The text says "A servant in the house where I was stopping, at Kingstown". The passage gives no date. Early 1847 follows from her arrival in December 1846 (Murphy) and from where it sits in the book, but that is an inference.
3. **PS-05 (woman and dog) is a story Nicholson was *told*, not something she saw.** In the next chapter she calls it "the story of the woman and dog [that] had been related". The passage names no place and no date. Don't present it as her own eyewitness account.
4. **Arrival date is disputed:** Maureen Murphy (RTÉ 2020; Project MUSE blurb for *Compassionate Stranger*) gives **December 1846**. The DIB entry (Deirdre Bryan, 2009) says only that she "returned in 1847". Use "December 1846 (Murphy)", or "the winter of 1846–47".
5. **The title says 1849, but she left Ireland in autumn 1848.** DIB: September 1848. Murphy: "the fall of 1848". So the "1849" in the title doesn't mean she was there in 1849.
6. **Page numbers (1851 E. French edition; two scans of the same edition agree):** PS-05 starts at the top of **p. 34**. PS-06 is on **p. 36** and probably runs onto p. 37. PS-07 runs across **pp. 37–38**, with p. 38 beginning at "heeds you not". The p. 34 and p. 38 breaks were confirmed in the OCR. The p. 35 and p. 37 breaks were not. Status: PROBABLE until someone checks against the page images.
7. **Cummins:** the brief's wording ("In the first, six famished and ghastly skeletons, to all appearances dead…") matches the Peel Web transcription. It is SECONDARY only; *The Times* page itself wasn't seen. The **date is muddled**: Peel Web says he *visited* Skibbereen on 15 Dec 1846. The worker remembers, but did not check, a letter date line of "Cork, December 17". The printing date, *The Times* 24 Dec 1846, is the one date to use for now.
8. **Mahony (ILN 13 Feb 1847):** no text read this session. UNVERIFIED. Don't use the brief's wording about where he stood until a later run checks it.

### PS-05 — The widow, the dog, and the kind judge
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): "A man had died from hunger, and his widow had gone into the plowed field of her landlord ... The judge gave her three pounds from his own purse; told her when she had used that to come again to him."
- FINDING: The passage exists. It is the first paragraph of the section that libraryireland.com headed "Kind Judge", in Chapter II. In the 1851 E. French edition the OCR shows the running head "34 ANNALS OF THE" directly before "A man had died from hunger". The words just before that head are "...the circumstances to me, all queries were removed." The paragraph probably finishes on p. 34 or runs onto p. 35; the p. 35 break could not be found in the OCR. The text names no place or date for the incident. The next chapter calls it a story that "had been related" to her, so it is secondhand.
- FULL TRANSCRIPTION (joined from exact pieces of the libraryireland.com transcription of the 1851 text):
  > A man had died from hunger, and his widow had gone into the plowed field of her landlord to try to pick a few potatoes in the ridges which might be remaining since the harvest; she found a few—the landlord saw her—sent a magistrate to the cabin, who found three children in a state of starvation, and nothing in the cabin but the pot, which was over the fire. He demanded of her to show him the potatoes—she hesitated; he inquired what she had in the pot—she was silent; he looked in, and saw a dog, with the handful of potatoes she had gathered from the field. The sight of the wretched cabin, and still more, the despairing looks of the poor silent mother and the famished children, crouched in fear in a dark corner, so touched the heart of the magistrate, that he took the pot from the fire, bade the woman to follow him, and they went to the court-room together. He presented the pot, containing the dog and the handful of potatoes, to the astonished judge. He called the woman—interrogated her kindly. She told him they sat in their desolate cabin two entire days, without eating, before she killed the half-famished dog; that she did not think she was stealing, to glean after the harvest was gathered. The judge gave her three pounds from his own purse; told her when she had used that to come again to him.

  The next paragraph begins: "This was a compassionate judge,—and would to God Ireland could boast of many such."

Scan: Internet Archive `annalsoffaminein00nich_0` (Boston College Libraries copy, 348 pp., call no. DA950.7 .N621), https://archive.org/details/annalsoffaminein00nich_0 ; OCR at https://archive.org/stream/annalsoffaminein00nich_0/annalsoffaminein00nich_0_djvu.txt
  - Second scan of the same edition: Internet Archive `annalsfamineini00nichgoog` (Harvard copy, digitized by Google), https://archive.org/details/annalsfamineini00nichgoog
  - Transcription: Library Ireland, "Kind Judge", https://libraryireland.com/annals-famine-ireland/kind-judge.php (no page numbers; source of the wording above)
  - Modern edition: *Annals of the Famine in Ireland*, ed. Maureen Murphy (Dublin: Lilliput Press, 1998), 240 pp., ISBN 1874675945 (Wellcome Collection catalogue record). Page numbers in that edition not checked.
- STATUS: VERIFIED (wording, from the transcription; check once against the scan) / PROBABLE (page: 34)

### PS-06 — "But the wave rolled on" / "There were some peculiarities in this famine"
- CLAIM: "But the wave rolled on; the slain were multiplied..." and "There were some peculiarities in this famine which history has not recorded in any other ... and be answerable for whatever statements are thus made."
- FINDING: Both passages exist, in the same Chapter II section ("Kind Judge" on Library Ireland). A few paragraphs before "But the wave rolled on" is the running head "36 ANNALS OF THE" (words before it: "these 'lazy' ones disgraced their very gates, and"; after it: "shocked all decency, were the rags which they had"), so "But the wave rolled on" is on p. 36. The text just before it reads "...as a reward of their 'religion and idleness.' But the wave rolled on," (wording only from WebFetch's summary of the OCR; the line breaks weren't confirmed). The "peculiarities" passage starts on p. 36 and probably runs onto p. 37. The OCR shows no page break between "jail, where the law must feed him" and "Let it be stated". The p. 37 break wasn't found. Library Ireland splits the "peculiarities" part over more than one paragraph; where its breaks fall wasn't recorded.
- FULL TRANSCRIPTION (joined from exact pieces, Library Ireland):
  > But the wave rolled on; the slain were multiplied; the dead by the way-side, and the more revolting sights of families found in the darkest corner of a cabin, in one putrid mass, where, in many cases, the cabin was tumbled down upon them to give them a burial, was somewhat convincing, even to those who had doubted much from the beginning.

  > There were some peculiarities in this famine which history has not recorded in any other. It may be scrupled whether any were heard to say that they did not deserve it—that they had not been such sinners above all others, that they must suffer so much—and so little plundering was never known in any famine as this; scarcely ever was a bread shop disturbed, though the poor creatures have been found dead under its window, in sight of it; the old proverb that "hunger will break through a stone wall," was never exemplified during the famine; some carts, laden with meal, have been pillaged, and some boats have been robbed, but these were not common occurrences; occasionally, in the cities, would a man throw a stone at a street lamp, or do some other trifling mischief, always in presence of a policeman, that he might be put in jail, where the law must feed him. This was certainly an alternative for a starving man not so much to be censured as admired. Let it be stated that these men had applied for work in vain. [possible paragraph break] I will descend to particulars; and state what my eyes have seen and my ears have heard, and be answerable for whatever statements are thus made.
- SOURCES: as PS-05 (1851 ed., ch. II, p. 36 and probably p. 37; IA `annalsoffaminein00nich_0` and `annalsfamineini00nichgoog`; Library Ireland "Kind Judge").
- STATUS: VERIFIED (wording, from the transcription) / PROBABLE (pages 36–37)
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "There were some peculiarities in this famine which history has not recorded in any other." — Nicholson, *Annals* (1851), ch. II, p. 36 [page probable].
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Her point that there was little plundering is one observer's impression, not a measurement. The 1851 OCR prints "jaij" for "jail" (an OCR slip). The quote marks around the proverb are curly in the transcription; check the scan.

### PS-07 — "The first starving person that I saw" (fills the user's [...] gap)
- CLAIM: "The first starving person that I saw was a few days after the story of the woman and dog had been related ..." through "...the impression then and there made never has been effaced." Where (Kingstown? Dublin?) and when (early 1847)?
- FINDING: The passage exists and opens the section headed "First Starving Person, and Means of Preserving him" (Library Ireland, Chapter II). **The ending in the user's copy is wrong:** the book reads "never *has* nor never *can* be effaced". **Place: Kingstown**, stated in the text. **Date: not given.** It is "a few days after" she heard the woman-and-dog story. Early 1847 is a fair inference, since Murphy has her arriving in December 1846 and working first in Dublin, but the text doesn't say it. **Page:** starts on p. 37 (probable). Both scans put the running head "38 ANNALS OF THE" between "he sees you not, he" and "heeds you not, neither does he beg." The two WebFetch reads of the Boston College OCR disagreed about whether that header is there; the Harvard/Google scan has it. So the passage runs across **pp. 37–38** (PROBABLE).
- FULL TRANSCRIPTION (joined from exact pieces, Library Ireland; italics shown as *asterisks*; Library Ireland breaks the paragraph before "The first stage"):
  > The first starving person that I saw was a few days after the story of the woman and dog had been related. A servant in the house where I was stopping, at Kingstown, said that the milk woman wished me to see a man near by, that was in a state of actual starvation; and he was going out to attempt to work on the Queen's highway; a little labor was beginning opposite the house, and fifteen-pence a-day stimulated this poor man, who had seven to support, his rent to pay, and fuel to buy. He had been sick with fever; the clothes of his family that would fetch any price, had been pawned or sold, and all were starving together. He staggered with his spade to the work; the overseer objected; but he entreated to be allowed to try. The servant went out and asked him to step into the kitchen; and, reader, if you have never seen a starving human being, *may you never!* In my childhood I had been frightened with the stories of ghosts, and had seen actual skeletons; but imagination had come short of the sight of this man. And here, to those who have never watched the progress of protracted hunger, it might be proper to say, that persons will live for months, and pass through different stages, and life will struggle on to maintain her lawful hold, if occasional scanty supplies are given, till the walking skeleton is reduced to a state of inanity—he sees you not, he [p. 38] heeds you not, neither does he beg.

  > The first stage is somewhat clamorous—will not easily be put off; the next is patient, passive stupidity; and the last is idiocy. The man before mentioned was emaciated to the last degree; he was tall, his eyes prominent, his skin shriveled, his manner cringing and childlike; and the impression *then* and *there* made never *has* nor never *can* be effaced; it was the *first*, and the beginning of these dreadful days yet in reserve.
- SOURCES:
  - Nicholson, *Annals* (New York: E. French, 1851), ch. II, pp. 37–38 [probable]; IA `annalsoffaminein00nich_0` and `annalsfamineini00nichgoog` (OCR checked at the p. 38 break).
  - Library Ireland, "First Starving Person, and Means of Preserving him", https://libraryireland.com/annals-famine-ireland/first-starving-person-and-means-of-preserving-him.php
  - Arrival date: Maureen Murphy, "Asenath Nicholson: the American who described the Great Famine", RTÉ, 20 Aug 2020 ("When she returned to Ireland in December 1846, she did not return as a Bible reader but as a relief worker.")
- STATUS: VERIFIED (wording and place) / PROBABLE (pages; date as early 1847)
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "and the impression *then* and *there* made never *has* nor never *can* be effaced; it was the *first*, and the beginning of these dreadful days yet in reserve." — Nicholson, *Annals* (1851), ch. II, p. 38 [page probable].
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Kingstown was a well-off seaside town near Dublin, not the far west. That's part of the force of the passage: she saw famine on the doorstep of the capital. Say "early 1847" as an inference from her arrival date. Her "stages" of hunger are her own observation, not medical data.

### BIO — Asenath Nicholson (1792–1855)
- CLAIM: Brief biography, with DIB and scholarly citation (Murphy, *Compassionate Stranger*, 2015).
- FINDING (DIB unless marked otherwise):
  - Born Asenath Hatch, **24 Feb 1792, Chelsea, Vermont**; youngest of three children of Michael Hatch (farmer) and Martha Hatch. She taught school in Chelsea from age 16.
  - Married Norman Nicholson, a merchant, in 1831 (he died in 1841); no children. After hearing Sylvester Graham lecture in 1831, she ran boarding houses in New York City built on vegetarian diet, temperance and support for abolition. She helped the Irish poor in New York, including during the 1832 cholera epidemic.
  - First Irish journey **June 1844 to July 1845**, giving out English- and Irish-language Bibles (DIB names the Hibernian Bible Society). Murphy (RTÉ) says she left New York on the *Brooklyn* in May 1844, for a fifteen-month trip. Book: *Ireland's Welcome to the Stranger* (1847).
  - Famine relief: back in Ireland **December 1846** (Murphy) or simply "1847" (DIB), as field agent for the New York Irish Relief Society (DIB). She worked first in Dublin (Murphy/MUSE), spent the winter of 1847–48 in Mayo (Murphy), then went back to Cork (Murphy's chapter title: "Back to Cork … 1848"). DIB mentions her work with Quaker relief agencies and the Presentation nuns, and her friendship with Fr Theobald Mathew in Cork.
  - Left Ireland **September 1848** (DIB) or "the fall of 1848" (Murphy). Back in the US in 1852.
  - Died **15 May 1855, Jersey City, New Jersey, of typhoid fever** (DIB and Murphy). Buried in Green-Wood Cemetery, Brooklyn (Murphy, RTÉ).
  - Works: *Nature's Own Book* (1835); *Ireland's Welcome to the Stranger* (1847); *Lights and Shades of Ireland* (London, 1850); *Annals of the Famine in Ireland* (New York, 1851). Per the IA catalogue record, the *Annals* is "part III, enlarged" of *Lights and Shades*.
- SOURCES:
  - Deirdre Bryan, "Nicholson, Asenath", *Dictionary of Irish Biography* (Royal Irish Academy, Oct 2009), doi:10.3318/dib.006207.v1, https://www.dib.ie/biography/nicholson-asenath-a6207
  - Maureen O'Rourke Murphy, *Compassionate Stranger: Asenath Nicholson and the Great Irish Famine* (Syracuse University Press, Irish Studies series, 2015), 440 pp., ISBN 9780815610441 (cloth) / 9780815652892 (e-book); TOC from Project MUSE, https://muse.jhu.edu/book/36909 . Ch. 5, "'If This Cup May Not Pass Away from Me': Nicholson Begins Her Famine Mission", pp. 145–174, is the chapter for the Kingstown/Dublin period.
  - Maureen Murphy, "Asenath Nicholson: the American who described the Great Famine", RTÉ History, 20 Aug 2020 (from the Atlas of the Great Irish Famine, Cork University Press).
  - Also cited in the DIB: Maureen Murphy, "Asenath Nicholson and the famine in Ireland", in M. G. Valiulis and M. O'Dowd (eds), *Women and Irish History* (1997); Murphy's introduction to the 1998 Lilliput edition.
- STATUS: VERIFIED (two independent scholarly sources). Arrival date CONTESTED in detail (December 1846 vs. "1847").
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none needed.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Safe line: "Asenath Nicholson (1792–1855), a Vermont-born teacher and reformer, came back to Ireland in the winter of 1846–47 to give relief, and stayed until the autumn of 1848." The spellings "Grahamite" and "Green-Wood" weren't checked against the sources.

### CUMMINS — Nicholas Cummins to the Duke of Wellington, *The Times*, 24 Dec 1846
- CLAIM: "In the first [hovel], six famished and ghastly skeletons..."; exact wording and date.
- FINDING: The wording was read in **one secondary transcription only** (Marjie Bloy's Peel Web). It matches the brief's phrase. Peel Web's framing: "On 15 December 1846, one of the Cork magistrates, Mr Nicholas Cummins, visited Skibbereen and sent a letter…" and the source is *The Times*, 24 December 1846. Note that **15 December is the date of the visit there**, not necessarily the date of the letter. The worker remembers, but did not check, that the letter's own date line is "Cork, December 17". Peel Web doesn't print the date line or the salutation. The transcription, joined from exact pieces:
  > Being aware that I should have to witness scenes of frightful hunger, I provided myself with as much bread as five men could carry, and on reaching the spot I was surprised to find the wretched hamlet apparently deserted. I entered some of the hovels to ascertain the cause, and the scenes which presented themselves were such as no tongue or pen can convey the slightest idea of. In the first, six famished and ghastly skeletons, to all appearances dead, were huddled in a corner on some filthy straw, their sole covering what seemed a ragged horsecloth, their wretched legs hanging about, naked above the knees. I approached with horror, and found by a low moaning they were alive [punctuation at this join not captured — probably a dash] they were in fever, four children, a woman and what had once been a man. It is impossible to go through the detail. Suffice it to say, that in a few minutes I was surrounded by at least 200 such phantoms, such frightful spectres as no words can describe, either from famine or from fever.

  Search results also show the "six famished and ghastly skeletons" phrase on the Irish Times "Famine Diary" series, irishmemorial.org, Victorian Web, and John O'Rourke's *History of the Great Irish Famine of 1847* (Project Gutenberg #14412). None of those were read.
