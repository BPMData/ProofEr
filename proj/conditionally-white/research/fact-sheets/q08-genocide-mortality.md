## Headline findings

1. **Wrong name in the brief.** It is **Cormac Ó Gráda** (UCD economic historian), not "Ciarán Ó Gráda" (brief §5, Ch5 readout plan). Fix everywhere.
2. **Ó Gráda does not support "premised on racialized contempt".** In *Black '47 and Beyond* (1999), p. 10 (Introduction), he writes that if policy failure caused deaths, "they were largely the by-product of a dogmatic version of political economy, not the deliberate outcome of anti-Irish racism" (SECONDARY: read only as quoted by Margaret Collins; the exact wording needs a check against the book). The brief's summary of his position ("premised on contempt and laissez-faire doctrine") overstates the contempt half. His main point is doctrine, not race.
3. **"Pre-famine population ~8.5M (1841 census)" mixes two numbers.** The 1841 census counted **8,175,124**. About 8.4M (1841) and about 8.7M (1846) are modern corrections for undercounting (O'Keeffe, RTÉ 2020). Label it "8.18M counted in 1841; about 8.5M or more by the eve of the famine (estimate)". The 1851 census counted **6,552,385** (Woodham-Smith p. 411, via Wikipedia; SECONDARY).
4. **Excess deaths: "about 1 million" is the consensus central figure; "1.0–1.5M" is not the right error bar.** Boyle & Ó Gráda (1986) give "one million", leaving out deaths among emigrants. Mokyr (1983) gives 1.1–1.5M for 1846–51, and his upper bound includes averted births (a population deficit, not deaths). Cousens' 800,000 is now considered too low. Honest wording: "about a million excess deaths (estimates run from below a million to about 1.1 million; more if births that never happened are counted)".
5. **Ruth Dudley Edwards quote: venue found.** It is from a *Sunday Independent* article dated **4 October 2015**, as quoted in a reader's reply letter (Christopher Fogarty, *Sunday Independent*, 11 Oct 2015). It is **not** from *The Faithful Tribe*. The original column was not read; another worker owns this check.
6. **Peter Gray, the leading historian of famine ideology, reviewed Coogan's *The Famine Plot* as "Polemic without plausibility"** (*Irish Times*, 19 Jan 2013). He accepts **moral culpability** for the failure, from late 1847, to provide affordable food and employment, but rejects **genocidal intent**. This is the cleanest one-source statement of the mainstream line.
7. **Liam Kennedy's famine/genocide chapter in *Unhappy the Land* (2016) is "Cry Holocaust" (pp. 81–104)**, per the IHR review. The title "Genocide in Ireland?" in the task prompt was **not confirmed**. A later chapter apparently discusses Lemkin (reviewer: "There was no sign, however, that Lemkin considered the Irish Famine as falling within his definition").

### Q8-04 — Tim Pat Coogan, *The Famine Plot* (2012), and its critics
- CLAIM: Coogan argues genocide; historians criticize him.
- FINDING: Coogan (*The Famine Plot: England's Role in Ireland's Greatest Tragedy*, Palgrave Macmillan, 2012) argues the famine was "an early example of ethnic cleansing and one of the first acts of genocide". He invokes the 1948 UN definition's "intent to destroy" and says the famine "ticks the boxes". He cites A.J.P. Taylor's verdict that the word "genocidal" could not be avoided.
  - Critics: **Peter Gray** (*Irish Times*, 19 Jan 2013) calls the book "a rather reductionist polemic" that "restates the Mitchelite case". Gray says it never explains why the government intervened at all (soup kitchens, promotion of charity), that it treats Trevelyan's evangelical providentialism as cover for anti-Irish racism instead of taking it seriously, and that the evidence supports moral culpability, not intent.
  - **Liam Kennedy** (BBC NI *Sunday Sequence* debate with Coogan, aired 10 Dec 2019; transcript in *Dublin Review of Books*, 18 Nov 2020): "The issue of intentionality is central to the whole discussion of genocide"; Coogan "failed utterly to establish" intent; "there is no smoking gun"; "there is no case for genocide."

## Part 2 — Mortality, emigration, census

### Q8-11 — Excess mortality: the ~1,000,000 figure and its range
- CLAIM (brief): "~1,000,000 (range 1.0–1.5M in older literature; Ó Gráda's ~1M)".
- FINDING:
  - **Boyle & Ó Gráda (1986):** "Ignoring deaths among emigrants, it is estimated that one million individuals perished as a result of the Famine" (abstract; p. 554 per Wikipedia's citation).
  - **Mokyr (1983), *Why Ireland Starved*, pp. 266–267, Table 9.1:** county estimates for 1846–51 summing to about 1.1M (lower bound, excess deaths) to about 1.5M (upper bound, which adds averted births). Ireland-wide annual averages are **24.3 (lower) and 33 (upper) per 1,000** (AEF paper citing Table 9.1). Ó Gráda (2017) gives Ireland 24.4 / 33.1, citing Mokyr 1985: 266–7 (the 2nd edition).
  - **Mokyr & Ó Gráda (1999 paper):** excess deaths "at over one million (Mokyr 1980, Boyle and Ó Gráda 1986)"; "excess mortality during the Famine (not including averted births) came to about 1.1 million"; "a total of 1.9 million people dying in Ireland in those five years" (all deaths, not excess).
  - **Mokyr & Ó Gráda, *European Review of Economic History*:** "The Irish Famine killed over a million people who would not have died otherwise."
  - **Older, lower figures:** S.H. Cousens (1960): just over 800,000 for 1846–50, now considered too low by about 200,000 (O'Keeffe). O'Keeffe also gives "about 1.4 million" if averted births are included.
  - **Curran & Fröling (2010):** "Estimates of deaths due to the Famine over the 1845–1852 period range from 800,000 to 1,500,000" (attributed to Mokyr 1980).

### Q8-12 — Regional distribution: which counties were worst
- CLAIM: Mayo, Sligo, Roscommon, Galway, Leitrim, Cork worst; rates per 1,000.
- FINDING:
  - **Connacht** carried **40.4%** of all excess deaths (almost 437,000, per Mokyr); **Leinster 8.6%** (O'Keeffe). Mokyr places the highest incidence in Connacht and in Cavan, Clare and Cork (O'Keeffe).
  - Across counties, Mokyr's average annual excess death rates run **from 8.6 to 60.5 per 1,000** (AEF 2024). The paper did not say which county or bound gives 60.5. It is very probably Mayo, which Mokyr & Ó Gráda call "probably the worst hit county in Ireland", but this is UNVERIFIED.
  - Verified county figures (Ó Gráda 2017, Table 1, from Mokyr): **Cavan 42.7 (lower) – 51.8 (upper)** per 1,000 per year 1846–51; Fermanagh 29.2–39.1; Monaghan 28.6–36.0; Derry 5.7–10.1 (least affected in Ulster); Ulster 18.9–25.6; **Ireland 24.4–33.1**.

The famine killed "roughly twice as many people in proportion in Munster and in Connacht" as in Ulster and Leinster (ERoEH article).
  - Clare: average excess mortality of 8.4 percent over the five famine years (O'Keeffe).
  - Population decline 1841–51 by province (Wikipedia table, no source given; LEAD ONLY): Connacht 28.8%, Munster 22.5%, Ulster 15.7%, Leinster 15.3%, Ireland 20%.

## Part 3 — How far the literature supports each word of "famine made lethal by policy premised on racialized contempt"

### Q8-15 — "made lethal by policy"
- FINDING: **Strongly supported, with one hedge.** Gray: moral culpability for policy after late 1847; "culpable neglect" called "indisputable". Ó Gráda (per Solow): relief was "too little, too slow, too conditional, and cut off too soon". Kennedy: responses "grossly inadequate" and the famine not economically inevitable. Mokyr (per Solow, paraphrase): Britain could have saved Ireland had it the will. The hedge: Ó Gráda holds that the fundamental problem until mid-1847 was absolute food shortage. So the blight made the famine, and policy made it **far more lethal**, especially from 1847 on, when the soup kitchens closed and the Poor Law became the only relief.
- STATUS: VERIFIED (as a mainstream position).
- SUGGESTED WORDING: "a famine that policy made far deadlier".

### Q8-16 — "premised on"
- FINDING: **Supported if the premise is doctrine.** The literature agrees policy rested on premises: laissez-faire political economy (Ó Gráda: "dogmatic version of political economy"), providentialism (Trevelyan's "direct stroke of an all-wise and all-merciful Providence", quoted in Donnelly; Gray on evangelical providentialism) and moralism about Irish character (Gray, *Famine, Land and Politics*: not checked this session). "Premised on contempt" as the main premise goes beyond Ó Gráda and Gray. Both treat ideology as the premise, and prejudice at most as reinforcing it. The 1997 *Irish Times* writer's formula is the best phrasing of the middle position: "Racial and sectarian prejudices confirmed economic dogma" (Brendan Ó Cathaoir's own sentence, not a historian's verdict).
- STATUS: CONTESTED (doctrine VERIFIED; contempt-as-premise contested).
- SUGGESTED WORDING: "policy premised on doctrine and hardened by contempt".

### Q8-17 — "racialized"
- FINDING: **The most contested word.**
  - For: Donnelly reports English opinion "often betrayed vehement racialist prejudices" before 1848 (citing Brian Osborne). L. Perry Curtis Jr., *Anglo-Saxons and Celts: A Study of Anti-Irish Prejudice in Victorian England* (Conference on British Studies, Bridgeport CT, 1968) and *Apes and Angels: The Irishman in Victorian Caricature* (David & Charles, 1971; rev. ed. Smithsonian, 1997) argue that Victorian anti-Irish attitudes were racial and simianizing. Michael de Nie, *The Eternal Paddy: Irish Identity and the British Press, 1798–1882* (Univ. of Wisconsin Press, 2004) argues the press "racialized" the Irish through a blend of race, religion and class.
  - Against: Sheridan Gilley, "English Attitudes to the Irish in England, 1780–1900", in Colin Holmes (ed.), *Immigrants and Minorities in British Society* (Allen & Unwin, 1978), argues that stereotypes were mostly cultural, religious and class-based, often with sympathy alongside, and not racial in the biological sense. Ó Gráda explicitly rejects "anti-Irish racism" as the deliberate cause of deaths. Gray faults Coogan for reducing providentialism to racism.
  - The Curtis, de Nie and Gilley bibliographic details and positions are from my background knowledge and were NOT read this session. Verify titles, years and the specific claims before citing.
- STATUS: CONTESTED.
- SUGGESTED WORDING: say what is documentable (racialized *language* about the Irish was common in the press and among some officials) and do not claim race drove policy. For example: "contempt that the era's press and some officials voiced in the language of race".

### Q8-18 — "contempt"
- FINDING: **Supported for the attitudes of specific actors, not as a historians' consensus term.** The quotes wall (Trevelyan "selfish, perverse and turbulent", Clarendon, *The Times*) documents contemptuous official and press attitudes. Those quotes are verified by Q1–Q4 workers, not here. Solow's review relays Colm Tóibín's charge of "official contempt". Tóibín is a novelist, not a historian of the famine. The historians read here prefer "dogma", "ideology", "moralism", "providentialism", "neglect".
- STATUS: PROBABLE (as description of attitudes); UNVERIFIED as a mainstream historians' characterization of policy.
- SUGGESTED WORDING: show the contempt through the quotes, and let the historians' words ("doctrine", "culpable neglect") carry the verdict.
