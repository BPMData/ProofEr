## Q1 · Ch6 institutions (police, fire companies, Tammany/Fourth Ward machines, Draft Riots vs. Union ranks — F50)

### 1. New York & Tammany Hall (Allen vol. 1, pp. 184–187, 195–196; Ignatiev p. 80; Roediger pp. 107, 140–141)
- **Electoral bargain & **$250** Black property test:** NY 1821 Constitution gave white manhood suffrage while imposing an explicit $250 property test exclusively on Black voters; Tammany defeated equal-suffrage restoration in 1826 and 1846 (Sixth & Fourteenth Wards, Allen pp. 184–185).
- **Police & patronage:** `"Graft-rich government contracts afforded benefits to Irish-American ward heelers, who had important local appointive powers, including appointment of the police in each ward."` (Allen vol. 1 p. 187, citing Ernst 1949 & Myers 1917). `"The historical affinity of the generality of Irish-Americans and Tammany Hall was thus from the beginning conditioned on denial of the rights of African-Americans, and on a concomitant attachment to the national program of the slaveholders."` (p. 187).
- **Custom House & docks (1861–63):** *The Leader* (15 June 1861) protested Black appointee Robert Vosburgh at the NY Custom House (Allen pp. 195–196). By **1863**, `"[a]lmost all longshoremen in New York City were Irish"` and resolved through the Longshoremen's Association to restrict dock work to `"white laborers"`; Manhattan's Black population fell 16% (1840–60) and ~25% (1860–65, to <10,000) (Allen p. 196).
- **Roediger's nuance (pp. 140–141):** `"Irish Catholics were welcomed as voters, party members and political muscle, though not typically as officeholders, by Democrats before the Civil War"` (citing Montgomery 1967).

### 2. Philadelphia police, volunteer fire companies & Fourth Ward machine (Ignatiev pp. 133, 143–144, 154–164, 169, 172–175)
- **Pre-consolidation:** 1833 ordinance: 24 day constables, 120 night watchmen (`"one patrolman for every 3,352 inhabitants"` vs. London's 1 per 434); post-1844 riots: **May 3, 1850** Marshal's Police District (`"one policeman per 400 inhabitants"`); **Feb 2, 1854** Consolidation Act. Black fire company suppressed in 1818 (44 white volunteer companies by 1829, p. 143 & n. 65).
- **1856–57 Irish police turn (pp. 163–164):** Following Democratic Mayor Richard Vaux's 1856 victory (backed by William McMullen's Fourth Ward / Moyamensing Hose), `"six members of the Moyamensing Hose were immediately named police officers"`, and in 1857 Vaux named **Samuel Ruggles** police commissioner (served through 1869).
  > `"The Irish cop is more than a quaint symbol. His appearance on the city police marked a turning point in Philadelphia in the struggle of the Irish to gain the rights of white men. It meant that thereafter the Irish would be officially empowered (armed) to defend themselves from the nativist mobs, and at the same time to carry out their own agenda against black people."` (Ignatiev pp. 163–164)
- **Not found:** overall numerical percentages/rosters for NYPD or Boston police/fire services.

### 3. Draft Riots vs. Union Army (Ignatiev p. 88; Allen pp. 198, 320 n. 77)
- **Ignatiev's comparison (p. 88, citing Bernstein 1990):** `"The number of Irish who took part in the riots was not less than the number who wore the blue uniform. Given that every rioter was a volunteer against official policy and all respectable opinion, it is likely that the riots expressed Irish attitudes at least as much as Irish service in the Union army."`
- **Other side & rhetoric:** Thomas Francis Meagher (Irish Brigade officer, **1864**, via Allen p. 198 citing Athearn): `"suffered themselves to be bamboozled into being obstinate herds in the political field... following with gross stupidity and the stoniest blindness certain worn out old path-ways described for them by their drivers"`. Contrast Catholic archdiocesan *Metropolitan Record* (28 Feb 1863, via Ignatiev p. 88): `'since fight we must, may it not be necessary yet to fight for the liberty of the white man rather than the freedom of the Negro?'`
- **Not found:** exact regimental muster/casualty counts for Irish Union units.

## Q3. O'Connell's 11 Oct 1843 Cincinnati Reply (`O2`) — Ignatiev pp. 28–29

- **Source:** Noel Ignatiev, *How the Irish Became White*, ch. 1, pp. 28–29 (verbatim printed text):
  > "It was not in Ireland you learned this cruelty," he declared. "Your mothers were gentle, kind, and humane ....How can your souls have become stained with a darkness blacker than the negro's skin?" It went beyond previous addresses of his in that it offered practical suggestions for what Irish in America could do to end slavery: first was never again to volunteer on behalf of the oppressor (this was particularly important in view of the threat of war over the U.S. annexation of Texas); it was followed by injunctions to help educate and secure the franchise for the free Negro, and to support political candidates who worked to abolish the internal slave trade and slavery in the District of Columbia. Let the Irish in America take these steps, he implored, and "never cease [their] efforts, until the crime ...of being the worst enemies of the men of color shall be atoned for, and blotted out, and effaced forever."79
- **Verification / caveat:**
  - Ignatiev prints internal ellipses (`....How`, `...of`) himself inside the Cincinnati Address excerpts.
  - Note `79` at the bottom of Ignatiev's chapter reads `79. U.S. Census Manuscripts, 1855, Ernst, Immigrant Life, 69` — a shifted/misnumbered endnote in Ignatiev's book; primary newspaper/AASS tract verification (`Daniel O'Connell upon American Slavery`, 1860) is still required before quoting beyond the two already-cleared sentences (`O2`).
  - Context: Two weeks after the Cincinnati Address O'Connell censured Garrison and praised Smith and Tappan (Ignatiev citing Riach, note 80); on 18 Nov 1843 Boston abolitionists held a Faneuil Hall meeting on Repeal and Slavery.

  > "DESTITUTION AT SKIBBEREEN... Disease and death in every quarter—the once hardy population worn away to emaciated skeletons—fever, dropsy, diarrhoea, and famine rioting in every filthy hovel, and sweeping away whole families—the population perceptibly lessened—death diminishing the destitution—hundreds frantically rushing from their home and country, not with the idea of making fortunes in other lands, but to fly from a scene of suffering and death—400 men starving in one district, having no employment, and 300 more turned off the public works in another district, on a day’s notice—seventy-five tenants ejected here, and a whole village in the last stage of destitution there—relief committees threatening to throw up their mockery of an office, in utter despair—dead bodies of children flung into holes hastily scratched in the earth without shroud or coffin—wives travelling ten miles to beg the charity of a coffin for a dead husband, and bearing it back that weary distance—a government official offering the one-tenth of a sufficient supply of food, at famine prices—neither mill nor corn store within twenty miles—every field becoming a grave, and the land a wilderness."
- **Kilmoe / Skibbereen Workhouse coffins — *Daily News*, 10 Jan 1847, Killen p. 95:**

And one gentleman, a good and charitable man, speaking of this case, says—‘The distress is so appalling, that we must throw away all feelings of delicacy;’ and another says—‘I would rather give 1s. to a starving man than 4s. 6d. for a coffin.’ One hundred and forty have died in the Skibbereen Workhouse in one month; eight have died in one day!
