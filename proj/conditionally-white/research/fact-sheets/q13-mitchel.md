## Headline findings
1. **The brief's "elected MP for Tipperary while in prison" is WRONG as phrased.** In 1875 Mitchel was not in prison. He had escaped from Van Diemen's Land in 1853 and was released from Fort Monroe in 1865. He was elected unopposed on 17 Feb 1875, the House declared him ineligible as a convicted felon who had not served his sentence, he won the March 1875 by-election, and he died 20 March 1875 at Newry. Suggested wording: "elected MP for Tipperary twice in 1875, and twice barred as an unpardoned felon". (The DIB check could not be made: PROBABLE.)
2. **The Bermuda leg is real and should not be compressed away.** The *Jail Journal*'s own chapter heads run "Six Months in Bermuda" → "Ten Months' Bondage — Arrival of the Neptune, Bound for the Cape" → "Van Diemen's Land Appears". Its title page names the convict ship *Neptune*, Pernambuco and the Cape of Good Hope. The route was Dublin → Spike Island → Bermuda hulks (1848–49) → *Neptune* via Pernambuco to the Cape (1849–50, where the ship was refused landing) → Van Diemen's Land (1850) → escape 1853. The brief's "sent to Van Diemen's Land" is compressed, not false. Wikipedia names the Bermuda-to-Cape ship as "Nautilus", but Mitchel's own book says *Neptune*. Use *Neptune*.
3. **The "plantation" quote exists and is datable.** It appeared in *The Citizen* (New York), 14 January 1854, the paper's second issue, in an editorial headed "Mr. Haughton to Mr. Meagher". Three independent transmissions confirm it. Punctuation differs between them ("plantation, well stocked … negroes, in Alabama" vs "plantation well-stocked … negroes in Alabama"), so check the page image before setting it in type.
4. **"Slavery man": not found.** I found no source in which Mitchel calls himself a "slavery man". Don't use the phrase as a quote until someone finds it.
5. **The *Last Conquest* edition is still unsettled here.** The only edition I opened is Glasgow: Cameron, Ferguson, catalogued 1882 (vi, 220 p.). The "1861" and "1876" dates in the pack were not confirmed in this run (another worker has the page anchors).
6. **The sons.** Willie was killed at Gettysburg in July 1863 and John at Fort Sumter in July 1864 (Wikipedia lead, citing McGovern pp. 179–189). That **James lost an arm** is UNVERIFIED: nothing I read says it.

### Q13.1 — Pre-famine/famine career, conviction, transportation route
- CLAIM (brief): "Mitchel was convicted in 1848 and sent to Van Diemen's Land, escaped in 1853" (✱ check Bermuda). The Q13 prompt adds Young Ireland, *The Nation*, the *United Irishman* (1848), arrest and conviction under the Treason Felony Act (May 1848, 14 years' transportation).
- FINDING:
  - Conviction and sentence: arrested May 1848 under the new treason-felony legislation; tried and convicted May 1848; sentenced to 14 years' transportation. The Wikipedia lead gives the trial as "June 1848", which is probably an error, since standard accounts put the trial and sentence at 26–27 May 1848. The exact days are UNVERIFIED in this run.
  - The route is confirmed from Mitchel's own *Jail Journal* (Gill edition, which reprints the *Citizen* text). Title page: "commenced on board the 'Shearwater' steamer, in Dublin bay", continued "on board the 'Neptune' convict ship — at Pernambuco — at the Cape of Good Hope". Contents: ch. VI "Six Months in Bermuda"; ch. VIII "Ten Months' Bondage — Arrival of the Neptune, Bound for the Cape"; ch. XIV "Van Diemen's Land Appears". The Wikipedia lead (citing Russell 2015, pp. 75, 83–85) says he went by HMS *Scourge* to Ireland Island, Bermuda, spent about a year on the hulk *Dromedary*, and was then held about five months off the Cape during the 1849 anti-convict crisis.
  - Dates: Bermuda arrival June 1848 and departure April 1849; Cape Town Sept 1849 – Feb 1850; Hobart April 1850; escape June 1853, reaching New York late 1853. These are PROBABLE (standard accounts, consistent with the chapter heads "Six Months" and "Ten Months"). I did not read them from a page.
  - Young Ireland, *The Nation*, *United Irishman* (founded Feb 1848): not re-verified in this run, because the DIB was unreachable. They are uncontested in every summary I saw.

### Q13.3a — The "plantation … healthy negroes … Alabama" quote
- CLAIM (prompt): "a good plantation, well-stocked with healthy negroes in Alabama". Find the exact wording, date and publication.
- FINDING: *The Citizen* (New York), 14 January 1854 (the second issue), editorial "Mr. Haughton to Mr. Meagher". It replies to the Dublin abolitionist James Haughton's appeal that Meagher and the Young Irelanders in America oppose slavery. Three independent chains:
  1. A transcription "from scans of The Citizen" (cartlann.org, PDF): "We deny that it is a crime, or a wrong, or even a peccadillo, to hold slaves" … "wish we had a good plantation well-stocked with healthy negroes in Alabama." In the same editorial Mitchel says of the Irish poor: "Slaves, we say, with no more rights, social or political, than Alabama negroes" … "than Alabama negroes – the difference being that an Alabama negro is of value to his master". The paragraph also says "We are not abolitionists".
  2. A contemporary quotation: D[aniel] Plumb to Frederick Douglass, New York, 14 Feb 1854: "We, for our part, wish we had a good plantation well stocked with healthy negroes in Alabama." He also quotes "We deny that it is a crime, or a wrong, or even a pecca dillo [OCR line-break], to hold slaves…".
  3. A scholarly edition: *The Frederick Douglass Papers*, Series 1, *Speeches, Debates, and Interviews*, vol. 2 (Yale UP, 1982), note 12 to Douglass's address of 10 May 1854. It dates the passage to the *Citizen*'s second issue, the reply to Haughton, and quotes: "we will not be silent when occasion calls for speech; and as for being a participator in the wrongs," … "we, for our part, wish we had a good plantation, well stocked with healthy negroes, in Alabama." It also quotes the denial that it is "a crime, or a wrong or even a peccadillo, to hold slaves, to buy slaves, to sell slaves," … "to keep slaves to their work by flogging or other needful coercion."
  - Reconstructed full denial sentence (the three chains overlap; check against the page image): "We deny that it is a crime, or a wrong, or even a peccadillo, to hold slaves, to buy slaves, to sell slaves, to keep slaves to their work by flogging or other needful coercion."
  - Punctuation varies across the three transcriptions: "plantation, well stocked … negroes, in Alabama" (Douglass Papers), "plantation well stocked … in Alabama" (Plumb), "plantation well-stocked … in Alabama" (cartlann).
- SOURCES:
  - *The Citizen* (New York), 14 Jan 1854, "Mr. Haughton to Mr. Meagher", transcription at cartlann.org (HTML page and PDF; page image not seen).
  - D. Plumb to Frederick Douglass, 14 Feb 1854, Frederick Douglass Papers Project digital edition, item 9358.
  - Frederick Douglass, "We Are in the Midst of a Moral Revolution", New York, 10 May 1854, *Frederick Douglass' Paper*, 19 May 1854. In *FDP* Ser. 1 vol. 2 (Yale, 1982), editorial note 12; FDPP digital edition item 16048.
  - Wikipedia (LEAD) cites Chenevix Trench (1984), p. 274, and Kennedy (2016), p. 215 for the same quote.
- STATUS: VERIFIED (wording and date via three independent chains; final punctuation needs the page image).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "We deny that it is a crime, or a wrong, or even a peccadillo, to hold slaves, to buy slaves, to sell slaves, to keep slaves to their work by flogging or other needful coercion." … "we, for our part, wish we had a good plantation, well stocked with healthy negroes, in Alabama." — John Mitchel, editorial, *The Citizen* (New York), 14 January 1854 (punctuation as in *The Frederick Douglass Papers*, ser. 1 vol. 2, Yale 1982, n. 12; confirm against the page).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The essay can use this quote with full force. The "Alabama negroes" comparison in the same editorial is the bargain in one breath: Irish misery measured against Black bondage, and the Black slave's "value to his master" used as the yardstick. Quote it, then let it sit.

### Q13.3b — The reaction (Douglass; Richmond's welcome)
- FINDING: Douglass, in his 10 May 1854 address, alludes to Mitchel without naming him: "By simply wishing for a plantation of negroes and for the privilege of urging them to toil under the sting of the whip," … "the vulgar traitor to liberty, who shall be nameless here, gets himself invited to visit New Orleans." The Yale editors correct him: the invitation came in spring 1854 from Richmond's mayor and council. At a late-May banquet Mitchel was told Virginians welcomed him "because you wished for a plantation in Alabama, and vindicated slavery against Reverend Beecher." The editors also say *Frederick Douglass' Paper* called him a "traitor to humanity". The Plumb letter (14 Feb 1854) quotes Mitchel's San Francisco arrival speech: "'we are not negroes,' says Mr. Mitchel".
- SOURCES: FDPP items 16048 and 9358 (above).
- STATUS: Douglass passage VERIFIED (primary speech text). Richmond toast SECONDARY (via the Yale editors' note). "We are not negroes" SECONDARY (a hostile contemporary quoting him; the speech text itself was not found).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: Douglass: "the vulgar traitor to liberty, who shall be nameless here" — *Frederick Douglass' Paper*, 19 May 1854 (FDP ser. 1 vol. 2).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: This gives the essay the Black abolitionist's verdict in his own words, in the same month.

### Q13.6 — 1875: Tipperary and death
- CLAIM (prompt): "elected MP for Tipperary while in prison, died 1875".
- FINDING: **The "while in prison" part is incorrect.** Mitchel's imprisonments were 1848–53 (transportation, ended by escape) and 1865 (Fort Monroe). In 1875 he was at liberty but legally an undischarged convicted felon, because he had escaped rather than served his sentence. Sequence (Wikipedia lead, citing Russell 2015, p. 193, and A. M. Sullivan): elected unopposed for Tipperary on 17 Feb 1875; the House of Commons declared him ineligible as a felon; re-elected at the March 1875 by-election with about 80% of the vote; on petition the seat went to his Conservative opponent (Stephen Moore) after Mitchel's death. He died 20 March 1875 at Drumalane, Newry. Contemporary anchors cited by the lead: *Freeman's Journal*, 17 Mar 1875; *Standard*, 22 Mar 1875; *New York Times*, 21 Mar 1875. The DIB check could not be made.
- STATUS: the correction PROBABLE (strong; no source of any kind places him in prison in 1875). Death date and place PROBABLE (consistent everywhere).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Write "In 1875 Tipperary elected him to Parliament twice; twice Westminster refused to seat a convicted felon. He died in Newry that March." Drop "while in prison".
