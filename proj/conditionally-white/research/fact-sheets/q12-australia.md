"Death or Liberty" was a shouted slogan and the answer to a parley, not a flag. **No "1848 'Death or Liberty' flag" was found in any Australian institution.** The image list should not show one. The real flag objects are: (a) the **Eureka flag, 1854**, the original held in Ballarat by the Art Gallery of Ballarat (the source calls it the "Ballarat Fine Art Gallery"), with a replica held by the NLA, which I saw in a snippet only; and (b) the **"Roll Up, No Chinese" banner** at Young, NSW. Killed in the fight: 12 (Whitaker), about 15 (NMA), about 21 (O'Donnell). Total deaths: "at least 39" (NMA) or "more than 30" (NSW State Archives). Executions: **9** in total, which the sources agree on. Show the deaths as a range.

The NSW State Heritage Register says the maker and date are *unknown*, that McCarthy only *hid* it after the riots, and that the Young Historical Society *acquired* it in **1964**. The holder is the Young Historical Society Inc., and the banner is shown at the Lambing Flat Folk Museum, which now also goes by the name Young Historical Museum.

The Irish evidence I could document is: **James Torpy, "an Irish publican"**, a Miners' Protection League delegate who was arrested and later acquitted; and the 30 June 1861 roll-up gathering at **Tipperary Gully**. The other named ringleaders were a Scot (John Stewart) and an ex-Glasgow police captain (Donald Cameron). Scholarship on the rioters' origins (Curthoys, Schamberger, Reeves) was **not reached** because the search budget ran out. Do not claim that the rioters were Irish.
- **Eureka is the strongest Irish-specific number.** Peter Lalor's casualty list, as relayed on Eurekapedia, gives Irish origins for **10 of the 18 dead whose origin is stated**. This is a secondary relay of a primary list. But historian John Molony (Senate lecture, 2004) stresses that the early Reform League leaders were **not** Irish.

### Q12-1a — Castle Hill uprising, 4–5 March 1804: events and numbers
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): Irish convicts rose at Castle Hill on 4–5 March 1804 under the cry "Death or Liberty". The rising was defeated at "Vinegar Hill" (Rouse Hill), and executions followed.
- FINDING: The date is confirmed. The rising began on the evening of Sunday 4 March 1804. On 5 March, Major George Johnston's NSW Corps detachment, with militia, defeated the rebels near what is now Rouse Hill, then called "Vinegar Hill". Governor King declared martial law for the districts of Parramatta, Castle Hill, Toongabbie, Prospect, Seven Hills and Hills by proclamation dated 5 March 1804. This was the colony's first declaration of martial law. The leaders were the Irishmen Phillip Cunningham and William Johnston. Asked what they wanted, the rebels answered "death or liberty, and a ship to take us home" (NMA's wording). The numbers vary by source:
  - Rebels: about 200 broke out (Migration Heritage, O'Donnell). Up to 300 mustered (NMA, O'Donnell). The contemporary watercolour inscription reads "two hundred and sixty six armed rebels".
  - Killed in the fight: 12 (Whitaker), about 15 (NMA, Migration Heritage), about 21 "with no garrison fatalities" (O'Donnell).
  - Total deaths, including the pursuit and executions: "at least 39" (NMA), "more than 30" (NSW State Archives), "may have exceeded 30" (O'Donnell).
  - Executions: 9 in total (Whitaker, NMA). Cunningham was hanged at Windsor on 6 March without trial (Migration Heritage, NSW State Archives). O'Donnell gives 8 hanged after drumhead courts martial on 8 March, which with Cunningham makes 9. Johnston and Samuel Humes were hanged and their bodies hung in chains (Migration Heritage).
  - Secondary punishments: 7 men flogged with 200 to 500 lashes. They and 23 others were sent to the Coal River (Newcastle) chain gang (NMA). Migration Heritage says 9 leaders were flogged and sent to Coal River, and others went to Norfolk Island. The two accounts differ, so report this loosely.
  - Background: the rising was "tied closely to the 1798 rebellion in Ireland, and sparked by news of further uprisings in Ireland in 1803" (Whitaker). By 1800 NSW was "at least one-third Irish" (O'Donnell).
- SOURCES:

  - Watercolour "Convict uprising at Castle Hill, 1804", artist unknown, National Library of Australia, 312 × 413 mm. Inscription as quoted by NSW Migration Heritage Centre, *Objects Through Time*: https://www.migrationheritage.nsw.gov.au/exhibition/objectsthroughtime/1804-convict-uprising-at-castle-hill-watercolour (PRIMARY image, read through a secondary page).
  - Anne-Maree Whitaker, "Castle Hill convict rebellion 1804", *Dictionary of Sydney*, 2009: https://dictionaryofsydney.org/index.php/node/6519
  - National Museum of Australia, *Defining Moments*: "Castle Hill rebellion": https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/castle-hill-rebellion
  - Ruan O'Donnell (University of Limerick), "Castle Hill and Vinegar Hill: the Australian Rising of 1804", *History Ireland* (c.2004; issue not stated on the page): https://historyireland.com/?p=5179
  - The Hills Shire Council, "Six little-known facts about the Castle Hill convict rebellion" (2024): https://www.thehills.nsw.gov.au/News-and-Publications/Six-little-known-facts-about-the-Castle-Hill-convict-rebellion (local council; supports martial law "first and only" and the Rouse Hill / Vinegar Hill name).

  - State Library NSW: not reached because the budget ran out.
- STATUS: VERIFIED for the date, leaders, martial law and slogan; 9 executions is VERIFIED (two sources agree). CONTESTED for the rebel and death counts (give ranges).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "Uprising by Irish prisoners at Castle Hill, north-west of Sydney … put down by the New South Wales Corps with 12 rebels killed and 9 later executed." (Whitaker, *Dictionary of Sydney*, 2009.) Also: "Governor Philip Gidley King was obliged to declare martial law for the first time in Australian history" (O'Donnell, *History Ireland*). The parley answer, as NMA prints it: "death or liberty, and a ship to take us home".
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Use "about 200–300 rebels; between 12 and about 21 killed in the fight, 30–39+ dead in all; 9 executed". "Vinegar Hill" was a deliberate echo of Wexford 1798.

### Q12-1b — The "Death or Liberty" flag: what object actually exists?
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): a "1848 'Death or Liberty' flag".
- FINDING: **No such object was located.** No account of Castle Hill 1804 that I read mentions a flag or banner. The sources are NMA, Whitaker, O'Donnell, Migration Heritage, NSW State Archives and The Hills Shire. "Death or Liberty" appears only as a shouted slogan and a parley answer. One search for "Death or Liberty" flag 1848 in Australia returned nothing relevant. The results were the Irish tricolour story (Meagher, April 1848, in Ireland; Meagher was later transported to Van Diemen's Land) and the Eureka flag. The Hills Shire notes only that "the code used in the rebellion resurfaced during the Eureka Stockade in Ballarat in 1854", meaning the password "Vinegar Hill" (see Q12-5). Real flag objects that can be pictured:
  - **Eureka flag (Southern Cross), 1854**, original. Migration Heritage names the holder as the Ballarat Fine Art Gallery (= Art Gallery of Ballarat). It was first flown at Bakery Hill on 29 November 1854 and at the Stockade on 3 December 1854. The page attributes it to Anastasia Withers, Anne Duke and Anastasia Hayes, from a design by Henry Ross. I did not verify where it is currently on display.
  - **NLA replica** of the Eureka flag ("[Replica of the flag flown by the miners at the Eureka Stockade, 1854] [realia]", nla.obj-141751288). Snippet only.
  - **"Roll Up, No Chinese" banner**, 1860–61, Young NSW (see Q12-2b). It has a painted Southern Cross.
  - The 1804 watercolour at the NLA (Q12-1a) is the only contemporary image of Castle Hill. It shows no flag that the sources describe.
  - Modern Castle Hill re-enactments and memorials exist (a 2004 Irish Times story on the re-enactment, a Monument Australia "Death or Liberty" entry), **snippet only**. Any "Death or Liberty" banner seen today is likely a re-enactment prop. This is UNVERIFIED.

### Q12-2a — Lambing Flat riots (Burrangong goldfield), 1860–61, and Irish involvement
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): Irish participation in anti-Chinese agitation at Lambing Flat, especially 30 June 1861.
- FINDING: The Miners' Protection League was formed at Lambing Flat on 31 January 1861 by about 80 men. Its first recorded use of the "Roll Up, No Chinese" banner was at a small riot on **18 June 1861**. On **Sunday 30 June 1861** a roll-up headed by the banner gathered at **Tipperary Gully**, "a mob of around 3000 men formed and rioted across the field", and Chinese camps were attacked. About 1,200 Chinese miners took refuge at Currawang Station. On **14 July 1861**, "between 500–1000 rioters" attacked the Gold Commissioners' Camp, and one miner, William Lupton, was killed. Of 17 men tried over 30 June, one (William Spicer) was convicted. Of 3 tried over 14 July, one (Claremont Owen) was convicted. **On origins**, the heritage listing calls the rioters "European miners and settlers". The named figures are James Torpy "(an Irish publican)", a miners' delegate who was arrested and acquitted; John Stewart, a Scottish weaver and ex-soldier; and Donald Cameron, an ex-Glasgow police captain. Migration Heritage likewise says only "European miners". The place-name Tipperary Gully suggests an Irish presence on the field. It is not evidence of who rioted. The scholarship (Curthoys, "Men of all nations, except Chinamen" (2001); Schamberger; Reeves) was **not read**, because the search budget ran out.
- SOURCES:
  - NSW State Heritage Register, "Lambing Flat Riot Site (14 July 1861) and Associated Banner", SHR 02047, gazetted 25 March 2022: https://apps.environment.nsw.gov.au/dpcheritageapp/ViewHeritageItemDetails.aspx?ID=5066415
  - NSW Migration Heritage Centre, "1860 Lambing Flat Roll Up Banner": https://www.migrationheritage.nsw.gov.au/exhibition/objectsthroughtime/lambingflatsbanner/index.html
- STATUS: VERIFIED for the events, dates and crowd sizes (one official source plus the Griffin account it cites; Migration Heritage supports "three thousand"). VERIFIED that Torpy was Irish. UNVERIFIED for any general claim that the rioters were Irish.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "On Sunday 30 June, another 'Roll Up' was called with the use of the 'Roll Up, No Chinese' banner at Tipperary Gully." and "and James Torpy (an Irish publican)." (NSW State Heritage Register, SHR 02047.)
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The honest line is that Irish-born men were among the leaders and the crowd, alongside Scots, English and others. It was a pan-"European" mob. Name Torpy. Do not say "the Irish led the riots".

### Q12-2b — The "Roll Up, No Chinese" banner: date and holder
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): the "Roll Up" banner, held at the Lambing Flat Folk Museum, Young NSW.
- FINDING: **Holder:** the Young Historical Society Inc., displayed in the Lambing Flat Folk Museum in Young, NSW. Directory listings now also give the name "Young Historical Museum"; I did not verify which name is current. **Date:** the State Heritage Register says the maker and date are "unknown … thought to be the work of a master sign writer", with first recorded use on 18 June 1861. Migration Heritage gives "c.1860" and says Tom McCarthy made it on the goldfield. The heritage listing says McCarthy, "one of the bannermen", **hid** it after the riots. **Acquisition:** 1964, with help from the Young Services and Citizens Club (State Heritage Register), or "c.1958" (Migration Heritage). **Description:** sail-cloth or cotton with a hand-painted Southern Cross; slogan "Roll Up, Roll Up, No Chinese". Size: about 1.83 m square (heritage listing) or about 1.2 × 1.2 m (Migration Heritage). It flew at William Lupton's funeral on 16 July 1861. Whether it was used on 14 July is "unclear".
- SOURCES: as Q12-2a.
- STATUS: VERIFIED for the holder and slogan. CONTESTED for the maker, date, acquisition year and size.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "A small riot on the 18 June 1861 is the first recorded instance of the use of the 'Roll-Up, No Chinese' banner." (NSW State Heritage Register, SHR 02047.)
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Caption: "'Roll Up, No Chinese' banner, Lambing Flat, c.1860–61. Young Historical Society, Young NSW." Do not call McCarthy the maker as fact, and do not call him Irish on the strength of his surname. The Southern Cross on the banner is a striking link to Eureka.

### Q12-5 — Eureka, 3 December 1854: Irish figures and the Irish dead
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): Eureka's Irish figures (Lalor, the oath, the Southern Cross), and the number of Irish among the dead, as a complicating anecdote.
- FINDING: Peter Lalor was born on 5 February 1827 in Raheen parish, Queen's County (Laois), Ireland (ADB, Ian Turner, 1974; header only read). He arrived in 1852 and was on the Ballarat field by 1854 (SLV). At Bakery Hill on 29–30 November 1854 he led the oath under the Southern Cross flag. The stockade was taken at first light on Sunday 3 December 1854 in about 15 minutes. "At least 22 miners, including one woman, and five soldiers were killed" (NMA, which elsewhere says five or six soldiers). Migration Heritage gives 22. Lalor lost an arm, went on to sit in the Victorian parliament, and resigned in 1887. SLV describes his politics as inconsistent, for example favouring land sales to the middle class rather than working men. **Irish dead:** Lalor's own casualty list, as quoted on Eurekapedia, gives Irish counties or "Ireland" for 10 of the 18 dead with a stated origin: Hynes, Diamond, Gittens, O'Neil, Mullins, Donaghy, Quin, Moore, Brown and McGlyn. The *Freeman's Journal* (Sydney), 31 March 1888, printed a list of "the Irish men who fell". **The counter-view:** John Molony, in a Senate lecture of 23 April 2004, said "none of those leaders was Irish" and "No Irish digger was directly involved with the League in its early days". Eurekapedia (Dorothy Wickham) notes that a full statistical analysis of the participants' origins "has still to be undertaken". **The "Vinegar Hill" password** linking Eureka to Castle Hill is supported only by The Hills Shire ("the code used in the rebellion resurfaced during the Eureka Stockade") and a forum post cited by Eurekapedia. I could not confirm it in Carboni's text: the Gutenberg page truncated before the relevant chapters. Treat it as SECONDARY.
- SOURCES:

  - John Molony, "Eureka and the Prerogative of the People", Senate Occasional Lecture, 23 April 2004: https://www.aph.gov.au/binaries/senate/pubs/pops/pop42/molony.pdf
  - National Museum of Australia, *Defining Moments*: "Eureka Stockade": https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/eureka-stockade

### Q12-6 — Ned Kelly's Jerilderie Letter (1879): Irish grievance as memory politics
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): the Jerilderie Letter, 1879, with its Irish grievances, as memory politics.
- FINDING: The letter runs to about 8,000 words. Kelly dictated it and Joe Byrne wrote it out in 1879, at the time of the Jerilderie raid. The SLV page gives the year but no month; I have not verified the commonly cited February 1879. The original is held by the **State Library Victoria**, donated in 2000 by the family of Edwin Living, the Jerilderie bank accountant. It frames the police as Irishmen who betrayed Ireland, and recalls transportation and flogging under "the Saxon yoke". Verbatim extracts follow, from the NMA transcription, which may regularise punctuation against the original; check against the SLV page images before final copy.
- SOURCES:
  - National Museum of Australia, "Ned Kelly's Jerilderie Letter: transcription": https://nma.gov.au/explore/features/ned-kelly-jerilderie-letter/transcription (page numbers as given there).
  - State Library Victoria, Ergo, "The Jerilderie Letter": https://ergo.slv.vic.gov.au/explore-history/rebels-outlaws/bushrangers/jerilderie-letter (holder and provenance).
- STATUS: VERIFIED (quotes taken from the NMA transcription).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT (NMA transcription, original page numbers):
  - p.30: "big ugly, fat necked, wombat headed, big bellied, magpie legged, narrow hipped splaw-footed" … "sons of Irish bailiffs or English landlords"
  - p.31: "left the ash corner, deserted the Shamrock, the emblem of true wit and beauty to serve under a flag and nation" (extract from a longer sentence about an Irishman who joins the police)
  - p.31: "many a blooming Irishman rather than subdue to the Saxon yoke were flogged to death"
  - p.31: "More was transported to Van Dieman's Land to pine their young lives away in starvation and misery"
  - p.32: "What would England do if America declared war and hoisted a green flag"
  - p.32: "rise old Erin's isle once more from the pressure and tyrannism of the English yoke"
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The letter recasts a colonial bushranger as heir to Irish rebellion. Its targets are the Irish-born police ("sons of Irish bailiffs"), which complicates any "Irish versus English" binary. It says nothing about Aboriginal people or Chinese miners, so it is evidence of grievance memory, not of the racial bargain.
