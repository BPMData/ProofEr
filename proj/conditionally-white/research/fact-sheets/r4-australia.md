## Headlines

1. **The exodus was large, and the numbers come from historians.** Libraries Tasmania (the Tasmanian Archives' home)
   says of convicts' later departures: "Historians say between 50-70%, while many locals in C19th Tasmania claimed
   100%." McCalman's *Vandemonians* (MUP/Miegunyah 2021, built on the Founders and Survivors data) says "More than
   half of all those transported to Van Diemen's Land as convicts would one day settle or spend time in Victoria."
   Marian Quartly's review gives McCalman's count as "more than 40,000". In the gold rush itself, VDL's adult male
   population "dropped by about 33 percent (10,129)" between March 1851 and October 1852 (*Companion to Tasmanian
   History*, "Gold rush", Wendy Rimon). Those were all men, not only convicts.
2. **Victoria tried to shut the door; that much is firm.** The Convicts Prevention Act of 1852 was aimed at VDL's
   conditional-pardon holders. A contemporary account by William Westgarth (1864) says ships could be heavily fined,
   the people "imprisoned for three years", and that the law was later stretched to bar the convict class "for three
   years after they had received even full and free pardons". **The Act's own text, number and assent date were not
   read.** One lead says the Act may be 16 Vict. No. 13, assented 23 Sept 1852. No NSW Act of the 1850s was found;
   the NSW "Influx of Criminals Prevention" bill that turned up is from 1894.
3. **Who could legally leave VDL:** a ticket-of-leave holder could not ("They could not leave the colony", NLA). A
   conditional pardon from 1847 was "valid in all parts of the World except Great Britain and Ireland" (MHNSW, NSW
   practice). A certificate of freedom meant you "were free to travel anywhere" (NLA). So the legal leavers were
   mostly the freed by servitude and the conditionally pardoned, and the conditionally pardoned were the people
   Victoria's law targeted.
4. **F54 is mostly resolved.** NAI's "some 30,000 men and 9,000 women" covers "the 62 years of transportation from
   Ireland" (1791–1853). It counts people **shipped from Ireland**, and an independent sum fits: about 30,231 Irish
   transportees reached Sydney by 1849 (McIntyre 2018, via Mayberry) plus 11,316 from Ireland to VDL 1840–53
   (Davis, F53). The figure of 26,500 is still unexplained and should be dropped. Irish-born convicts tried in
   Britain are extra to the 39,000 and were not counted this round.
5. **F63:** O'Malley's place among the Ayes is now backed by a second reading of the PDF: Watson, the mover, and the
   Ayes tellers Batchelor and Hughes are in his block, and Barton and Deakin are in the other block of 34 names plus
   2 tellers, which matches "Noes, 36". It still rests on text extraction, not a page image, so it stays PROBABLE.
   **There is now a scholarly anchor:** Malcolm & Hall, *A New History of the Irish in Australia* (Cork UP, 2018/19),
   ch. 3 "The Irish and the Chinese in white Australia", pp. 73–103. A review by Laurence Marley (*Irish Times*, 2 Feb
   2019) says "Irish-born politicians often took a lead from their working-class constituents … in supporting
   legislation that restricted the intake of Chinese and other immigrants of colour."
