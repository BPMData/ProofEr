## Headline findings (changes / contradictions to the brief)
1. **Hobsbawm's "Traditions which appear or claim to be old…" is NOT the opening line.** It is the THIRD sentence of the Introduction's first paragraph (p. 1). The opening line is "Nothing appears more ancient, and linked to an immemorial past, than the pageantry which surrounds British monarchy…". Cite it as "p. 1", not as "the opening line".
2. **Edward Blewitt did not emigrate "in 1851" by Biden's own Ballina telling:** at Ballina, Biden said son *Patrick* returned "in 1850, as the famine hit Mayo particularly hard… to gather the entire Blewitt clan", and they sailed on the *Excelsior*; no year is given for the voyage. In the Dáil (13 Apr) Biden said Blewitt "lived with his family before relocating in 1851". Irish Times (McGreevy, 8 Apr 2023) dates the crossing to June 1851 on the SS *Excelsior*. So: "1850–51" or "1851" (Irish Times) is safe; "1851" as Biden's Ballina line is not.
3. **Bricks:** the 1828 brick payment is real in Biden's text ("21 pounds and 12 shillings… 27,000 of them"), but the Irish Times prints "£20.12". Small discrepancy — give the 27,000 figure, avoid the sum or note it. There is **no line about Ballina bricks at Scranton**; Biden said he held one of the 1828 bricks "in my hand today".
4. **Blewitt and famine relief: Irish sources disagree on his exact post.** Ciarán Reilly (Maynooth) says he oversaw the public relief works in the Ballina area (1848–50) and kept "thousands" alive; Irish Family History Centre (Fitzsimons & Moss) and Irish Times say he was an overseer at the Ballina Union **workhouse** from Feb 1848. **"Sold land to the Ballina workhouse": UNVERIFIED — none of the four sources I read says it. Drop it.**
5. **Megan Smolenyak is not the source for the famine-relief/workhouse detail** — the genealogists named in Irish sources are Fiona Fitzsimons & Helen Moss (Eneclann/Irish Family History Centre, 2016) and the historian is Ciarán Reilly. (Smolenyak appears in a 2016 Irish Times headline on the Louth/Mayo links — search listing only, not read.)
6. **Fintan O'Toole's Irish Times headlines differ from their URL slugs.** The 11 Apr 2023 piece is now headlined "JFK embodied an idea of what Ireland aspired to be, Biden embodies an idea of what we used to be"; the 15 Apr 2023 piece is headlined "Abortion is one of the unstated issues behind Joe Biden's trip to Ireland". Cite by the headline on the page + date, not the slug. And O'Toole explicitly says Biden's Irishness is **not** phoney — don't present him as calling it fake.
7. The most-quoted "last great avatar of Irish-American culture" line is **Hugh Linehan**, not O'Toole (Irish Times, 8 Apr 2023).
8. NYC parade: the organisers say **1762** (with Irish soldiers in the British Army); the 1762-vs-1766 question was not resolved against Cronin & Adair (not read). Clan na Gael sums (item 3): **UNVERIFIED** — budget ran out before any source was read.

### Q14-1 — Hobsbawm & Ranger, *The Invention of Tradition* (1983)
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): Thesis + Trevor-Roper chapter as Highland/kilt analogue + exact quotable passage, e.g. the "opening line" "'Traditions' which appear or claim to be old are often quite recent in origin and sometimes invented."
- FINDING: Sentence confirmed verbatim, but it is the third sentence of the first paragraph of Hobsbawm's "Introduction: Inventing Traditions" (chapter pp. 1–14; the sentence is on p. 1). It follows: "Nothing appears more ancient, and linked to an immemorial past, than the pageantry which surrounds British monarchy [in its public ceremonial manifestations]." and "Yet, as a chapter in this book establishes, in its modern form it is the product of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries." (The bracketed end of sentence 1 was cut off in the fetch; the tool truncated at 125 characters. Only the part before the bracket is checked.) Also on the chapter's first page: "The term 'invented tradition' is used in a broad, but not imprecise sense." The often-quoted definition sentence ("'Invented tradition' is taken to mean a set of practices…") was NOT visible in the extract I read; I could not check its wording or page.
  Chapter 2: Hugh Trevor-Roper, "The Invention of Tradition: The Highland Tradition of Scotland", pp. 15–42 (Cambridge Core listing). Cambridge's chapter summary: the emblems of Scottish identity (kilt, clan tartans), "to which they ascribe great antiquity, is in fact largely modern"; developed after the Union, partly in protest against it; "the whole concept of a distinct Highland culture and tradition is a retrospective invention"; and before the late 17th century the Highland west "Racially and culturally… was a colony of Ireland." The well-known specifics of the chapter (the "small kilt" credited to the English Quaker industrialist Thomas Rawlinson in the 1720s–30s; clan-specific tartans popularised later, incl. the Sobieski Stuarts' *Vestiarium Scoticum*, 1842) were NOT in the summary I read — UNVERIFIED this session; check the chapter text before using them.
  Book chapters (Wikipedia footnote, lead only; matches Cambridge Core TOC): Hobsbawm (Intro); Trevor-Roper (Highland); Prys Morgan (Welsh past); David Cannadine (British monarchy c.1820–1977); Bernard S. Cohn (Victorian India); Terence Ranger (colonial Africa); Hobsbawm, "Mass-Producing Traditions: Europe, 1870–1914". Criticism noted (lead only): Peter Burke, review, *English Historical Review* 101 (1986) — the phrase "hides serious ambiguities"; Richard Handler, review, *American Anthropologist* 86 (1984) — the genuine/spurious distinction "may be untenable"; Guy Beiner, *Remembering the Year of the French* (2007) prefers "reinvention of tradition".

### Q14-2 — St Patrick's Day parade, NYC: origins and 19th-century transformation
- CLAIM: NYC parade origins (1762? 1766? Irish soldiers in the British army) and 19th-century transformation into an Irish-American nationalist/ethnic display (Cronin & Adair 2002).
- FINDING: The parade's organisers state the first parade was 17 March 1762, made up of Irish expatriates and "Irish military members serving with the British Army stationed in the colonies in New York"; after the War of 1812, Irish fraternal and benevolent societies took over; around 1851 the 69th Regiment began leading the march, and the Ancient Order of Hibernians became official sponsor, with the societies merged under one Grand Marshal. I could not read Cronin & Adair, *The Wearing of the Green: A History of St Patrick's Day* (Routledge, 2002) — budget ran out — so the 1762-vs-1766 distinction (often given as a 1762 private gathering vs a 1766 public, military fife-and-drum procession) is UNVERIFIED. Supporting colour (O'Toole, Irish Times, 28 Mar 2006): the 69th Regiment "annually leads off the St Patrick's Day parade in New York".
- SOURCES:
  - NYC St. Patrick's Day Parade (organisers), "History": https://www.nycstpatricksparade.org/about/history/ — an interested, non-scholarly source
  - Fintan O'Toole, "The real 'Fighting Irish' story", *Irish Times*, 28 Mar 2006: https://www.irishtimes.com/opinion/the-real-fighting-irish-story-1.1289584
  - Mike Cronin & Daryl Adair, *The Wearing of the Green: A History of St Patrick's Day* (London: Routledge, 2002) — NOT READ this session.
- STATUS: PROBABLE (1762 + British Army soldiers, organiser source only); UNVERIFIED (1766 variant, Cronin & Adair page refs, "nationalist display" framing).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none from a scholarly source. Organiser wording: "The first parade was on March 17, 1762 — fourteen years before the signing of the United States Declaration of Independence." (nycstpatricksparade.org, History page)
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Safe line: "New York's parade began in the 1760s, marched by Irish soldiers of the British Army; by the mid-19th century it was run by Irish-American societies and led by the 69th Regiment." Get a Cronin & Adair page before using "1762" without "the organisers say".
