# Q6 — The exports data (Kinealy) — fact sheet

Research worker, batch Q6. Worked 2026-11-24. **Access limits this session:** Kinealy's *History Ireland* article is paywalled (teaser only read); her books (*This Great Calamity*, *A Death-Dealing Famine*, *The Great Irish Famine*, 2002) are on Internet Archive as lending items only, and the archive's search-inside, Open Library search and the Google Books API all refused. Ó Gráda's *Black '47 and Beyond* and Solar's 1989 essay were not readable online. The shared web-search budget ran out mid-task and the fetch budget was throttled. So **no figure below was read in Kinealy's own text**. Every Kinealy number reached me through a chain of secondary pages, and each chain is written out. The next step is a library check: physical or e-book copies of Kinealy 1997 (HI) and 2002, plus Ó Gráda 1999, ch. 4.

## Headline findings (what changes or contradicts the brief)

1. **Grain did NOT keep flowing out at famine-peak scale.** On the grain series Wikipedia prints (in thousands of quarters), exports fell from 3,252 (1845) to 970 (1847), while imports rose from 147 to 4,519. From 1847 to 1851, Ireland was a **net importer** of grain every year. Kinealy herself accepts this: an Irish America profile (2012), summarizing her work, says "by 1847 more food was coming into Ireland than going out." This breaks the brief's Ch.4 dual-chart premise of food leaving "sustained or rising" 1845–50 **for grain**. Only livestock and dairy figures may support "sustained or rising" exports, and no annual livestock series was verified.
2. **"Over three million live animals exported 1846–50" is not traceable to Kinealy here.** Every copy I found gives it with no source. The fullest version, which also carries the 9,992 calves and 4,000 vessels figures, is reproduced from **Christopher Fogarty, *Ireland 1845–1850: The Perfect Holocaust, and Who Kept it "Perfect"***. That is a polemical, non-scholarly book. The "more than total emigration" comparison never appears with a source. → UNVERIFIED. Don't put it on the ledger slip as "Kinealy".
3. **The butter figure is split by port:** 56,557 firkins to Bristol **and 34,852 firkins to Liverpool**, first nine months of 1847 (secondary chain). The brief's "to Bristol" covers only part of it. One derivative page converts firkins to gallons ("firkin = nine gallons"; "822,681 gallons"; "one million gallons of butter"). That conversion is a liquid-measure error: a butter firkin is a cask sold by weight. Never chart butter in gallons.
4. **"~4,000 vessels" carries no "military escort" in any version found.** The wording is "almost 4,000 vessels carried food from Ireland to the ports of Bristol, Glasgow, Liverpool and London" (1847). The escort idea is a separate claim, and I found no primary evidence for it this session.
5. **6,624 barrels of oats from Clare: not found in any source read.** UNVERIFIED (port, county and scope all unknown).
6. **No official annual excess-mortality series exists.** What exists: (a) reported deaths by year from the 1851 Census *Tables of Deaths*, which are known to be under-recorded; (b) totals for the whole famine (Cousens ~800k excess deaths 1846–50; Mokyr ~1.1–1.5m; consensus "about one million"); (c) Mokyr's *annualized average* rates per 1,000 for 1846–51.
7. The brief's "History Ireland vol. 5 no. 1, Spring 1997" citation is **not confirmed**. The HI page shows no issue metadata to a fetch. One derivative page says "spring 1998 issue". Confirm before printing.

### Q6-1a — Butter: 56,557 firkins to Bristol, first nine months of 1847
- CLAIM: "56,557 firkins of butter to Bristol in nine months of 1847" (brief §Ch4, ledger, figures table).
- FINDING: A derivative page attributes it to Dr Christine Kinealy and adds a Liverpool figure. Its wording: "56,557 firkins were exported" to Bristol and "34,852 firkins" to Liverpool in the first nine months of 1847. It then sums both into "822,681 gallons" using its own conversion (firkin = 9 gallons). That conversion is wrong for butter, and the page's author made it, not Kinealy. The page cites Kinealy's *This Great Calamity*, *A Death-Dealing Famine* and "the spring 1998 issue of History Ireland" without giving a page. The underlying primary source is presumably port or customs returns (Kinealy worked from shipping returns and port records), but I couldn't identify which.
- SOURCES:
  - Kinealy, "Food Exports from Ireland 1846–47", *History Ireland*. https://www.historyireland.com/18th-19th-century-history/food-exports-from-ireland-1846-47 . Teaser only (paywall). Issue and volume not shown on the page.
  - "Food exports from Ireland during the famine years (1845–1849)", usbornefamilytree.com (unsigned; says it is drawn from a Nebraska Dept. of Education Irish-famine curriculum page). https://usbornefamilytree.com/irishfoodexports.htm . **SECONDARY/tertiary, lead only.**
- STATUS: SECONDARY (one tertiary chain; Kinealy's original not read).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none from Kinealy. Derivative wording only: "56,557 firkins were exported" (usbornefamilytree.com).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Use only after the figure is checked in Kinealy's own text (HI article or her 2002 book). Say "to Bristol" (or "to Bristol and Liverpool"), "Jan–Sept 1847", in firkins, and never in gallons. If shown with a weight, state the firkin weight from a source.

### Q6-3 — Over 3 million live animals exported 1846–50, "more than total emigration"
- CLAIM: "3,000,000+ (> total emigration)".
- FINDING: On the Mayo page (reproducing Fogarty): "In total, over three million live animals were exported from Ireland, between 1846 and 1850." The usborne page also gives it with **no source**, unlike its Kinealy items. Neither page says which species (cattle, sheep, pigs, horses) or what the base series is. The "exceeds emigration" comparison appears in no source I read. Verifiable livestock pieces, all via the secondary chain attributed to Ó Gráda (*Ireland Before and After the Famine*): 257,257 sheep exported to Britain in 1845; 480,827 swine and 186,483 oxen in 1846. Also via the chain: 4,000 horses and ponies in the twelve months after the second potato failure (attributed to Kinealy).
- SOURCES: mayo-ireland.ie (Fogarty); usbornefamilytree.com (unsourced for this line; Ó Gráda attributions for the 1845–46 livestock).
- STATUS: UNVERIFIED (3 million total and the emigration comparison); SECONDARY (1845–46 livestock counts).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none usable.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Don't use "3,000,000 live animals (Kinealy)" on the ledger slip. If the 1846 pigs plus oxen (~667k in one year) hold up, a multi-year total in the millions is plausible, but a plausible total isn't a sourced one. The real annual series exist in the parliamentary returns of imports into Great Britain from Ireland; they should be pulled before any livestock line is charted.

### Q6-4 — Food convoys moved under military or police escort through starving districts
- CLAIM: "food shipped out under military escort (same years, same counties, same ports)"; "exported under guard".
- FINDING: **No primary evidence was reached this session.** What I did read:
  - Wikipedia quotes Fr Nicholas McEvoy, PP of Kells (Oct 1845): "vessels laden with our sole hopes of existence, our provisions, are hourly wafted from our every port" and "fifty dray loads of meal moving on to Drogheda, thence to go to feed the foreigner". These are exports with no escort, and from 1845, before the mass deaths.
  - usbornefamilytree.com, unsourced: "British regiments guarded the ports and warehouses in Ireland" (credited to Kinealy's research, no citation).
  - O'Rourke, *History of the Great Irish Famine of 1847* (1875; 3rd ed. 1902, Gutenberg #14412), ch. I: troops ("eighteen foot soldiers and a troop of horse") break up a crowd stopping a boat of oats at Carrick-on-Suir in **1740–41**. That is an earlier famine and a precedent only. I read only the first ~100k characters; the 1846–47 chapters were not reached.
- Leads not verified this session: Woodham-Smith, *The Great Hunger*, on the autumn 1846 food riots in Co. Waterford and Co. Cork (Dungarvan, Youghal), where troops were deployed; the Commissariat correspondence (*Correspondence … relating to the measures adopted for the relief of distress in Ireland*, Commissariat Series, Parliamentary Papers 1847); escorts of relief and export cargoes on the Shannon. Check page numbers in the books before quoting.
- SOURCES: Wikipedia, "Great Famine (Ireland)" (McEvoy quotes, footnote 116 there); usbornefamilytree.com; Gutenberg #14412.
- STATUS: UNVERIFIED (as the brief frames it).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: McEvoy (Oct 1845), via Wikipedia: "vessels laden with our sole hopes of existence, our provisions, are hourly wafted from our every port". SECONDARY; check against the original letter (published in the press, 1845) before use.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Troops were used to protect property and suppress food riots. That is a defensible line once Woodham-Smith's page is in hand. "Convoys through starving districts" is stronger, needs its own primary document, and shouldn't go in on current evidence.

### Q6-5 — Scholarly dispute: Ó Gráda and Solar vs. the exports argument
- CLAIM (prompt): Ó Gráda argues net grain imports exceeded exports from 1847; Solar on imports vs exports 1845–50.
- FINDING:
  - **Grain table (Wikipedia, "Irish grain trade in units of 1,000 quarters", 1842–1851):** no source line on the table. Its 1845 export figure (3,252k qrs) matches "3,251,907 quarters" credited to Ó Gráda in the derivative chain, which suggests Ó Gráda's compilation of Bourke-type official returns. The table shows net imports in every year from 1847 to 1851.
  - Wikipedia text: besides maize, wheat imports at the height of the famine were four times wheat exports. Cited to Woodham-Smith (*The Great Hunger*, p.76; one in-text cite reads "Woodham-Smith 1991, p.175") and to Ó Gráda 2000, p.123 (likely *Black '47 and Beyond*, paperback). Page numbers not checked.
  - Kinealy concedes the net position (Irish America profile, 2012): exports "had indeed continued throughout the Famine from ports all over Ireland to Britain", but "by 1847 more food was coming into Ireland than going out". Her argument therefore rests on 1846 timing, the regional pattern and policy choices, not on a net-export total.
  - **Solar**, as quoted by L.A. Clarkson's review (Reviews in History no. 43, 1 July 1997): "the loss of the potato crop in the seasons 1845-49 was so great that there was an absolute shortage of food in the country", and "the major change affecting the entitlements of the Irish people in the late 1840s was a direct fall in income". These are excerpts quoted by Clarkson; Solar's 1989 essay itself was not read.
  - Clarkson (same review): "The grain that was exported during the Famine was legally owned by producers or merchants." Also: "Every ear of corn and bag of flour that might have been stopped from leaving Ireland in the late 1840s was one ear and bag that might have saved a life."
  - The HI teaser frames the debate: "The issue of food exports during the Famine has fuelled an on-going debate in the historiography of the crisis." The traditional view is that exports added to excess mortality, read as doctrine winning out over humanitarian concern; the opposing view's argument is cut off by the paywall.
  - Primary-series anchor: P.M. Austin Bourke, "The Irish grain trade, 1839–48", *Irish Historical Studies* 20:78 (Sept 1976), 156–169, doi:10.1017/S0021121400024093. It holds the most complete official annual series (1839–48) and monthly exports for 1846; per its notes, exports to foreign countries were negligible, so exports to Great Britain = total exports. Abstract page only.
- STATUS: CONTESTED (interpretation); VERIFIED that the national grain balance turned to net imports in 1847, on two concurring secondary sources (the Wikipedia table plus Kinealy's own concession). Ó Gráda's own figures not read.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: Clarkson, *Reviews in History* no. 43 (1 July 1997), https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/43/print: "The grain that was exported during the Famine was legally owned by producers or merchants."
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The honest framing is that grain exports in 1846 (1.8m quarters leaving as the potato failed) and continuing meat and dairy exports were policy choices. From 1847, imports (mostly maize) far exceeded grain exports; the problem was entitlement (people couldn't pay), not a net outflow. The brief's planned footnote ("exports were partly estate-owners' property; the argument is about policy") is on the right track, but the chart itself must not imply a net outflow of grain after 1846.

**Series notes**
- **(a)(b)**, grain and meal in thousands of quarters (1 quarter = 8 bushels; a quarter is a volume, so wheat, oats and maize weigh differently): Wikipedia, "Great Famine (Ireland)", table "Irish grain trade in units of 1,000 quarters". No source line on the page. The 1845 figure is corroborated by "3,251,907 quarters" (credited to Ó Gráda via usbornefamilytree.com). Status: SECONDARY, two concurring copies. Before publishing, replace with the original table: Bourke 1976 (IHS 20:78, Tables 2–3) for 1839–48, and Ó Gráda's compilation (likely *Black '47 and Beyond* p.123 or *Ireland: A New Economic History*) for 1849–51.
- **(c)** Livestock: no annual series verified. † = single secondary chain (usbornefamilytree.com crediting Ó Gráda for 1845–46 and Kinealy for calves); species and scope differ by year, so **don't chart these as a line**. The 3 million total for 1846–50 is UNVERIFIED (see Q6-3).
- **(d)** Emigration: not verified this session; web budget exhausted before I reached it. Standard sources to pull: Commission on Emigration and Other Population Problems 1948–54, *Reports*, statistical appendix (overseas emigration from Irish ports, 1851 on, with earlier estimates); the Emigration Commissioners' annual reports (emigration from UK ports by destination); David Fitzpatrick, *Irish Emigration 1801–1921* (1984). Note that official counting of Irish-port emigration started 1 May 1851, so 1846–50 figures are **estimates** built from the Emigration Commissioners' and port returns.
- **(e)** ‡ = "Reported Deaths in Ireland", New York State Great Irish Famine Curriculum, unit 13.9 (https://alansinger.net/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/13.9-demography-of-gif.pdf). The page gives no source; the figures match the shape of the 1851 Census *Tables of Deaths* (Census of Ireland 1851, Part V, vols I–II; listed at DIPPAM, https://www.dippam.ac.uk/eppi/documents/13665, not opened). These are **recorded** deaths, known to be under-recorded (whole families died or emigrated and no one was left to report them; no civil registration until 1864). They are **total** deaths, not excess deaths. 1851 likely covers only the months up to the census night (30–31 March 1851); confirm.
- **Excess mortality:** there is **no annual series**. What exists:
  - Cousens (1960s): just over 800,000 excess deaths 1846–50, totals only (RTÉ / O'Keeffe 2020).
  - Mokyr (*Why Ireland Starved*, 1985, pp. 266–7): about 1.1–1.5m (RTÉ: Mokyr put Cousens about 200,000 too low). Annualized excess-death rates per 1,000 for 1846–51: Ireland 24.4 (lower bound) / 33.1 (upper bound, "demographic deficit"); Connacht 50.9 / 61.7; Munster 24.9 / 36.3; Leinster 8.9 / 14.2; Ulster 18.9 / 25.6 (Ó Gráda, UCD WP17/13, Table 1, citing Mokyr 1985). These are *averages over the period*, not year-by-year.
  - Consensus: "at least one million"; about 1.4m counting averted births (O'Keeffe, RTÉ 2020).
  - Wilde, 1851 Census: 1,361,051 deaths recorded between the two censuses (1841–51), about 73% of them in the five famine years (RTÉ 2020).
- **Chart recommendation:** The honest dual chart is **grain exports vs. grain imports** (two bars per year from zero, or two lines), with deaths as a separate panel. It shows exports collapsing after 1846 and imports surging in 1847, with mortality peaking in 1847 and staying high to 1849 while the country was a net grain importer. That is the entitlement story, and it is documented. A "people leaving vs. food leaving" chart needs a verified emigration series and a verified livestock or dairy series; neither is in hand.
