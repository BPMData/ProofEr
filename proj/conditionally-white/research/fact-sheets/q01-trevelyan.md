## Headline findings

1. **The best-known "Trevelyan" line is partly a historian's paraphrase.** "The judgement of God sent the calamity to
   teach the Irish a lesson, that calamity must not be too much mitigated" is, per a published critique of Tim Pat
   Coogan's *The Famine Plot* (pp. 63-4), a composite: a sentence of **Jenifer Hart's** ("Sir Charles Trevelyan at the
   Treasury", *English Historical Review* 75, 1960, p. 99: "Trevelyan believed the Irish famine was the judgment of God
   on an indolent and unselfreliant people...") spliced to a Trevelyan letter quoted by Woodham-Smith (*The Great
   Hunger*, p. 156). **Do not put the "judgement of God ... too much mitigated" sentence on the quotes wall as
   Trevelyan's words** unless Hart p. 99 and Woodham-Smith p. 156 are checked and show otherwise. (Chain is SECONDARY; see B2.)
2. **"Direct stroke of an all-wise Providence" exists in two forms, in two documents.** (a) A private letter to Lord
   Monteagle, October 1846: "...the cure has been applied by the direct stroke of an all-wise Providence in a manner as
   unexpected and as unthought of as it is likely to be effectual" (traced via Haines 2004 p. 6 n. 55; Coogan pp. 97-8;
   also quoted by Hart and Woodham-Smith). (b) The longer "all-wise **and all-merciful** Providence" / "deep and
   inveterate root of social evil" form is attributed to *The Irish Crisis* (1848). I could **not** read the relevant
   pages of *The Irish Crisis* this session (all online texts truncated before about p. 130 of 201), so form (b) and its
   page are UNVERIFIED. The brief's framing ("from The Irish Crisis, the best-attested line") needs adjusting: the
   letter form is the better-traced one.
3. **What *The Irish Crisis* (1848) verifiably says on p. 1:** "...Supreme Wisdom has educed permanent good out of
   transient evil." This is VERIFIED in two independent transcriptions and is the safest providentialist line for the wall.
4. **B2 ("moral evil... selfish, perverse and turbulent"): date, recipient and opening word are all unsettled.** Popular
   copies say both "The **real** evil" and "The **great** evil"; dates offered are 9 Oct 1846 (Monteagle), "December
   2nd" (1846) and "1848". Its scholarly trail runs through Woodham-Smith p. 156. Not seen in a primary document this
   session: SECONDARY at best.
5. **B8 may be misdated.** The Commissariat Series volume for July 1846 to January 1847 (BPP 1847 vol. LI) lists **no**
   Trevelyan-to-Routh letters for July 1846 in its contents, and its relief-depot letters run Dec 1846 to Jan 1847. A
   "habitually dependent ... bring ... to a close" letter fits the closing of Peel's first relief scheme in **summer
   1846** better than July 1847. That paper series is printed in an earlier 1846 volume I could not reach. UNVERIFIED.
6. **B17 ("Cork prices") has a probable home:** Commissariat Series (BPP 1847 vol. LI), contents entry "Mr. Trevelyan
   to Sir R. Routh ... Respecting prices to be charged at the Government depôts," **2 Jan. 1847, p. 443**. It is also
   near a 6 Jan. 1847 Trevelyan-to-Hewetson letter "showing that high prices are a necessary consequence of the scarcity
   of food" (p. 440). The wording has not been read, and the OCR page mapping is shaky.
7. **B15 (no orders abroad): the policy is confirmed in Trevelyan's own published words.** *The Irish Crisis* says "no
   orders for supplies of food would be sent by the Government to foreign countries." The "unheard of" letter wording
   itself is UNVERIFIED.
8. **B20 ("too much has been done ... worse instead of better"): nothing found that settles Trevelyan vs Charles
   Wood.** UNVERIFIED. A verified Trevelyan substitute with the same idea: *The Irish Crisis* p. 129, "...to promote
   their moral advancement, by awakening and encouraging a spirit of independent exertion."

### B2 — "The real evil ... is not the physical evil of the Famine but the moral evil of the selfish, perverse and turbulent character of the people"
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): Trevelyan, December 1846, perhaps a letter to Lord Monteagle of 9 Oct 1846.
- FINDING: I could not reach a primary document with this sentence. Its scholarly chain runs through Cecil
  Woodham-Smith, *The Great Hunger* (p. 156 in the edition the reviewer used), which quotes a Trevelyan letter. A
  critique of Coogan (*The Famine Plot*, 2012, pp. 63-4) says Coogan's version is a **composite**: Hart's 1960
  paraphrase ("judgment of God ...") stuck onto a Trevelyan letter the reviewer dates "December 2nd", taken from
  Woodham-Smith p. 156. Separately, Wikipedia cites the **9 Oct 1846 letter to Monteagle** for nearby Trevelyan phrases
  ("defective part of the national character"; "as if they have themselves no part to perform in this great crisis";
  "the government establishments are strained to the utmost to alleviate this great calamity and avert this danger").
  So the Monteagle letter is real and in the same vein. Whether the "moral evil" sentence is in it, or in a December
  letter, is **unsettled**. The opening word varies in circulation: "real evil" (Galway Advertiser 2015; Madras
  Courier) versus "great evil" (Pellegrino teaching page, which gives both versions in different places and dates one
  to 1848).
- SOURCES:
  - Woodham-Smith, Cecil. *The Great Hunger: Ireland 1845–1849*. London: Hamish Hamilton, 1962 (reviewer cites a 1992 Penguin printing), p. 156. NOT READ this session; page per the review below.
  - Hart, Jenifer. "Sir Charles Trevelyan at the Treasury." *English Historical Review* 75 (1960), p. 99. NOT READ; quoted in the review below.
  - "The neo-Mitchelites: One book of no use", *Die drei Raben* (Substack, pseudonymous "Denying-History"), 25 Nov 2022, https://diedreiraben.substack.com/p/the-neo-mitchelites-one-book-of-no (LEAD ONLY: a blog, not citable for the quote).
  - Wikipedia, "Sir Charles Trevelyan, 1st Baronet", read via https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Sir_Charles_Trevelyan,_1st_Baronet, its note [16]: the Monteagle letter of 9 Oct 1846 (LEAD ONLY).
  - O'Gorman, Ronnie. "A 'selfish, perverse and turbulent' people", *Galway Advertiser*, 26 Mar 2015, https://www.advertiser.ie/galway/article/76676/a-selfish-perverse-and-turbulent-people. Gives no source (LEAD ONLY).
- STATUS: SECONDARY (wording via Woodham-Smith, not read; date and recipient UNVERIFIED)
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none yet. If it is used, quote it as "quoted in Woodham-Smith, *The Great Hunger*, p. 156" after checking that page, and give no date unless Woodham-Smith's note gives one.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Real Trevelyan sentiment, but don't print "December 1846, to Monteagle" until a copy of
  Woodham-Smith p. 156 and its endnote (which should cite the Trevelyan Letter Books, Bodleian / T64 at TNA) is read.
  Never pair it with "judgement of God ... too much mitigated", which is Hart's paraphrase.

### B8 — Trevelyan to Routh: "The only way to prevent the people from becoming habitually dependent on Government is to bring the food depots to a close..."
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): July 1847, Commissariat Series correspondence.
- FINDING: I located the printed Commissariat Series volumes on Internet Archive (Southampton/EPPI scans). The volume for
  **July 1846 to January 1847** (BPP 1847, vol. LI) shows **no Trevelyan-to-Routh letters dated July 1846** in its
  table of contents. Its depot letters cluster in Dec 1846 to Jan 1847 (for example "Depots of food; grinding"). The
  date "July 1847" is doubtful: by summer 1847 the soup-kitchen Act, not Commissariat depots, was the main relief. From
  background knowledge, **not checked this session**: this sentence is usually quoted (from Woodham-Smith) as Trevelyan
  to Routh, **July 1846**, about winding up Peel's first relief operations, with "operations" rather than "food depots".
  That would place it in the 1846 paper "Correspondence explanatory of the measures adopted by Her Majesty's Government
  for the relief of distress arising from the failure of the potato crop in Ireland" (Commissariat Series to Aug 1846),
  which I could not reach.
- SOURCES:
  - *Correspondence relating to the measures adopted for the relief of distress in Ireland (Commissariat Series), July 1846 – January 1847*. Parliamentary Papers 1847, vol. LI. London: W. Clowes for HMSO. Internet Archive op1246627-1001, https://archive.org/details/op1246627-1001; text https://archive.org/stream/op1246627-1001/op1246627-1001_djvu.txt (contents read; the text view cut off after Routh's letter of 11 Aug 1846).
  - Index to the same, first and second parts: BPP 1847 vol. LI–LII p. 535, https://archive.org/details/op1246628-1001 (metadata only).
  - Commissariat Series, January–March 1847: BPP 1847 vol. LII p. 333, https://archive.org/details/op1246629-1001 (metadata only).
- STATUS: UNVERIFIED (wording and date); the brief's "July 1847" is likely wrong
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Hold it back until it is read in the 1846 Commissariat paper (Southampton EPPI or HathiTrust). If
  it can't be checked, describe the policy instead: relief was wound down to stop "dependence". The verified *Irish
  Crisis* p. 129 line (B20) covers that idea in Trevelyan's own words.

### B15 — "We attach the highest public importance to the strict observance of our pledge not to send orders abroad... For a numerous people like the Irish to be fed from foreign countries is a thing unheard of."

### Providence — The Irish Crisis "direct stroke of an all-wise and all-merciful Providence" passage
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): in *The Irish Crisis* (1848), the best-attested Trevelyan line.
- FINDING: Three separate things, often blurred together:
  1. **VERIFIED, *The Irish Crisis* p. 1:** "...on this, as on many other occasions, Supreme Wisdom has educed permanent
     good out of transient evil." Found in both the Gutenberg and the CELT transcriptions of the 1848 first edition.
  2. **SECONDARY, letter to Lord Monteagle, October 1846** (Wikipedia gives 9 Oct 1846 for that letter): "...being
     altogether beyond the power of man, the cure has been applied by the direct stroke of an all-wise Providence in a
     manner as unexpected and as unthought of as it is likely to be effectual." The chain: Haines 2004, p. 6, n. 55;
     Coogan 2012, pp. 97-8; also cited by Hart 1960 and Woodham-Smith. I assembled the wording from fragments in two
     secondary web pages, so check it against Haines p. 6 before printing.
  3. **UNVERIFIED, the "all-wise and all-merciful Providence" / "deep and inveterate root of social evil" / "sharp but
     effectual remedy" form**, which Wikipedia attributes to *The Irish Crisis* without a page. Every online text I could
     open (Gutenberg, CELT, the Internet Archive OCR) was cut off by the fetch tool before the end of the book (CELT got
     to about p. 130 of 201), and the phrase was not in the parts read. It is probably in the closing pages, but that is
     unconfirmed.
- SOURCES:
  - Trevelyan, C. E. *The Irish Crisis*. London: Longman, Brown, Green & Longmans, 1848; reprinted from *Edinburgh Review* no. CLXXV, Jan 1848. CELT e-text E840001-002 (ed. Beatrix Färber, 2015; first edition, 201 pp.), https://celt.ucc.ie/published/E840001-002.html, p. 1.
  - Same, Project Gutenberg #56727, https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/56727/pg56727-images.html (the p. 1 line confirmed independently).
  - Same, Internet Archive scan (Boston College copy), https://archive.org/details/irishcrisis00trev (the PDF scan is the place to check the closing pages).
  - Haines, Robin. *Charles Trevelyan and the Great Irish Famine*. Dublin: Four Courts Press, 2004, p. 6, n. 55 (the Monteagle letter). NOT READ; via the *Die drei Raben* review.
  - Pellegrino, Joe. "An Gorta Mór", course page, https://jpellegrino.com/teaching/irishhistory/02-angortamor.html. Dates the "direct stroke of an all-wise Providence" line to Oct 1846 (LEAD ONLY).
- STATUS: p. 1 line VERIFIED; Monteagle letter SECONDARY; "all-merciful" *Irish Crisis* form UNVERIFIED
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "Supreme Wisdom has educed permanent good out of transient evil." — C. E. Trevelyan, *The Irish Crisis* (London: Longman, Brown, Green & Longmans, 1848), p. 1.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The p. 1 line is the safe providentialist line, public and published under his name in 1848. The
  "direct stroke of an all-wise Providence" letter is real but private; quote it as "quoted in Haines (2004), p. 6"
  after checking. Note that Haines reads the letter as Trevelyan casting the famine as an opportunity for landlords to
  reform estates, not as rejoicing at deaths. One more line, for balance, that the review attributes to Trevelyan's
  letter to Father Mathew (Oct 1846, per Hart; Haines p. 5): "I fear less from the judgment of God than from the
  aggravation of man." It is SECONDARY and unchecked.

### H — How historians characterize Trevelyan (calibration)
- FINDING (what I could verify is thin; the "background" lines below are not checked this session):
  - **Woodham-Smith (1962):** the source of the modern "villain" picture. She quotes Trevelyan's private letter books at
    length, and many of the quotes circulating online (B2, B8, B17) come through her. Verified this session only
    indirectly: the review cites her p. 156 for the "moral evil" letter.
  - **Jenifer Hart (1960, EHR):** p. 99 summarizes Trevelyan's view as "the Irish famine was the judgment of God on an
    indolent and unselfreliant people". That is a historian's paraphrase that later writers turned into a "Trevelyan
    quote" (verified only as quoted in the review).
  - **Peter Gray (1999):** as quoted in the review, Gray says Trevelyan's "influence over policy was heavily dependent"
    on his political superiors (p. 231). Background, unchecked: Gray places Trevelyan among the Whig "moralists"
    (with Charles Wood and Earl Grey), whose providentialist, market-first ideology shaped policy. Responsibility is
    spread to the cabinet, but it is not removed.
  - **Robin Haines (2004):** a defence. She argues Trevelyan was an overworked official carrying out ministers' policy,
    and reads the Monteagle letter as being about landlord duties (p. 6; via the review).
  - **Cormac Ó Gráda (1999):** not reached this session. Background, unchecked: he sees Trevelyan's providentialism and
    doctrinaire non-interference as making relief less effective, and he rejects the genocide charge. Needs a page cite
    from *Black '47 and Beyond* (Princeton UP, 1999).
- SOURCES:
  - "The neo-Mitchelites: One book of no use", *Die drei Raben*, 25 Nov 2022 (LEAD ONLY; it gives the page references above to Hart p. 99, Woodham-Smith p. 156, Gray p. 231 and Haines pp. 5-6).
  - The four books themselves: NOT READ this session.
- STATUS: SECONDARY (Hart, Gray and Haines page pointers via a review); Ó Gráda UNVERIFIED
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none (check the pages before quoting any historian).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Calibration that holds up even on what was verified: Trevelyan's providentialist and
  anti-"dependence" views are **documentary** (*The Irish Crisis* p. 1, p. 129; the Treasury depot-pricing letters
  exist in the Parliamentary Papers). The cartoon villain who "said God sent the famine to teach the Irish a lesson" is
  partly **retrospective**: Hart's paraphrase, passed on through Coogan and the web. His power was real but delegated
  (Gray; Haines). Say both.
