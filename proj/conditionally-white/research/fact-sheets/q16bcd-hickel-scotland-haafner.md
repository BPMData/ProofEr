## Headline findings

1. **"100–165M excess deaths in India 1880–1920, against a baseline of 1880s death rates" mixes two different
   estimates.** In the peer-reviewed paper (World Development 161, 2023, p. 12) the **1880s-death-rate baseline gives
   about 50 million excess deaths, for 1891–1920**. The **165 million figure (1880–1920) uses a different baseline:
   England's 16th–17th-century average death rate** (27.18 per 1,000, per the authors' Al Jazeera piece). Neither
   baseline gives 100 million.
2. **"100 million" is not in the paper.** It comes from the authors' Al Jazeera opinion piece (2 Dec 2022), which calls
   it "approximately 100 million excess deaths during the 1881-1920 period". The authors' own range is **50–165 million
   (1881–1920)**. The scope is India under British rule, 1880/81–1920, not a longer period.
3. **No formal reply in World Development was found.** The main published critique is by Tirthankar Roy (LSE): first on
   Twitter, then as an essay on History Reclaimed (18 Jan 2023), a pro-empire advocacy site. Sullivan & Hickel replied
   on 7 Jan 2023. Roy, in their account, "does not dispute our estimate of the scale"; he disputes the cause
   (geography and monsoon in the Deccan, not policy). CONTESTED.
4. **Davis, Late Victorian Holocausts (Verso 2001), Table P1: India total 12.2–29.3 million** (1876–79: 6.1–10.3M;
   1896–1902: 6.1–19.0M). Grand total across India, China and Brazil: **31.7–61.3 million**. Davis's own prose says "could
   not have been less than 30 million … Fifty million dead might not be unrealistic". The "30–60 million" shorthand is a
   rounding of this.
5. **Haafner: the famine is 1782, not 1771.** The Dutch source is *Lotgevallen op eene reize van Madras over
   Tranquebaar naar het eiland Ceilon* (Haarlem, 1806), not the 1808 palanquin book or the 1820 work. The pack's
   "balconies … revelling with their whores" passage **was not found**. The DBNL text has the Company's grain warehouses
   ("pakhuizen"), the English feasting over the fall of Negapatnam, and the dying "in het stof". In that text the only
   "whores" line ("hoeren (garces)") is spoken by a French count in a later, unrelated episode. **Treat the pack's
   English wording as UNUSABLE** until a printed English translation is found.
6. Scotland (Q16c): nothing was verified this session (budget ran out). The "let her burn" line, the trial details and
   the Betsy Mackay attribution all need a re-run against MacLeod's 1857/1892 text and the Napier Commission evidence.

### Q16b-1 — Sullivan & Hickel: 100–165M excess deaths in India, 1880–1920
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): Sullivan & Hickel (World Development 2023) estimate 100–165 million excess
  deaths in India 1880–1920, with mortality measured against a baseline from 1880s death rates.
- FINDING: The paper gives two estimates on two baselines (p. 12, Section 3.4 South Asia, Table 3, Appendix V):
  (a) "If we estimate excess mortality from 1891 to 1920, with the average death rate of the 1880s as normal
  mortality, we find some 50 million people lost their lives under the aegis of British capitalism." The authors
  call this conservative, because India's 1880s death rate was already high. (b) "If we measure excess mortality over
  England's 16th- and 17th-century average death rate, we find 165 million excess deaths in India between 1880 and
  1920 (Appendix V)." Then: "This figure is larger than the combined number of deaths from both World Wars, including
  the Nazi holocaust." Data: Indian crude death rates are reconstructed from the censuses (Dyson 2018); the English
  benchmark is Wrigley & Schofield (1981). The 100M figure appears only in the authors' Al Jazeera op-ed, as a rounded
  figure between the two. The op-ed itself says the result is "sensitive to the assumptions we make about baseline
  mortality". The op-ed states the 165M baseline as 27.18 deaths per 1,000. The paper does not separate famine deaths
  from epidemic deaths (plague, 1918 influenza); see Q16b-2.
- SOURCES:
  - Dylan Sullivan & Jason Hickel, "Capitalism and extreme poverty: A global analysis of real wages, human height, and
    mortality since the long 16th century", *World Development* 161 (2023) 106026, doi:10.1016/j.worlddev.2022.106026,
    p. 12 and Table 3. Read via the UAB repository PDF: https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/artpub/2023/319e76b138b3/Capitalism_and_extreme_poverty.pdf
    (the quotes above were extracted in short fragments by a fetch tool; check punctuation against the PDF before
    printing).
  - Dylan Sullivan & Jason Hickel, "How British colonialism killed 100 million Indians in 40 years", Al Jazeera
    (opinion), 2 Dec 2022, https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/opinions/2022/12/2/how-british-colonial-policy-killed-100-million-indians
  - Sullivan & Hickel, "On the mortality crises in India under British rule: A response to Tirthankar Roy", jasonhickel.org,
    7 Jan 2023, gives the range as "50-165 million excess deaths during the mortality crisis of 1881-1920":
    https://www.jasonhickel.org/blog/2023/1/7/on-the-mortality-crises-in-india-under-british-rule-a-response-to-tirthankar-roy
- STATUS: VERIFIED as to what the authors claim; the estimate itself is CONTESTED (see Q16b-2).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "If we estimate excess mortality from 1891 to 1920, with the average death rate of the 1880s as
  normal mortality, we find some 50 million people lost their lives under the aegis of British capitalism." / "…we find
  165 million excess deaths in India between 1880 and 1920" (Sullivan & Hickel, *World Development* 161 (2023)
  106026, p. 12). Check the exact punctuation against the PDF.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Say "an estimated 50 to 165 million excess deaths in British India, 1881–1920, depending on
  what death rate is taken as 'normal' (Sullivan & Hickel 2023)". Never "100 million" as if it came from the paper,
  never "1880s baseline → 165M", and never "across the full colonial period".

### Q16b-2 — Responses and critiques (methodological dispute)
- CLAIM: Published responses and critiques exist (Roy; possibly Caplan, OWID/Roser, a formal World Development reply).
- FINDING: Tirthankar Roy (LSE economic historian) critiqued the claim on Twitter in early Dec 2022, then wrote
  "Colonialism did not cause the Indian famines", History Reclaimed, 18 Jan 2023. Roy's points: geography and monsoon
  failure in the Deccan caused the famines, and the Indo-Gangetic north was largely spared; pre-colonial famine data
  (travelogues, hagiographies) can't be compared with colonial statistics; the north-Indian wage series is weak; grain
  exports followed good harvests (citing Ravallion 1987); famines ended about 1900 through railways and market
  integration (McAlpin; Burgess & Donaldson), which colonial "apathy" can't explain. Sullivan & Hickel replied on 7 Jan
  2023: excess deaths were India-wide, not only in the Deccan; Dyson's data and the 1918 influenza show the north was
  not spared; Mughal famine relief was more effective; famine is political. They state that "Roy does not dispute our
  estimate of the scale of excess deaths during the 1881-1920 period". Tamoghna Halder, "Colonialism and the Indian
  famines: a response to Tirthankar Roy", Developing Economics blog, 20 Feb 2023, was seen in search results only and
  not read. **No formal comment or reply in *World Development* was found**, though the search was limited. No
  Caplan or Our World in Data/Roser response on the India figure was found either; the earlier Hickel–Roser debate
  (2019) was about the "90% in extreme poverty" claim, not India mortality (from memory, not re-checked here).
  - Summary of the dispute: the 50M and 165M figures are counterfactual. They are the deaths above an assumed "normal"
    death rate, and the result depends almost entirely on that assumption. The 1880s baseline is itself drawn from a
    famine-scarred decade (the 1876–78 famine), and the authors say so. England's 1500s–1600s rate is a borrowed
    benchmark with no Indian data behind it, and the authors say they "do not know for sure" what India's
    pre-colonial rate was. The excess also lumps together famine, plague (from 1896), malaria and the 1918 influenza
    pandemic. That makes it a measure of total excess mortality under British rule, not of deaths "caused by"
    policy. The causal step is what Roy disputes; the size of the excess, on the 1880s baseline, he does not.
- SOURCES:
  - Tirthankar Roy, "Colonialism did not cause the Indian famines", History Reclaimed, 18 Jan 2023,
    https://historyreclaimed.co.uk/colonialism-did-not-cause-the-indian-famines/ (the author is a credible scholar; the
    platform is a pro-empire advocacy group, so name Roy, not the site).
  - Sullivan & Hickel reply, 7 Jan 2023 (URL above); it links Roy's tweet thread (twitter.com/RoyHistory1/status/1600192087789826049, not opened).
  - Al Jazeera op-ed (URL above), for the "do not know for sure" caveat and the 27.18/1,000 benchmark.
- STATUS: CONTESTED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "The British Raj did not start the famines. Geography did." (Roy, History Reclaimed, 18 Jan
  2023; fetched in short fragments, check the page before printing).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: If the India figure is used at all, present it as one contested estimate, with both baselines
  and Roy's objection about cause. It's a side-light to an Irish essay; a parallel, not a proof.

### Q16b-3 — Mike Davis, *Late Victorian Holocausts* (2001): 30–60M; India alone
- CLAIM: Davis estimates 30–60 million famine deaths 1876–1902 across India, China and Brazil; India 12.2–29.3M?
- FINDING: Confirmed from the book's Preface, Table P1 "Estimated Famine Mortality". India 1876–79: 10.3M (Digby), 8.2M
  (Maharatna), 6.1M (Seavoy). India 1896–1902: 19.0M (*The Lancet*), 8.4M (Maharatna/Seavoy), 6.1M (Cambridge
  Economic History of India). **India total 12.2–29.3 million.** China 1876–79: 20M (Broomhall), 9.5–13M (Bohr); China
  1896–1900: 10M (Cohen); China total 19.5–30M. Brazil 1876–79: 0.5–1.0M (Cunniff); Brazil total 2M (Smith). **Total
  31.7–61.3 million.** Prose: "The total human toll of these three waves of drought, famine and disease could not have
  been less than 30 million victims. Fifty million dead might not be unrealistic." The table's note says chapters 3
  and 5 discuss the estimates. Davis's India range is a spread of other people's estimates (Digby 1901 and *The Lancet*
  1901 at the top, which are contemporary polemical or medical-press figures; Maharatna 1996 and Seavoy 1986 nearer the
  bottom), not his own reconstruction. Page: the Preface; Sullivan & Hickel cite "Davis, 2002, p. 7" for the Brazil
  2M figure, which fits Table P1 being on p. 7, but no page number was visible in the OCR (UNVERIFIED page). Wikipedia
  (lead only) cites the 30–60M for p. 9. George Monbiot's review (quoted second-hand) gives "between 12 and 29 million
  Indians".
- SOURCES:
  - Mike Davis, *Late Victorian Holocausts: El Niño Famines and the Making of the Third World* (London: Verso, 2001),
    Preface, Table P1. Full text on Internet Archive: https://archive.org/stream/late-victorian-holocausts/Late%20Victorian%20Holocausts%3A%20El%20Nino%20Famines%20and%20the%20Making%20of%20the%20Third%20World_djvu.txt
    (the OCR layout of the table is garbled; the rows were reconstructed by the fetch tool, so check them against the
    page scan).
  - Second, independent checks for the India components: World Peace Foundation, "Historic Famines" synthesis table
    (2024) gives India 1876–78: 6.1M; 1896–97: 5.5M; 1899–1901: 1.0M excess deaths. That is near Davis's low end. https://worldpeacefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Historic-Famines-synthesis-table-011024.docx
  - Critiques (named in Wikipedia, not read): Amartya Sen, "Apocalypse Then", *New York Times*, 18 Feb 2001; Tirthankar
    Roy, LSE working paper 2016; Alexander Morrison, *Ab Imperio* 2025.
- STATUS: VERIFIED (what Davis wrote); the numbers themselves are CONTESTED (a wide range built from sources of
  uneven quality).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "The total human toll of these three waves of drought, famine and disease could not have been
  less than 30 million victims. Fifty million dead might not be unrealistic." (Davis 2001, Preface; page to confirm,
  probably p. 7).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Use "Davis put Indian famine deaths, 1876–1902, at roughly 12 to 29 million, drawing on
  estimates that differ widely". Don't attribute the 30–60M total to India alone. Note that the WPF lower-end
  figures put India at about 12.6M.

### Q16d — Jacob Haafner's eyewitness account of the Madras famine
- CLAIM (pack): Haafner witnessed a Madras famine in 1771; the English on their balconies "revelling with their
  whores" while people died in the square and the Company's warehouses were full of grain.
- FINDING:
  - **Year: 1782**, not 1771. Haafner's account is of the famine at Madras in 1782, during the Second Anglo-Mysore War
    (Haidar Ali's invasion of the Carnatic) and the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War. He was a Dutch prisoner/resident there.
  - **Source work:** *Lotgevallen op eene reize van Madras over Tranquebaar naar het eiland Ceilon* (Haarlem, 1806),
    Haafner's first travel book. That is not *Reize in eenen palanquin* (1808) and not *Lotgevallen en vroegere
    zeereizen* (1820). The DBNL web edition gives no page numbers; the DBNL book title and editor were not captured
    (the fetch budget ran out).
  - **What the Dutch text says** (DBNL transcription, original spelling, short fragments): the crowds cheer the arrival
    of the ships, hoping "de blanken zouden zonder twijfel de armen en behoeftigen … mildaadiglijk te hulp komen"; but
    "dat zij alle gedoemd waren om den martelenden hongersdood te sterven". Rice was held back while "waar van zij nog
    overvloedig in hunne pakhuizen voorzien waren" (the Company's warehouses). Haafner asks whether the English will
    say that the "gastmaalen die zij toen ten tijde elkanderen gaven, wegens den val van Nagapatnam" made them forget
    "hoe veele ongelukkigen magteloos ter nedergestrekt binnen hunne muuren kwijnden? Hoe veele reeds, een prooi van het
    gebrek, in het stof lagen?" He also charges "dat de Engelschen dezen hongersnood veroorzaakten, om zich in haast te
    kunnen verrijken" and "dat zij ten dien einde drie millioenen onschuldige menschen lieten doodhongeren".
  - **Not found:** any "balcony/balkon/galerij/veranda" passage, and any "whores" in the famine narrative. The text's
    only "hoeren (garces)" line is spoken by a French count in a later episode at sea, unconnected to the famine. The
    pack's English sentence looks like a loose paraphrase or a mash-up. No English translation was located
    (budget ran out). Secondary use by Davis, Jon Wilson or Tharoor was not checked.
- SOURCES:
  - Jacob Haafner, *Lotgevallen op eene reize van Madras over Tranquebaar naar het eiland Ceilon* (Haarlem, 1806), as
    transcribed on DBNL: https://dbnl.org/tekst/salv007uitm01_01/salv007uitm01_01_0008.php (a modern DBNL edition;
    full bibliographic details not captured).
- STATUS: Year and source work PROBABLE (one source, DBNL); the Dutch fragments VERIFIED as transcribed on DBNL; the
  pack's English "balconies … whores" quote UNUSABLE (not found).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT (Dutch, original spelling; extracted in fragments, check against the DBNL page): "Hoe veele
  reeds, een prooi van het gebrek, in het stof lagen?" / "…dat zij ten dien einde drie millioenen onschuldige menschen
  lieten doodhongeren" (Haafner, *Lotgevallen*, Haarlem 1806, via DBNL). Any English must be marked as a **DRAFT**
  translation.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Haafner can be used, carefully: a Dutch eyewitness, hostile to the English (a wartime enemy),
  accusing the Company of hoarding rice while Madras starved in 1782 and feasting over Negapatnam. His "three million"
  is an uncounted polemical figure; don't present it as data. Drop the balconies/whores wording.
