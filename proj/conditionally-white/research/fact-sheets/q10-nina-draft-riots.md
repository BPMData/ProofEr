## Headline findings
- **The "~11 ads" figure is fuzzy in Jensen's own words.** The 2002 article (revised web text, 12-22-2004) says the *New York Times* 1851–1923 had **two** NINA ads for men; an endnote in the same text calls an 1854 *Times* ad "the only NINA ad for men anyone has ever found." In 2015 Jensen himself summarized his 2002 study as having found "**12** NINA newspaper ads and no evidence of a window sign." Use "a dozen or so" with attribution, never "11" as Jensen's count.
- **Jensen's 2002 claim was stronger than "signs were rare":** his abstract says job-market evidence "shows no significant discrimination against the Irish"; the body says "The overwhelming evidence is that such signs never existed." By 2015 he had retreated to "The signs were more rare than later Irish-Americans believed they were."
- **Jensen conceded women's (domestic-service) NINA / "Protestant only" ads were real** in 2002; his denial was about ads/signs aimed at **men** and about systematic job discrimination.
- **Fried's count is 69 cases** (Irish Examiner; Jensen's own HNN tally), not "hundreds." The "hundreds/1,400" figure is a separate IrishCentral reader keyword search of Newspapers.com (1,418 raw hits, 1828–2012), which includes non-ad mentions (e.g., the popular song) — not a scholarly count.
- **New York Sun figure differs by source:** "15 ads in 1842" (History.com, Smithsonian) vs "20 NINA newspaper ads in one New York City newspaper in 1842–43" (Jensen's description of Fried). Fried's own article text was not reachable — report as "15–20 in the *Sun*, 1842–43."
- **Draft Riots death toll:** Bernstein (2002 essay) "at least 105 people were dead and possibly many more"; 119 (or 119–120) is the standard documented figure; the 1,000–2,000 figures are older or unsourced. A contemporary Black newspaper (per HNN 2021) estimated 175 deaths of people of color — unverified primary.
- **Colored Orphan Asylum: 233 children**, per institutional records (Adelman, *Commonplace* 2017); contemporary accounts ranged 200–1,000; **no child died**. How they escaped is contested in the sources.
- **Irish share of rioters/arrests: NO numeric arrest-record figure was found this session.** Bernstein: "Irish Americans dominated the crowds, but many Irish New Yorkers stayed aloof from or fought against the mobs." Do not print a percentage.

### Q10-1 — Jensen 2002: what he actually claimed
- CLAIM (as the brief/pack states it): Jensen found ~11 NINA ads (NY Times?) and claimed signs were essentially never displayed; NINA a "myth of victimization."
- FINDING: Citation: Richard J. Jensen, "'No Irish Need Apply': A Myth of Victimization," *Journal of Social History* 36:2 (Winter 2002), pp. 405–429. Read in a **revised web version dated 12-22-2004** (posted to a mailing list 9 April 2005), not the printed journal text, so wording may differ slightly from print and no page numbers inside the article are verified.
  - Thesis (abstract): NINA signs were "extremely rare or nonexistent"; "Evidence from the job market shows no significant discrimination against the Irish"; "The NINA myth justified physical assaults, and persisted because it aided ethnic solidarity."
  - Ads: "In the entire file of the New York Times from 1851 to 1923, there are two NINA ads for men" (one for a teenager); "NINA ads for men were extremely rare--fewer than two per decade" (covering Brooklyn Eagle, Washington Post, Chicago Tribune). Endnote 18 calls the 25 March 1854 *Times* ad "the only NINA ad for men anyone has ever found" — internally inconsistent with "two."
  - Jensen's own later summary (HNN, 11 Aug 2015): his 2002 study found "12 NINA newspaper ads and no evidence of a window sign."
  - Signs: "There are no contemporary or retrospective accounts of a specific sign at a specific location"; "no photograph or drawing exists"; Museum of the City of New York's "Gaelic Gotham" exhibit "did not have any NINA signs"; surviving signs "are all modern fakes, made by novelty sign makers for the Irish market."
  - Women's ads conceded: "Newspaper ads for women sometimes did include NINA, but Irish women nevertheless dominated the market for domestics"; the refusing submarket "could not have been more than ten percent."
  - Origin: NINA "seems to have originated in England, probably after the 1798 Irish rebellion"; a cliché in London by the 1820s; first American use a Philadelphia song-sheet of 1862; John Poole's song (1862/63) popularized it.
  - Discrimination: "There have been no documented instances of job discrimination against Irish men"; "Know-Nothing employers, for example, were never accused of firing their Irish employees."
  - Later position (HNN 2015, as quoted by Burke): "The signs were more rare than later Irish-Americans believed they were."
- SOURCES:
  - Jensen, revised text of JSH 36:2 article, dated 12-22-2004, reproduced on Paleopsych list, 9 Apr 2005: https://lists.extropy.org/pipermail/paleopsych/2005-April/002523.html (read via WebFetch)
  - Richard Jensen, "No Irish Need Apply" response to Fried, History News Network, 11 Aug 2015: https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/no-irish-need-apply
  - Timothy Burke, "Yes, We Have 'No Irish Need Apply'," Easily Distracted blog (Swarthmore), 29 Jul 2015: https://blogs.swarthmore.edu/burke/blog/2015/07/29/yes-we-have-no-irish-need-apply (quotes Jensen p. 405: "The complete absence of evidence suggests that probably no such signs ever existed." — SECONDARY for the print wording)
- STATUS: VERIFIED (claims, from author's own revised text); the exact print wording/pages SECONDARY.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "No one has ever seen one of these NINA signs because they were extremely rare or nonexistent." — Jensen, JSH 36:2 (2002), abstract (revised 2004 web text). Also: "In the entire file of the New York Times from 1851 to 1923, there are two NINA ads for men" (same text). Check against print before final.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Don't say "Jensen found 11 ads." Say Jensen (2002) found almost no NINA ads aimed at men (two in the *Times*, 1851–1923; about a dozen overall by his 2015 account) and argued signs never existed — while conceding ads for women's domestic work did carry NINA.

### Q10-2 — Fried 2015/2016: the rebuttal and Jensen's reply
- CLAIM: Fried found dozens/hundreds of NINA ads (NY Sun, NY Times, Boston, 1840s–1900s) and newspaper references to signs.
- FINDING: Citation: Rebecca A. Fried, "No Irish Need Deny: Evidence for the Historicity of NINA Restrictions in Advertisements and Signs," *Journal of Social History* 49:4 (Summer 2016), pp. 829–852, doi:10.1093/jsh/shv066 (online July 2015). Fried was 14, an eighth-grader / incoming Sidwell Friends freshman, Washington DC.
  - Total: **69 cases** (Irish Examiner 13 Sep 2015; Jensen HNN 2015 "69 cases"; Burke quoting Jensen "69 examples from 21 cities").
  - New York Sun: "15 advertisements ... in 1842" (History.com; Smithsonian "The New York Sun ran 15 such ads in 1842 alone") vs. Jensen's description: "20 NINA newspaper ads in one New York City newspaper in 1842-43."
  - Jensen's breakdown of her cases: "21 classified ads for actual jobs," 1845–1932 (4 Boston, 3 NYC, 2 Chicago, 2 St Louis, 1 each Cincinnati, Cleveland, Philadelphia, Washington, 6 small places); "a total of 8 ads in New York City papers" in the eight decades after 1843; about a third of cases predate 1845.
  - Signs: Jensen says her evidence contains "only two reports of NINA signs, one in 1883 and one in 1932" (Port Washington, Ohio, 1883, a patronage job; Glens Falls NY 1932 bulletin board). History.com reports she found newspaper accounts of NINA signs in workplaces and public accommodations, Irish protests/strikes, and court cases of 1853 and 1881.
  - Abstract (per Columbia memory-studies index, summary not verbatim): evidence includes lawsuits, housing ads, Irish-American responses and NINA ads used in Confederate propaganda; Jensen's claim of a "highly limited extent of NINA postings" needs revision.
  - Example ad (Irish Examiner): Washington *Evening Star*, 1855: "Wanted immediately, a servant. A slave preferred...No Irish need apply" (SECONDARY; newspaper's quotation of Fried's find).
  - Jensen's reply (HNN 2015): "I reject her claim"; "Apart from the 1883 and 1932 cases, her cases are misinterpretations"; NY ads likely placed by employment agencies; a reader "would have to read over 41,000 pages of small print to encounter even one NINA" (search of 1,841 newspapers, 9.7 million pages). To the Washington Post (per History.com): "there was very little for an actual Irishman to see." To IrishCentral: "a very big glass, with a couple of drops of water at the bottom, and you call it half-full."
  - Separate, non-scholarly: IrishCentral (6 Aug 2015) reported a reader's Newspapers.com keyword search giving 1,418 instances of the phrase, 1828–2012, peak 1873–1887 (>400); "Not all of the mentions are classified ads or direct references to NINA signs." Raw keyword hits; includes the song and other uses.

### Q10-3 — Discrimination beyond the ads (nativism, riots, Know-Nothings)
- CLAIM: Scholarly consensus records anti-Irish/anti-Catholic discrimination and violence: Ursuline convent 1834, Philadelphia 1844, Know-Nothings.
- FINDING:
  - **Ursuline convent, Charlestown MA, night of 11 Aug 1834:** burned by a Protestant workmen's mob (initial "150 to 200" disguised men, spectators "computed at from some thousands"); ringleader John R. Buzzell, brickmaker. Lyman Beecher had preached three anti-Catholic sermons beforehand. School had "fifty to sixty students," "fewer than ten" Catholic. 13 arrested; "all but one went free" (Marvin Massey, 16, convicted, later pardoned). Legislature refused indemnity 1835 (67 for, 412 against); later offer of $10,000 (1846) rejected by Catholics as inadequate.
  - **Philadelphia nativist riots, May (3–10) and July (5–8) 1844:** "Philadelphia suffered some of the bloodiest rioting of the antebellum period" (Schrag). May: St Michael's (Kensington) and St Augustine's (city) churches burned, a seminary and homes gutted; two killed early (incl. nativist George Shiffler) plus mortally wounded. July (Southwark, St Philip de Neri): four militiamen and "probably about a dozen rioters" killed. Trigger: dispute over Bible reading in public schools (Bishop Kenrick).
  - **Know-Nothings:** in the 1854 Massachusetts election the American Party won every state senate seat and all but three House seats, plus the governorship (Henry J. Gardner); proposed a 21-year wait before immigrants could vote (amendment failed); deported some Irish immigrants (Mass. Secretary of State exhibit). Economists Alsan, Eriksson & Niemesh (NBER WP 28078, Nov 2020) link Know-Nothing voting in Massachusetts towns to exposure to Irish labor competition and deskilling.
  - Kenny (*The American Irish: A History*, Longman 2000) and Kerby Miller (*Emigrants and Exiles*, OUP 1985): NOT read this session (budget). UNVERIFIED as to specific wording; standard syntheses to cite after checking.
- SOURCES:
  - Carmine A. Prioli, "The Ursuline Outrage," *American Heritage* 33:2 (Feb/Mar 1982): https://www.americanheritage.com/ursuline-outrage
  - Zachary M. Schrag, "Nativist Riots of 1844," Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia (Rutgers, 2013): https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/?p=5879
  - Massachusetts Secretary of the Commonwealth, Commonwealth Museum, "Massachusetts Votes" exhibit: https://www.sec.state.ma.us/divisions/commonwealth-museum/exhibits/online/Massachusetts-votes/elections-in-ma_7.htm
  - Alsan, Eriksson & Niemesh, "Understanding the Success of the Know-Nothing Party," NBER WP 28078 (2020): https://www.nber.org/papers/w28078
- STATUS: VERIFIED for the events as summarized (one strong secondary each; second independent source still needed per house rule); Kenny/Miller UNVERIFIED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: "Philadelphia suffered some of the bloodiest rioting of the antebellum period" — Schrag, Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia (2013).
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The 1844 death toll is soft ("probably about a dozen" rioters in July); give a range ("roughly 15–20") only after a second source. Both riots were anti-Catholic as much as anti-Irish — say so.

### Q10-4 — 1863 New York Draft Riots
- CLAIM: Riots 13–16 July 1863; ~119 dead (vs inflated 1,000+); Colored Orphan Asylum burned (233 children); named lynching victims; rioters largely Irish; $300 commutation; Conscription Act March 1863.
- FINDING:
  - **Dates:** riots began Monday 13 July 1863 (Adelman gives July 13–17; most sources 13–16). Draft under the Enrollment (Conscription) Act passed by Congress in March 1863 (Harris 2003, pp. 279–88, per Wikipedia lead — page UNVERIFIED). $300 commutation clause "widely believed to favour the rich" (Joyce); Bernstein: perceived by poor Democrats "as an effort to transfer the burden of the war" to immigrant laborers; Tweed's County Loan ordinance later "paid the $300 draft waiver for poor conscripts."
  - **Death toll (range):** Bernstein 2002: "at least 105 people were dead and possibly many more" (p. 17). Joyce (History Ireland): officially 105, "can be raised to 119"; ~80% of the dead were rioters; 12 African-American. Bernstein 1990 book p. 288 n. 8: "119 or 120" (per Wikipedia lead; NOT read). Baruch/CUNY NYC Data: "estimated at 119; however many numbers have been exaggerated." History.com: published toll 119, "estimates ... as high as 1,200" (no source named). Barnes, *The Draft Riots in New York* (1863), pp. 5–6, 12: 18 known killed by rioters, 11 Black (per Wikipedia lead; NOT read). HNN (Mitchell 2021): "estimates ranging from more than 100 to more than a thousand"; a Black newspaper of the time estimated 175 deaths of people of color (primary NOT seen). Herbert Asbury's 2,000 killed / 8,000 wounded is widely disputed. Schecter's figure: NOT checked.
  - **Colored Orphan Asylum (Fifth Ave, 43rd–44th St):** attacked, looted and burned afternoon of 13 July 1863; **233 children**, "Institutional records confirm this figure, though contemporary accounts ranged from 200 to 1,000"; all escaped, none killed; mode of escape varies across managers' 1868 and 1896 reports, NYT, Harper's Weekly (Adelman). Joyce credits an Irish boy, Paddy McCafferty, in one account.
  - **Named Black victims (Headley 1873 list, "Colored Victims of the Riots"):** William Henry Nichols; James Costello (hanged and mutilated, 14 July); Abraham Franklin (27th St & Seventh Ave); Augustus Stuart; Peter Heuston; Jeremiah Robinson (body thrown in river); William Jones (lynched, Clarkson St); Joseph Reed, age 7; Joseph Jackson; Samuel Johnson; "Williams"; Ann Derrickson (a white woman married to a Black man, beaten defending her son). HNN 2021 notes Peter Heuston, 63, was a Mohawk veteran mistaken for Black. Also killed: Col. Henry O'Brien (Union officer, Irish-born, beaten to death by rioters — Joyce).
  - **Who rioted:** Bernstein 2002, p. 17: "Irish Americans dominated the crowds, but many Irish New Yorkers stayed aloof from or fought against the mobs"; p. 26: Tuesday rioters "more predictably Irish and Catholic, young and male, and employed in the foundries, railroad shops, and construction and dock gangs." Joyce: rioters mostly from "respectable" working-class occupations, not an underclass; riots "a civil war among the Irish of New York in particular." **No arrest-record percentage was obtained this session** (Cook, *Armies of the Streets*, 1974, and Bernstein 1990 are the sources to check). Convictions: History.com: "67 of the indicted rioters were convicted"; Joyce: "A couple of dozen received gaol terms for looting."
  - **Motives (scholarship):** Bernstein: draft "triggering a social panic among the city's immigrant and Catholic poor"; Democratic warnings that freed Black workers "would flood north to steal Irish jobs, but such fears were never realized"; poorer Catholics saw the post-emancipation war as violating "an implicit agreement"; "racist efforts to drive African-American men from the city's black enclaves"; he calls the attack on Black New Yorkers a "pogrom" (p. 27). Black population fell from 12,414 (1860) to 9,945 (1865) (History.com, census figures, second source needed).
  - **"Four days from emigrants":** what share were recent immigrants is not knowable from what was read; no source found giving it.
- SOURCES:
  - Iver Bernstein, "The Volcano Under the City: The Significance of Draft Rioting in New York City and State, July 1863," in Harold Holzer (ed.), *State of the Union: New York and the Civil War* (Fordham UP, 2002), pp. ~17–33, PDF hosted by NY Courts Historical Society: https://history.nycourts.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Chapter2.pdf (pages inferred from OCR running heads)
  - J. T. Headley, *The Great Riots of New York, 1712 to 1873* (E. B. Treat, 1873), "Colored Victims of the Riots," transcribed at https://accessgenealogy.com/?p=61684 (transcription site; verify against a scan)
  - Sarah Mulhall Adelman, "'Permitted to Proceed Unmolested': Childhood and Race in the Burning of the Colored Orphan Asylum," *Common-place* 17.2 (Winter 2017): https://commonplace.online/article/permitted-to-proceed-unmolested-childhood-and-race-in-the-burning-of-the-colored-orphan-asylum/
  - Toby Joyce, "The New York draft riots of 1863: an Irish civil war?" *History Ireland* (issue not shown): https://historyireland.com/the-new-york-draft-riots-of-1863-an-irish-civil-war/
  - Elizabeth Mitchell, "The Real Story of the 'Draft Riots'," HNN, 18 Feb 2021: https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/179269
  - Baruch College NYC Data, "Draft Riots": https://www.baruch.cuny.edu/nycdata/disasters/riots-draft.html
  - CUNY Commons page quoting History.com (18 Aug 2018): https://macleod2.commons.gc.cuny.edu/reckoning-with-the-draft-riots
  - Wikipedia, "New York City draft riots" — LEAD ONLY, for Bernstein 1990 p. 288 n. 8, Barnes 1863, Harris 2003 page refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_draft_riots
- STATUS: death toll CONTESTED (documented ~105–120; inflated claims up to 1,200–2,000); orphan count 233 VERIFIED (institutional records via Adelman; second source needed); named victims VERIFIED (1873 primary-era list) ; Irish dominance of crowds VERIFIED (Bernstein, Joyce) but numeric share UNVERIFIED.
