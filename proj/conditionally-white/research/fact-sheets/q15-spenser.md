## Headline findings

1. **The famine passage is spoken by IRENIUS, not in Spenser's own voice.** Every witness I read labels it "IREN." The text is a dialogue. A scholarly consensus that Irenius speaks for Spenser exists, but Ciaran Brady disputes it (see Q15-6). Write "Spenser has Irenius argue…" or "Spenser's spokesman Irenius…", not "Spenser wrote 'we should starve them'".
2. **The plan is a wartime measure against rebels in arms, not a famine policy for a whole people in peacetime.** Irenius's words: "although there should none of them fall by the sword… yet thus being kept from manurance, and their cattle from running abroad, by this hard restraint they would quickly consume themselves, and devour one another" (Morris 1895 text). In the same speech he cites the Munster famine as proof that the method works. It is a counter-insurgency tactic, and it was also put into practice (Munster 1580–83).
3. **Irenius blames the dead for the famine.** The passage goes on: "yet sure in all that war there perished not many by the sword, but all by the extremity of famine… which they themselves had wrought" (Renwick 1970, p. 104, as quoted in Canny 2018). Canny (2018, p. 505) reads this as Irenius presenting the famine as the result of "an official policy of resource destruction designed to force those opposing the crown in arms to surrender". The essay can use this blame-the-victim turn. It echoes later "providential"/self-inflicted framings of 1846–47, but that parallel is the essay's own and must be marked as such.
4. **No scholarly work linking Spenser's proposal directly to 1840s famine policy was found** in the time and budget available. The scholarly "continuity" literature I read (Canny 2018; Amos in *History Today*) traces Spenser's influence forward to Mountjoy, the Ulster Plantation and Cromwell, not to Trevelyan. Nineteenth-century nationalist writers did invoke Spenser's starvation proposals after the Famine (Cusack 1868, verified; Mitchel, lead only, UNVERIFIED). So **"finally executed 250 years later" is the essay's own juxtaposition** and must be signalled as such. "Executed" also implies a plan carried out to schedule, which no historian claims; see the suggested wording at the end of Q15-4.
5. **Spelling varies by edition.** There is no single "exact wording". Morris/Globe (1895, MS-based spelling): "anatomyes of death". The Wikipedia/MS-spelling version: "Anatomies [of] death". Renwick's modernised text (1970): "anatomies of death… ghosts". Quote one named edition consistently.
6. Minor date issue in the brief: "1586 — Spenser settles at Kilcolman". The Munster plantation scheme dates from 1586, but Spenser's occupation of Kilcolman is usually dated c. 1588–89 (UNVERIFIED this session; see Q15-2). Tie the 1586 date to the plantation, not to his arrival.

### Q15-1 — The starvation passage: exact wording, speaker, location
- CLAIM (as the brief states it): In *A View of the Present State of Ireland* (written 1596, published 1633), Spenser advises that "garrisons could starve the population into submission". The brief asks for the exact wording and its location.
- FINDING: The passage is in the reform section, where Irenius sets out his military plan. Irenius predicts a short war. Rebels kept from tilling their fields ("manurance") and from driving their cattle out to graze will "consume themselves, and devour one another". As proof, he describes the Munster famine of the Desmond wars. In the Renwick 1970 edition this falls on pp. 104–5 (per Canny 2018, notes 33–34). The Morris/Globe 1895 edition's full *View* runs pp. 609–663. The exact page of this speech within that range was **not** confirmed.
  The wording below is the Morris/Globe 1895 text as transcribed on Encyclopedia.com, in consecutive pieces with no gaps between them. Speaker: IREN.
  > "The end (I assure me) wil be very shorte and much sooner then can be (in soe greate a trouble, as it seemeth) hoped for allthough there should none of them fall by the swoorde, nor by slayne by the souldiour, yet thus being kept from manuraunce, and theyr cattell from running abroade, by this harde restraynte they would quickly consume themselves, and devoure one another. The proof wherof I sawe sufficiently ensampled in those late warres in Mounster; for notwithstanding that the same was a most riche and plentifull country, full of corne and cattell, that you would have thought they would have bene able to stand long, yet ere one yeare and a halfe they were brought to such wretchedness, as that any stonye harte would have rued the same. Our of every corner of the woodes the glinnes they came creeping foorthe upon theyr handes, for theyr legges could not beare them; they looked like anatomyes of death, they spake like ghostes crying out of theyr graves; they did eate of the dead carrions, happy were they yf they could finde them, yea, and one another soone after, insoemuch as the very carcasses they spared not to scrape out of theyr graves; and yf they founde a plotte of water-cresses or shamrokes, there they flocked as to a feast for the time, yet not able long to continue therewithall; that in shorte space there were none allmost left, and a most populous and plentifull countrey suddaynly made voyde of man or beast."

  Transcription problems on that page: "nor by slayne" and "Our of every corner of the woodes the glinnes" are almost certainly transcription errors on Encyclopedia.com ("Out of every corner of the woods and glens"). The MS-spelling version reads "Out of everye corner of the woode and glenns". Check against the printed Morris page before quoting. Do not "correct" the quote silently.

  The sentence that follows (MS spelling, as given on Wikipedia): "yett sure in all that warr, there perished not manye by the sworde, but all by the extreamytie of famine … they themselves had wrought." Modern spelling, Renwick p. 104 via Canny: "Yet sure in all that war there perished not many by the sword, but all by the extremity of famine." Canny quotes "which they themselves had wrought" from the same page (note 34).

  The Encyclopedia.com excerpt cuts out the "extremity of famine" sentence with an ellipsis and jumps to the land-redistribution plan ("all the landes I will give unto Englishmen…"). The famine passage and the planter's land scheme are therefore in the same stretch of Irenius's argument.

Morris (Globe edn, 1895), pp. 609–663 for the whole excerpt; transcribed at Encyclopedia.com, "From A View of the Present State of Ireland", https://www.encyclopedia.com/international/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/view-present-state-ireland (read; page numbers for this speech not shown).
  - Spenser, *A View of the Present State of Ireland*, ed. W. L. Renwick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), pp. 104–5. **SECONDARY**: read only as quoted in Nicholas Canny, "Irish Sources for Spenser's *View*", *Spenser Studies* 31/32 (2018), pp. 495–510, DOI 10.1086/694440, block quote at p. 504, notes 6, 33, 34; open-access copy: https://researchrepository.universityofgalway.ie/server/api/core/bitstreams/b70b2608-83b7-4a0a-877e-a459744abf48/content
  - MS-spelling version: Wikipedia, "A View of the Present State of Irelande", which cites luminarium.org. Read only as a lead, not a citation: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_View_of_the_Present_State_of_Irelande
  - Not reached:
    - CELT (UCC), E500000-001, which uses Grosart's 1894 privately printed edition as its source (from the CELT file's header). The fetched text cut off before this passage. https://celt.ucc.ie/published/E500000-001.html

The PDF was truncated before the passage; the Toronto mirror showed a bot check. https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/xmlui/bitstream/handle/1794/825/ireland.pdf?sequence=1
    - The 1633 Ware edition.
    - Hadfield & Maley, Blackwell 1997.
- STATUS: VERIFIED (wording, with the transcription caveats above; speaker IREN; Renwick pp. 104–5). The page in Morris and the Ware/Hadfield–Maley readings are UNVERIFIED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT (shortest honest set; modern spelling, as Renwick 1970 prints it via Canny):
  - "Yet sure in all that war there perished not many by the sword, but all by the extremity of famine." (Renwick 1970, p. 104)
  - Original spelling, Morris/Globe 1895: "by this harde restraynte they would quickly consume themselves, and devoure one another." and "they looked like anatomyes of death, they spake like ghostes crying out of theyr graves". Before printing, re-check both against the Morris page or the CELT/Grosart text.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Attribute to Irenius ("Spenser's spokesman Irenius proposes…"). Say "rebels" or "the Irish in arms" rather than "the Irish population". It is fair to note that in a war of devastation the line between rebels and population vanished, which is what the passage itself describes. Pick ONE edition and keep its spelling. If the page is modern-spelling, use Renwick (or Hadfield & Maley 1997) and say "spelling modernised".

### Q15-2 — Context: Grey, Smerwick, Kilcolman, 1598, death
- CLAIM: Spenser came to Ireland in 1580 as Lord Grey's secretary; Smerwick (1580); Kilcolman and his Munster Plantation grant (c. 1586–89, ~3,000 acres?); burned out in 1598; died 1599.
- FINDING:
  - VERIFIED (Spenser Archive intro): Spenser went to Ireland in 1580 as secretary to Arthur, Lord Grey de Wilton; stayed after Grey's recall in 1582; fled to England in 1598 and died in 1599.
  - VERIFIED: 22 manuscripts of the *View* survive, none in Spenser's hand, dated 1596 to the mid-17th century. A note at the end of Rawlinson MS B. 478 ties the text to its 1598 entry in the Stationers' Register. The work was first printed in James Ware's *The Historie of Ireland* (Dublin, 1633).
  - VERIFIED (Broadview anthology intro): Ware's 1633 edition toned down Spenser's attacks on the Anglo-Irish/Old English. So the 1633 printing is not the plainest witness; manuscript-based editions are.
  - VERIFIED (Amos, History Today): Spenser received land in the Munster plantation and lived at Kilcolman Castle. No acreage is given there.
  - UNVERIFIED leads, to check in the Dictionary of Irish Biography (dib.ie, "Spenser, Edmund") and Hadfield, *Edmund Spenser: A Life* (OUP 2012):
    - Kilcolman estate ≈ 3,028 acres, occupied c. 1588–89 (patent c. 1590).
    - Stationers' Register entry 14 April 1598 by Matthew Lownes, on condition that he get "further authority" before printing.
    - Smerwick (Dún an Óir), November 1580: Grey's forces killed the surrendered papal garrison (commonly c. 600). Spenser was probably present as secretary, and Irenius defends Grey's conduct in the *View*.
    - Kilcolman burned October 1598 in the Nine Years' War.
    - Spenser died 13 January 1599 (New Style) at Westminster and was buried in Westminster Abbey.
- SOURCES:
  - Spenser Archive (Washington Univ. in St Louis), "Vewe Introduction", https://talus.artsci.wustl.edu/spenserArchivePrototype/html/Vewe_Introduction.html
  - Broadview Anthology of Sixteenth-Century Poetry and Prose, online Spenser section (PDF), https://sites.broadviewpress.com/16cpoetryprose/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2011/09/11-16C-Anth-Online-SPENSER.pdf
  - Howard Amos, "Edmund Spenser and British Policy in Ireland", *History Today* (prize-winning student essay, Julia Wood Award 2004; no date on page), https://www.historytoday.com/print/pdf/node/48651/debug. Weak source: use only as a lead.
  - Cambridge (Ceres) transcription of Gonville & Caius MS 188/221, textual notes: https://www.english.cam.ac.uk/ceres/haphazard/vewe/textual.html
- STATUS: Secretary 1580, fled 1598, died 1599, 1598 registration, Ware 1633: VERIFIED. Acreage, exact grant dates, Smerwick numbers, exact death date: UNVERIFIED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: "Secretary to the Lord Deputy, then a planter on confiscated Desmond land" is safe. Hold "3,000 acres" and "600 at Smerwick" until DIB/Hadfield confirm them. Keep 1586 for the plantation scheme, not for Spenser's arrival at Kilcolman.

### Q15-3 — Munster famine mortality, 1579–83
- CLAIM: "30,000 died of famine in Munster in six months" (attributed to the Annals or to Sir Warham St Leger's 1582 report).
- FINDING: Not verified this session; budgets ran out before I could reach a primary or scholarly source. None of the sources I read gives a figure: Canny 2018 quotes Hooker only qualitatively ("very few or none remaining alive"), and Amos and Cusack give none.
  - Lead (UNVERIFIED): the "30,000 dead of famine in Munster in less than half a year" figure is usually traced to a letter of Sir Warham St Leger (1582), calendared in the *Calendar of State Papers, Ireland, 1574–85*. The Annals of the Four Masters (s.a. 1582) carry the qualitative line that the lowing of a cow or a ploughman's voice was not heard from Dún Caoin to Cashel. Do not attribute the 30,000 number to the Annals unless the Annals text shows it. Wider claims that "a third of Munster" died are estimates with no census behind them.
  - Second source to look for: John Hooker's continuation in Holinshed's *Chronicles* (1587). Canny (2018, p. 503, n. 32) shows that Spenser's famine description closely echoes Hooker's account. Hooker is the near-contemporary narrative source.
- SOURCES: Canny 2018 (above), qualitative only. No figure verified.
- STATUS: UNVERIFIED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: If it is used at all, present it as a contemporary official's estimate: "an English official reported some 30,000 dead of famine in Munster within six months (St Leger, 1582)". Only do so once the CSPI entry is read. No modern demographic estimate exists to set beside it, so give no precise death toll.

### Q15-4 — Scholarship linking Spenser's proposal to later famine policy / colonial continuity
- CLAIM: The essay's line is "the famine was a policy proposal, finally executed 250 years later" (brief: "Spenser proposing famine in 1596 vs. Charles Trevelyan administering it in 1846 — 250 years of continuity").
- FINDING:
  - **What scholars say (verified):**
    - Canny (2018, p. 505): for Irenius the Munster famine resulted from deliberate policy ("an official policy of resource destruction designed to force those opposing the crown in arms to surrender"). Hooker, by contrast, treated famine as providential punishment. Canny's continuity is with 16th-century writing (Hooker, Bale, Derricke); his article does not mention the 1840s.
    - Amos (*History Today*) traces the View's influence to Mountjoy's campaign and the Ulster plantation, and concludes "the View's solutions would have to wait for their apotheosis in the work of Cromwell". This is a 17th-century continuity, not 1840s, and the source is a student essay.
  - **Nineteenth-century nationalist use of Spenser (partly verified):** M. F. Cusack ("the Nun of Kenmare"), *An Illustrated History of Ireland* (1868), ch. XXVI, "Spenser's View of Ireland", summarises Spenser's proposal to deprive the Irish of food in winter. That page's summary of her wording is "deprived of all sustenance by the chasing and driving of his cows". Read it only through the fetch tool: it may be Cusack's paraphrase, not a Spenser quotation. So a famous post-Famine Irish popular history used Spenser as an emblem of English policy. Cusack does not explicitly link him to the 1840s.
  - UNVERIFIED leads:
    - John Mitchel's *History of Ireland* (1868) and/or *The Last Conquest of Ireland (Perhaps)* (1860/61) may quote Spenser's famine passage. Q13 (Mitchel) may be able to check this.
    - The scholarly debate on how representative the *View* is: Canny, "Edmund Spenser and the Development of an Anglo-Irish Identity", *Yearbook of English Studies* 13 (1983); Ciaran Brady, "Spenser's Irish Crisis: Humanism and Experience in the 1590s", *Past & Present* 111 (1986); Canny's reply, *Past & Present* 120 (1988); Brendan Bradshaw, "Sword, Word and Strategy in the Reformation in Ireland", *Historical Journal* 21 (1978); Canny, *Making Ireland British 1580–1650* (OUP 2001), ch. 1.
    - Famine-studies works that may open with Spenser: David Nally, *Human Encumbrances: Political Violence and the Great Irish Famine* (2011). Not checked.
  - **Bottom line:** I found no work making the explicit 1596→1846 "policy finally executed" claim. The essay should present the juxtaposition as its own.
- SOURCES:
  - Canny 2018 (above), pp. 503–505.
  - Amos, *History Today* (above).
  - M. F. Cusack, *An Illustrated History of Ireland* (1868), ch. XXVI, at Library Ireland: https://www.libraryireland.com/HistoryIreland/Spenser-View-Ireland.php
- STATUS: "No explicit scholarly 1596→1846 link found": PROBABLE (my search was cut short). Canny's "deliberate policy" reading: VERIFIED. Cusack 1868 using Spenser: VERIFIED (paraphrase only). Mitchel: UNVERIFIED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: Canny 2018, p. 505: for Irenius the famine was the result of "an official policy of resource destruction designed to force those opposing the crown in arms to surrender."
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY:
  - Mark the line as the essay's own: "The proposal and the policy are 250 years apart; the juxtaposition is ours, not a historian's."
  - "Finally executed" overstates; no one claims 1846 carried out Spenser's plan. A defensible alternative: "Famine as an instrument had been written down, by a great English poet, 250 years before it was administered as policy."
  - Note also that Spenser's own text calls the starvation self-inflicted ("which they themselves had wrought"). That is the stronger, sourced parallel to later providentialist readings of 1846–47.

### Q15-5 — *The Faerie Queene* Book V as Ireland allegory
- CLAIM: Book V (Artegall, Talus, Irena, Grantorto) is an Ireland allegory; the "savage nation" cleansing cantos.
- FINDING: Not verified with a scholarly citation this session.
  - UNVERIFIED (standard reading): Artegall, the knight of Justice, figures Lord Grey. Talus, the iron man with the flail, figures merciless force. Irena ("Eirene"/Ireland, also "peace") is the lady freed from the tyrant Grantorto (Spain, papal power and/or Irish rebellion), mainly in Book V, canto xii. Artegall's recall before he finishes "reforming" her realm mirrors Grey's recall in 1582.
  - Citations to verify before use: Andrew Hadfield, *Spenser's Irish Experience: Wilde Fruit and Salvage Soyl* (Oxford, 1997); Richard McCabe, *Spenser's Monstrous Regiment* (Oxford, 2002).
  - The verified detail I have: Cusack (1868) quotes "That even the wild beast shall die in starved den" and cites it to FQ III.iii. The fetch tool's summary gives that line; I have not checked it against an edition of the poem.
- SOURCES: Cusack 1868 (above), for the FQ line only. No scholarly source read.
- STATUS: UNVERIFIED.
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: The "poet drafting starvation between stanzas" joke is safe in outline: FQ Books IV–VI appeared in 1596, the year the *View* is dated. Hold the specific allegory identifications until Hadfield or McCabe is cited.

### Q15-6 — Dialogue form: who says what; does Irenius speak for Spenser?
- CLAIM: The essay should not overstate the passage as Spenser's first-person voice.
- FINDING: The speakers are Eudoxus (who has never been to Ireland and asks questions) and Irenius (recently back from Ireland, experienced in its affairs). The starvation plan, the Munster famine description, the "extremity of famine… which they themselves had wrought" verdict and the land-to-Englishmen plantation scheme are all **Irenius's** (labelled IREN. in the Morris text; Canny 2018 treats them as Irenius's).
  - Amos describes Irenius as one "who speaks from experience of Ireland, much like Spenser himself".
  - Canny's work, and most readings since, treat Irenius as Spenser's spokesman.
  - UNVERIFIED: Ciaran Brady (1986) argued that the *View*'s position is unstable and not a coherent blueprint. That is the main scholarly dissent on how far the text is a settled programme; it is not a claim that Irenius opposes Spenser.
- SOURCES:
  - Encyclopedia.com/Morris text (speaker labels)
  - Canny 2018
  - Amos, *History Today*
  - Wikipedia (characterisation of Eudoxus and Irenius; lead only)
  - Spenser Archive intro (Irenius is "experienced in Irish affairs")
- STATUS: Speaker attribution: VERIFIED. "Irenius = Spenser's voice" as broad consensus: PROBABLE (supported by Canny and Amos; Brady's dissent not read).
- QUOTE-READY EXCERPT: none beyond Q15-1.
- NOTES FOR THE ESSAY: Use "Spenser, through his spokesman Irenius, …" once, then "Spenser's Irenius" after that.
