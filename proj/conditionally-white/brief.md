## 0. The one-line brief

**Whiteness was not an identity the Irish were denied and then granted; it was a price they were quoted, and the essay's argument is about what the installments cost — at home (the land, the food, a million lives), abroad (participation in colonial brutality against Black Americans and Indigenous Australians), and in memory (a diaspora that honors the escape and distorts the cause). Build a seven-chapter scrollytelling data essay that proves the offer was conditional, itemizes the payments, and lands the moral: the bargain is still running, and the ledger is still open.**

The essay's engine is a set of oppositions the charts make visible:

- ~1,000,000 dead vs. food shipped out under military escort (same years, same counties, same ports)
- 60% of the land → 5% of the land (Catholic share, 1641 → 1778) vs. the story the diaspora tells about itself
- "the Irish became white" vs. the fine print: conditionally, generationally, and at someone else's expense
- one stolen horse vs. the entire apparatus that made stealing the horse the rational escape route
- Edmund Spenser proposing famine in 1596 vs. Charles Trevelyan administering it in 1846 — 250 years of continuity

**The personal frame (do not lose this):** the essay opens and closes on the user's ancestor — a great-great(-great?) grandfather (confirm the exact number of "greats" with the user during outline review) who either accidentally got caught stealing a horse or deliberately stole one in front of an Englishman in order to be deported to Australia and escape the famine. This story survives in family memory as cunning; the essay's job is to show the system that made it a survival strategy. The horse thief is the reader's tour guide: he appears in the hero, is traced through the transportation chapter, and gets the final line.

**Thesis calibration (mandatory, matches the user's claim-by-claim standard):**
- The essay does NOT assert the famine was a genocide. It asserts the strongest defensible claim: **the famine was made lethal by policy premised on racialized contempt**, and it presents the genocide verdict as CONTESTED (Ciarán Ó Gráda's mainstream position vs. the Mitchel tradition) with both sides fairly stated.
- The essay does NOT claim the Irish were "legally non-white" (in America, the Naturalization Act of 1790 admitted "free white persons" — the Irish always qualified on paper). It claims the Irish were **racialized as an inferior intra-European caste** (Beddoe's "Index of Nigrescence," the Punch bestiary) and that full whiteness was **provisionally re-offered, conditional on colonial participation**. That distinction — legal vs. social whiteness — is a load-bearing wall of the essay and gets its own claims-plate entry.
- Every contested figure (Sullivan & Hickel's 100–165M excess deaths, the Duke of Cambridge quote, NINA ads) gets flagged per §4. Nothing contested ships as settled.

## 2. Thesis and chapter plot

### Hero (paper) — the horse

### Chapter 1 — The Prototype (paper): Ireland as the laboratory
- **Thesis beat**: before there were Highland clearances and Indian famine codes, there was Ireland — England's proving ground for the legal machinery of expropriation. The chronological correction is the chapter's argument: **Ireland was the prototype, not a copy.** England's parliamentary enclosure peak and Scotland's burnings came *later*; the first systematic clearance of a Celtic population, with plantations, confiscation statutes, and a starving policy, happened in Ireland under Elizabeth and Cromwell.
- **Prequel gyre** (the house five-careers mechanic, transposed): a horizontal timeline strip the reader scrolls, each mechanism earning a stamp as it goes live:
  1. **1556 — the first plantations** (Mary's reign: Laois–Offaly confiscated and planted; the experiment fails; the template survives)
  2. **1586 — Munster** (the Desmond rebellions crushed; the province planted; Edmund Spenser settles at Kilcolman as a planter and writes *A View of the Present State of Ireland*, advising that garrisons could starve the population into submission — the essay's aphorism lands here, in a footnote-bearing caption: **the famine was a policy proposal, finally executed 250 years later**)
  3. **1609 — the two settlements of the same year**: the Plantation of Ulster *and* the Statutes of Iona (the Scottish crown legislating the Highland Gaels into Protestantism — the shared-Gaeldom beat: London treated both as one problem)
  4. **1652 — "To hell or Connacht"** (Cromwell's Act of Settlement: ~80% of Ireland confiscated in one statute; the transplanted named in acreage, the destination a land already failing)
  5. **1695–1750s — the Penal Laws** (the legal architecture of a caste: no purchase of land, no inheritance of land, no education, no arms — the lock on the ledger)
- End of strip: a map of Ireland redrawn in confiscation shades (✱ see imagery directive), and the line that hands off to Chapter 2: the machinery worked; now count what it took.
- **Sticky scene**: the clearance-mechanism conveyor — five mini-diagrams (plantation / statute / penal law / ejectment act / poor law), each a tiny labeled SVG that assembles as the reader scrolls. Caption bands carry the dates and one-line verdicts.
- Tone: this is the early gyre and it may be funny in the house way — Spenser the great poet drafting starvation policy between stanzas of the *Faerie Queene* is exactly the essay's species of joke (the machinery is absurd; the dead are not the punchline). The Statutes of Iona beat is dry: the same parliament-season, two coasts, one Gael.

### Chapter 2 — The Ledger (turf-green field, `--turf`)
- **Thesis beat**: the essay's title phrase — "the wealth of gentlemen" — sourced and receipted. Enclosure in England is usually told as an English story with English victims (the commons, the handloom weavers). This chapter tells it as a *system* chapter: the same gentlemen, the same statutes, the same ideological cover, applied across three islands, with Ireland's version arriving first and hardest. The theft is chartable; chart it.
- **Sticky scene — the accumulation machine**: the reader scrolls through the enclosure pipeline as a conveyor: commissioners who are themselves landowners → MPs voting on county bills they benefit from → costs assessed by acreage so smallholders are forced to sell allotments they cannot fence → the landless poor, whose rights were customary and unwritten, receive nothing. Each stage a small diagram that locks into place. Side text carries the user's enclosure document's receipts: W.E. Tate on the Board of Agriculture's monotony; Arthur Young's honest 1801 survey suppressed by the Board he served; Ricardo, Burke, Bentham, and Malthus supplying the arguments against compensating the dispossessed.
- **Specimen plate — the tragedy, retracted**: the "Tragedy of the Commons" as propaganda plate. Segmented control: [Lloyd 1833 | Hardin 1968 | Hardin retracts | Fairlie's verdict]. Each selection updates the readout: the iron law, the most-cited paper in the history of *Science*, the early-1990s retraction ("the tragedy of the *unmanaged* commons"), and the reversal — it was not commoners' irrationality that destroyed the commons but the rich crowding beasts onto them ("The rich crowded their beasts on, and literally eat out the poor" — attribute ✱). The punchline is declarative: the paper spent twenty years providing intellectual cover for the enclosers' retrospective innocence.
- **Sticky scene — the land-share ledger (the chapter's climax)**: three bars, Catholic share of Irish land: **1641 ≈ 60% → 1703 ≈ 14% → 1778 ≈ 5%**. The bars drain as the reader scrolls; each drop annotated with its mechanism (confiscations, Williamite settlements, penal-law lock-in). Footnote the sourcing properly (✱ these figures circulate widely; anchor them in a scholarly source during research — see §8 of the research-prompts file).
- Text beat: J.M. Neeson's arithmetic of the commons — a cow on the common worth roughly half a laborer's wage; geese on Otmoor a full year's income. What enclosure "freed" was not land but people, freed from the option of not working for wages. Then the 1872 Return of Owners of Land (0.6% of the population holding ~98% — ✱ verify exact figures), and the chapter's closing definition: the Victorian gentleman, whose grandfather fenced what your grandmother gleaned, called it improvement.

### Chapter 3 — Not Quite White (paper): the invention of whiteness
- **Thesis beat**: race as science arrives in the nineteenth century and inventories Europe; the Irish are measured and found wanting — darker, hairier, more simian, Celt as a degeneration of the Anglo-Saxon. This chapter stages the measurement itself: the calipers, the indices, the cartoons. The argument is not that the Irish suffered what enslaved Africans suffered — it is that nineteenth-century racial science placed the Irish *adjacent to* Blackness as a graded ladder, and that adjacency is documented, quantified, and illustrated.
- **Specimen plate — the measurement**: segmented control over the instruments: [Beddoe's Index of Nigrescence | the cranial schools | the Punch bestiary]. Readouts: John Beddoe's *The Races of Britain* (1885) and its "index of nigrescence" scoring peoples of the British Isles by darkness; the phrenological/craniometric literature's treatment of the "Celtic" skull; and the Punch school of cartoon (specific cartoons with dates — ✱ source 5–8 concrete examples with Commons file names; candidates: the simian "Irish Frankenstein," ape-like Paddy figures, the "missing link" genre). Each instrument gets one image and one readout; the plate's frame is a caliper motif.
- **Sticky scene — the ladder**: a vertical racial ladder as drawn by the era's science (a labeled SVG reconstruction of the graded hierarchies, sourced to the literature, no invented rungs), with the Irish placed — and then, as the reader scrolls, the ladder is annotated with what whiteness *excluded* the Irish from at each historical moment: property (Penal Laws), franchise (1829 disfranchisement of the forty-shilling freeholders — the Mitchel receipt, Ch4-ready), relief in famine (Ch4's quotes).
- **The counter-quotation beat**: Daniel O'Connell, 1845, to Americans: "Over the broad Atlantic I pour forth my voice, saying, come out of such a land, you who love liberty" — the Irish abolitionist position, before the famine rewired Irish-American politics (the transition to Ch6 is seeded here: the O'Connell–Frederick Douglass exchange of 1845, Douglass's tour of Ireland; ✱ verify the exchange and quote sources).
- Text beat: Theodore W. Allen's *The Invention of the White Race* and Noel Ignatiev's *How the Irish Became White* as the chapter's scholarly anchors — stated with their theses *and* their critics (David Roediger's refinements, Peter Kolchin's methodological skepticism; see research prompts). The essay is a synthesis, not a disciple.

### Chapter 4 — Black '47 (indigo field, nearly textureless): the policy executed
- **Thesis beat**: the blight arrives in 1845; the response is doctrine. This is the essay's gravity zone: the machinery chapters (1–3) were funny; here the humor stops at the doorway, and what remains is the accounting. The chapter's argument is made of documents: the quotes, the export tables, the eyewitness record.
- **Sticky figure (two-column ≥960px)**: left text, right the dual chart — the famine's two lines: **people leaving** (emigration, rising) against **food leaving** (exports, sustained or rising) 1845–1850, drawn from the Kinealy data (§6). The lines' relationship is the argument; let the caption be one sentence: "The market cleared what it was built to clear."
- **The quotes plate (the chapter's set piece)**: a wall of the era's own words — house-styled quote cards, each with a verdict-relevant attribution and its own footnote: Trevelyan's "moral evil... of the selfish, perverse and turbulent character of the people" (December 1846); *The Times*' "we regard the potato blight as a blessing" (September 1846); Wood's "purgatory of misery and starvation" (August 1846); Russell on interfering with trade (August 1846); Jowett recalling Nassau Senior's fear that 1848 "would not kill more than a million... scarcely enough to do any good"; Clarendon on the Irish "always in the mud" (September 1847); the Newry Telegraph's industrial-Ulster sermon (March 1849); the 1851 Census finding the population collapse "on the whole, satisfactory"; the bailiff's "black potatoes" (Freeman's Journal, April 1846 — ✱). Opposite the wall, one honest inclusion: Ruth Dudley Edwards' counter-claim ("no deliberate cruelty and no intention to kill anyone") — the chapter shows the wall and lets the reader hold both. Every quote is verified against a primary source before it ships (see the research-prompts file, batch Q1–Q4); anything that fails verification leaves the wall and the wall's caption notes the eviction.

And Mitchel's arithmetic of the pre-famine machine: the Devon Commission's Table 95 (~192,368 families — about a million people — to be "removed" by consolidation, per Mitchel's reading ✱ verify the table), O'Connell's "consult butchers about keeping Lent."

### Chapter 5 — Test the Claims (paper; verdict stamps)
- **Thesis beat**: the diaspora's memory runs on slogans; the paperwork is less tidy. A claim-by-claim plate in the knife-essay style: segmented buttons, five claims, each answered with a stamped verdict (CONFIRMED / BURNISHED / CONTESTED) that thuds in with a scale animation. Semantics per §4.
- The five claims and verdicts (fine print in §6):
  1. **"The Irish famine was a genocide."** → **CONTESTED** — Ó Gráda's mainstream position (policy catastrophic, premised on contempt and laissez-faire doctrine; intent to destroy not demonstrable) vs. the Mitchel tradition and the genocide argument (distinguished: the essay argues "famine made lethal by policy premised on racialized contempt" and shows why the stronger word fails in court but survives in the street).
  2. **"Ireland was starved while food was exported under guard."** → **CONFIRMED** — the Kinealy receipts (56,557 firkins of butter to Bristol in nine months of 1847; 9,992 calves; oats out of dying Clare; 3M+ live animals 1846–50 ✱); the qualifier footnoted honestly (exports were partly estate-owners' property; the historical argument is about policy choices, not confiscated dinner pots).
  3. **"The Irish were considered non-white."** → **BURNISHED** — the tidy version is false (never legally barred from whiteness; the 1790 Naturalization Act's "free white persons" included them); the spirit is documented (racialized as an inferior intra-European caste, measured for "nigrescence," cartooned as simian). The distinction legal/social is the readout's centerpiece.
  4. **"The Irish were welcomed to America with 'No Irish Need Apply.'"** → **BURNISHED** — the sign existed (Jensen's famous count: eleven surviving ads in the 1860s) but the omnipresence is memory outrunning evidence (Jensen's "myth of victimization" thesis vs. Fried's rebuttal — both cited, verdict on the *claim as popularly stated*, not on the lived discrimination which the 1863 Draft Riots, the tenements, and the barrack-style jobs evidence amply).
  5. **"The Irish became white by joining the oppression of others."** → **CONTESTED** — Ignatiev's thesis stated at full strength, then the pushback (the becoming was structural and generational — property, franchise, police uniforms, mortgages — not a single signed contract; and the anti-Black violence of 1863 is documented alongside Irish abolitionists like O'Connell and the Irishmen in the Union ranks; both ledgers open at once).
- Each verdict gets a 2–4 sentence readout in the house voice with its own footnote. This plate is the essay's immune system against its own thesis — it must be the fairest thing in the document.

### Chapter 6 — The Bargain (paper, gold accents): the conditional offer
- **Thesis beat**: whiteness was re-offered, and the terms were participation. Two destination chapters in one: America and Australia — the two big Irish diasporas, and the two different installments.
- **America side — the union card**: the mechanism inventory: the Democratic machine ward, the police uniform, the draft that excluded Black men from the ballot but not the Irish from the ranks, the fire and police departments that became Irish job trusts, the property deed that whitened by mortgage (Roediger's term "whiteness as wages" or the standard reading — ✱ cite carefully). Then the 1863 Draft Riots, straight: five days in July, ~119 dead (✱), the Colored Orphan Asylum burned, Black New Yorkers lynched on lamp-posts — and the essay states plainly that the men in the mob were four days from emigrants themselves, which is not an excuse but the point: the bargain's first payment came due fast, and it was collected from someone who had not made the offer.
- **Australia side — the horse thief's chapter (the essay's personal climax)**: the frame returns. A vertical timeline of transportation: the ancestor's era options — NSW closed to convicts 1840, Van Diemen's Land 1853, Western Australia open 1850–1868 — meaning a famine-era horse thief was most likely bound for the far side of the continent (✱ verify dates and check the Irish transportation database for famine-period horse-theft sentences). The famine orphans scheme (Earl Grey, 1848–1850: ~4,000 girls, workhouse orphans, sent to Australia — ✱) as the alternate rescue route. Then the second generation's bargain in Australia: the Irish ex-convict and ex-orphan population positioned against the next rung down — Chinese goldfields workers at Lambing Flat (the 1861 riots, the push that fed White Australia ✱) and Aboriginal dispossession (Irish-born among the squatters; the Stolen Generations' administrative machinery staffed by people who had fled caste themselves — state this with care and receipts, this is a contested register: cite the scholarship on Irish participation in settler colonialism in Australia, e.g. work on the Irish in the Queensland police ✱).
- **Mitchel's route (moved here from Chapter 7; one paragraph)**: the bargain's other passenger list. Mitchel was convicted in 1848 and sent to Van Diemen's Land, escaped in 1853, and reached America, where Chapter 7 picks him up (✱ Q13(1): check whether he was held in Bermuda first, since the route may be compressed here; the Dictionary of Irish Biography is the check).
- **Sticky scene — the gauge**: "the offer" visualized as a credit-score dial, the marker sweeping as the reader scrolls through three generations: [arrival: refused] → [naturalization, uniform, deed: provisional] → [grandchildren: full]. Each tick annotated with its instrument (papers, ballots, batons). The dial's fine print, permanently visible: *provisional — revocable at the border of any empire.*
- The O'Connell–Douglass exchange closes the chapter: the Irish Liberator publicly told American slaveholders he wanted none of their alliance ("come out of such a land"); Irish America largely ignored him. One line carries the transition: the offer was not whispered; it was mailed, and most households framed it.

### Chapter 7 — The Memory (paper): honor and distortion
- **Thesis beat**: the diaspora keeps two ledgers — one it reads aloud (the escape, the struggle, the shamrock on the astronaut's cuff) and one it keeps in the drawer (what the escape was from, and what the arrival was into). This chapter audits the second ledger, with affection. It must never sneer at the user's heritage; the tone is an heir reading the family accounts, not a prosecutor.
- **Spine — John Mitchel**: the one figure the essay follows from the famine to the end of his life, and its most instructive ghost. He wrote, from experience and fury, "the Almighty sent the potato blight, but the English created the Famine" (1861, edition ✱); he read the Devon Commission as a death-warrant and the cheap-ejectment acts as machinery. Chapter 6 covers his transportation to Van Diemen's Land and his escape; this chapter starts when he reaches America. There he wrote for pro-slavery papers, including the *Southern Citizen* (✱ Q13(3): quote his own words), then backed the Confederacy. Quote his pro-slavery writing directly from digitized sources; never paraphrase it into something softer. The point is not hypocrisy-thunder. ## 5. Tone and voice (non-negotiable)

**Register**: literary longform magazine — the house voice. Long, rolling sentences that earn their semicolons; dry wit; direct address to the reader; erudite asides footnoted like a paper. **Voice samples to calibrate against** (mimic this rhythm):

**Rules**
1. Every number carries a footnote; every uncertain number is hedged in prose ("roughly," "by Mitchel's reading of the table," "catalogues of the ads disagree"). Never fabricate a source, a date, or a quote. The quotes-wall is the essay's credibility — it is verified quote by quote before it ships (see the research-prompts file, batches Q1–Q4), and any quote that fails verification leaves the wall.

4. No emojis anywhere in the document. No exclamation marks in body copy. The wit is declarative.
