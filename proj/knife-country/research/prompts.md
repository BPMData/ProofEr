---

I'm writing a long, interactive web infographic for curious adults ("knife nerds") about knife-making in China
(especially Yangjiang), how Germany and Japan became known as the countries for good knives, and how China is
competing now. Readers need a primer on chef knives first, or the history won't make sense. Research that primer.

**Rules for the report**
- Every factual claim gets a source link right next to it. Prefer primary or expert sources: metallurgy papers and
  textbooks, steel makers' data sheets (Böhler, Uddeholm, Sandvik/Alleima, Takefu, Hitachi/Proterial, Aichi, Daido,
  Chinese mills), knife makers' published specs, controlled tests (CATRA edge-retention tests, toughness tests such
  as Knife Steel Nerds' Charpy data), and trade or government data. Mark forum posts, retailer copy and marketing as
  weak sources.
- Where experts disagree or evidence is thin, say so. Separate measured facts from craft lore.
- Give numbers with units and ranges (degrees, mm, grams, HRC), not adjectives.
- Write for a smart non-expert; define each term once.

**1. Anatomy and shape**
- Parts of a chef knife (tip, belly, heel, spine, choil, bolster, tang, handle) and what each does.
- The main kitchen shapes and how they're used: German/Western chef's knife, gyuto, santoku, nakiri, usuba, deba,
  yanagiba, bunka, kiritsuke, and the Chinese cleaver/caidao (slicer vs chopper vs bone cleaver). How the blade
  profile (flat vs curved belly) changes the cutting motion (rocking vs push-cutting vs chopping).

**2. The edge and grind**
- Single bevel vs double bevel: how each cuts, who uses which, why single bevels are traditional in Japan.
- Edge angles: typical German vs Japanese vs Chinese factory angles (per side and inclusive), and the trade-off
  between sharpness and edge stability.
- Grind types (flat, full convex, hollow, sabi/kataba, scandi, S-grind) and "thinness behind the edge": why it often
  matters more than the steel for how a knife feels when cutting.
- Spine thickness and distal taper: typical ranges and what they change.

**3. Size and balance**
- Typical lengths and weights for each shape; how weight and balance point affect control and fatigue.
- Full vs partial vs hidden tang; Western vs Japanese (wa) handles.

**4. Steel**
- What hardness (HRC) means, and the trade-off between hardness, toughness, edge retention and corrosion resistance.
- Carbon vs stainless vs powder-metallurgy steels. For each of these, give composition, typical hardness in kitchen
  knives, and strengths/weaknesses: X50CrMoV15 (1.4116), 5Cr15MoV, 3Cr13/4Cr13, 7Cr17MoV, 8Cr13MoV, 9Cr18MoV,
  AUS-8, AUS-10, VG-10, 10Cr15CoMoV, Sandvik 14C28N, Aogami (Blue) 1/2/Super, Shirogami (White) 1/2, SG2/R2, ZDP-189,
  HAP40, M390, Damascus/san-mai cladding (and whether the pattern adds performance).
- Which Chinese steels are copies or equivalents of Japanese or European ones, and who makes them.
- How much heat treatment matters compared to steel choice. Evidence that the same steel performs very differently
  between makers.

**5. Does the country matter?**
- What actually separates a great knife from a mediocre one in measurable terms (geometry, heat treatment, grind
  consistency, fit and finish, QC), and how much of that is tied to a place vs to a single maker.
- OEM/white-label manufacturing: which well-known Western or Japanese-branded knives are documented as made in China
  (especially Yangjiang), and what the evidence is. Country-of-origin labeling rules (US, EU, Japan) and loopholes.
- Any blind tests or controlled comparisons of knives across countries and price points.

**6. Yangjiang today (if found along the way)**
- Production and export figures for Yangjiang and China's cutlery industry, with dates and sources (Chinese
  government or industry association data preferred).
- Supply chain: steel mills, forging, grinding, heat treatment, handles, packaging: how much sits in one region.
- Technology: automated grinding, laser cutting, robotic sharpening, in-house powder steels.

**End with:** a table of the 10 most important design choices in a chef knife, each with: what it is, the usual
options, the measurable trade-off, and one source.

## Round 2 (gaps)

Paste everything below the line into a deep-research tool and bring back the full report with its sources. It

---

I'm writing a long, interactive web infographic for curious adults about knife-making in China (especially Yangjiang),
and how the reputation of "the best knife country" has moved between places. I already have research on Yangjiang,
Solingen, Seki, Sakai and knife metallurgy. I need three gaps filled, plus one wider pattern.

**Rules for the report**
- Every factual claim gets a source link next to it. Prefer primary sources, academic history, museum and archive
  material, government records and serious journalism. Mark company marketing, retailer blogs, forums and
  Wikipedia-only claims as weak.
- Where sources disagree or evidence is thin, say so. Separate documented fact from legend.
- Give dates, numbers and names exactly; no rounding without saying so.
- For each section, end with 3–5 "infographic-ready" facts: one sentence each, with a source and a confidence level.

**1. Solingen and Seki at war (World Wars I and II)**
- What Solingen's blade makers produced for the German military in WWI and WWII: bayonets, swords, daggers (including
  Nazi-era dress daggers), other arms. Which named firms (e.g. Zwilling J.A. Henckels, Wüsthof, Böker, Eickhorn and
  others) made what, with sources.
- Forced or slave labor in Solingen's blade industry during WWII: documented cases, firms named, and any later
  acknowledgment, reparations or company histories that address it.
- The *Gesetz zum Schutz des Namens Solingen* of July 25, 1938: why it was passed then, who pushed for it, and whether
  it connects to Nazi economic policy. Also confirm the date of the 1994 *Solingenverordnung* (sources disagree:
  October 16 vs December 16, 1994).
- Seki in the 1930s–40s: production of military swords (*guntō*) for the Imperial Japanese Army and Navy. Scale,
  methods (traditional vs machine-made, "Seki stamp" marks), the smiths involved, and how Seki moved back to cutlery
  after 1945 (including the US occupation's sword confiscation policy).
- Allied bombing or occupation effects on both towns' industry, and how quickly each rebuilt.

**2. Sheffield: the knife capital that lost the title**
- Sheffield's dominance in cutlery and steel from roughly 1700 to 1900: crucible steel (Benjamin Huntsman, 1740s),
  the Cutlers' Company (1624), "Sheffield" as a quality mark, export markets (especially the US).
- How and when Sheffield lost ground: competition from Solingen (and evidence on prices, mechanization, tariffs,
  "Made in Germany" marking under the UK Merchandise Marks Act 1887), US tariffs and US makers, and later Asian
  competition. Workforce and output numbers over time if available.
- Whether "Sheffield" has any legal protection like Solingen's, and what's left of the industry today.

**3. "Made in Japan" once meant cheap**
- How Japanese manufactured goods were seen in the US and Europe from the Meiji era through the 1960s: cheap,
  imitation, low quality. Concrete evidence: press coverage, trade data, consumer surveys, government reports.
- **Ceramics specifically:** Japanese export porcelain and pottery (e.g. Noritake/Morimura Brothers, Nippon-marked
  ware, "Occupied Japan" marks 1947–52), the US McKinley Tariff of 1890 that required country-of-origin marking
  ("Nippon" then "Japan"), and how its reputation changed.
- The turnaround: when and how "Made in Japan" came to mean quality. Role of W. Edwards Deming and statistical quality
  control, JIS standards, MITI export inspection laws (e.g. the Export Inspection Law of 1957), and specific
  industries (cameras, electronics, cars).
- Japanese kitchen knives in the West, 1950s–1980s: when they first appeared in US/European kitchens and how they
  were perceived before Global (1985) and Anthony Bourdain's *Kitchen Confidential* (2000).

**4. The wider pattern: cheap to quality**
- Other documented cases of a country's goods moving from "cheap copies" to "quality leader": e.g. Germany ("Made in
  Germany" was imposed by Britain in 1887 as a warning label), the US in the 19th century (copying British
  manufacturing), South Korea, Taiwan. For each: the stigma, the turning point, and how long it took.
- Evidence on whether "Made in China" perception is shifting, and in which product categories (e.g. EVs, phones,
  drones, folding knives), with survey data if any exists.

**End with:** a timeline table (year | place | event | source) across all four sections, and a list of anything you
looked for and couldn't verify.
