The Holocaust, 1939–1945: the fate of 22 Jewish communities

Nine and a half million people.

That was the Jewish population of these 22 countries before the war. By 1945, nearly six million of them had been murdered.

    Who was in control

    Occupation mattered, but it did not decide everything. Among Germany's allies that kept their own governments, results ranged from almost no deaths to near-total destruction. What those governments chose to do made the difference.

    What made the difference

    Historians point to a handful of conditions that, together, decided whether a community was destroyed or mostly survived.

    Who held real power

    Where the SS governed directly (occupied Poland, the Baltic states, the occupied Soviet Union, the Netherlands' Nazi civilian regime), death rates were highest. Where the German army ran things, as in Belgium, persecution was often slower.

    What local authorities chose

    Slovakia paid Germany to take its Jews, and Romania killed on its own initiative. Bulgaria's parliament and church, Finland's government and Italy's army refused to hand over Jews. Local police did the arresting in France, Hungary and Norway.

    Timing

    Communities reached late had more warning and more chances to hide. Hungary is the terrible exception: untouched by deportation until 1944, it then lost about 437,000 people in under two months.

    Geography and escape routes

    Neutral Sweden saved most of Denmark's Jews and many of Norway's. Estonian, Polish and Soviet Jews who fled east often lived. The flat, crowded Netherlands offered almost nowhere to hide.

    Citizens and refugees

    Stateless and foreign-born Jews were usually handed over first. Governments bargaining with Germany often protected citizens longer, as in France and Bulgaria, at the cost of refugees and people in occupied territory.

    About these numbers

    The population and death figures come from “Statistics of the Holocaust,” The History Place. They are estimates, drawn with pre-war or wartime borders that differ from today's. Other scholarly estimates of the total range from about 5.1 million (Raul Hilberg) to about 6 million Jewish victims.

    Percentages are shown as printed in the source, with one correction: Finland is printed as 0.03%, but 7 deaths out of 2,000 is 0.35%. Bar and circle sizes are drawn from the counts themselves.

    Each country panel also shows the US Holocaust Memorial Museum's estimate from its country-by-country table. It sometimes uses different census years and borders: Germany's population is counted in 1939, after most emigration, and Hungary's within its pre-1938 borders. That is why the two sources can differ sharply. Where later research differs from the main source, the panel says so. Notable cases:

    • Bulgaria: no deaths are listed for Bulgaria proper, but Bulgaria deported 11,343 Jews from territory it occupied in Greece and Yugoslavia.
    • Romania: an official commission estimated 280,000–380,000 Jews died in Romania and the territories it controlled, plus 135,000 Romanian Jews under Hungarian rule.
    • Estonia: most research puts Estonian Jewish deaths at about 950–1,000, not 2,000.
    • USSR: the survivor figure mostly reflects Jews living in areas Germany never occupied. In occupied Ukraine, about 60% of Jews were murdered; see the USSR panel.

    The outline categories describe who ruled each country during the deportations. Hungary, Italy and Slovakia were German allies with their own governments until Germany occupied them in 1943–44. Denmark was occupied but kept its own government until 1943.

    Country accounts draw on the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum's Holocaust Encyclopedia, Yad Vashem, the Anne Frank House, the Final Report of the International Commission on the Holocaust in Romania, and other sources linked in each panel.
